## Bringing heterogeneous demonstrations together

Execution and evaluation constrain progress alongside data scarcity. Updating targets at 50 Hz leaves 20 ms per cycle. If a large model takes longer, a system needs separate fast low-level control and slower high-level decisions, action chunks, or asynchronous execution. Adding robots, objects, locations, and instructions also multiplies evaluation combinations. Generalist-policy scaling changes not only collection effort but also the cost of repeatedly establishing where the model works.

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={8} caption="The cost of collecting demonstrations" />

Recording one teleoperated cup placement requires arranging initial positions, resetting after failures, and synchronizing sensor and motion records. These physical repetitions create costs unlike collecting web documents.

[Original lecture, 5:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=302s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={9} caption="Putting existing data into a common format" />

Despite different laboratory recording methods, an episode can expose observations and actions in time order. A common storage format aligns data access, while coordinate and semantic transformations remain separate.

## How Octo turns goals and observations into actions

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={19} caption="Octo's training coverage" />

Octo is an open policy trained on about 800,000 episodes selected from OXE. It accepts language instructions or goal images, and is evaluated directly on represented hardware or fine-tuned with data from new hardware. [Octo project](https://octo-models.github.io/)

[Original lecture, 14:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=870s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={20} caption="Task tokens and observation tokens" />

“Place the knife on the plate” specifies a goal; external and wrist images show current positions. Following the two diagram paths separates what to do from where things are now.

Pretrained T5 represents language, while a small convolutional encoder converts images into observation tokens. A Transformer reads both and gathers action-generation information into purple readout tokens. Their numerical values are passed to a diffusion head rather than directly to motors. [Octo paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12213)

[Original lecture, 15:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=913s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={21} caption="Time direction and permitted references" />

Octo uses block-causal attention. Representations generating current actions read past and current observations, preventing references to future camera frames unavailable during execution.

[Original lecture, 16:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=970s)

The block-causal structure defines both temporal constraints and token roles. Cameras at the same time can reference one another, and current observations can read goals and earlier observations but not future ones. Readouts aggregate goals and observations for output heads. Reference directions also prevent observation or output-readout information from flowing backward into goal representations. Rather than summarizing every mask with the transcript's sentence “task tokens cannot reference each other,” consult the paper's token-type permission table. [Octo paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12213)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={22} caption="Imitating demonstrations conditioned on a goal" />

With a goal-state image, the difference between a completed knife-on-plate image and the current image informs action selection. Language goals likewise condition the policy.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_{\le t},g)`} label="Goal-conditioned action selection" />

[Original lecture, 16:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1006s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={23} caption="Action chunks from a diffusion module" />

The action module receives information aggregated in readout tokens and generates multistep continuous controls from noise. A distribution helps represent approaching the same cup from the left or right.

[Original lecture, 17:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1040s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={24} caption="Aligning the meaning of gripper values" />

In one dataset, 1 means open; in another, closed. Other datasets store continuous values or change directions. Opposite binary absolute conventions can be aligned by inversion, while relative commands require examining opening/closing events and state.

<Math display tex={String.raw`g_{\mathrm{open}}=1-g_{\mathrm{closed}}`} label="Converting absolute gripper conventions" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e02" />

[Original lecture, 18:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1135s)

Octo's common convention is open=1 and closed=0. But alignment goes beyond flipping numbers: datasets may record current aperture, a relative “open farther” command, or binary closure. Recovering absolute opening state from relative commands may require reconstructing earlier history. Aligning this dimension after initial grasp failures illustrates that even a large Transformer does not automatically reconcile opposite target meanings. It does not mean every dataset's coordinate frames were fully standardized.

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={25} caption="Direct execution and fine-tuning" />

Zero-shot results use a model without additional training on new demonstrations for that evaluation. Fine-tuned results for coffee making or precision insertion follow training on those tasks' data. The two figure groups therefore have different starting conditions.

[Original lecture, 20:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1205s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={26} caption="Successes and failures behind the average" />

Videos revealing where a gripper closes and when an object slips can expose errors hidden by aggregate success rates. More hardware requires repeated trials and failure records by condition.

For the first Google-robot grasp shown, the policy ran on a remote server and Quan Vuong assisted with local evaluation. Google robot records were included in training, but testing occurred in another building. One successful video demonstrates operation at an unfamiliar location; success rate requires separate repeated trials.

[Original lecture, 21:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1280s)

Rather than applying a large ResNet separately to every image before fusion, Octo concentrated computation in small image tokenizers and a shared Transformer. It initially learned representations from robot data alone. The lecturer revisits this choice because it differs from later reuse of Internet-scale VLMs. The paper's gains over RT-1-X and comparability with the much larger RT-2-X concern its particular hardware, tasks, and data. Fewer parameters do not establish superiority for every semantic-understanding or new-hardware adaptation problem. [Octo research team](https://octo-models.github.io/)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={27} caption="New cameras and command formats" />

Adding a wrist camera or force sensor changes input format. Outputting end-effector target poses instead of seven joint velocities changes output format too.

[Original lecture, 22:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1340s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={28} caption="Change inputs and outputs while reusing representations" />

The upper Octo diagram shows pretraining and the lower fine-tuning. New sensor-tokenization modules and output heads are connected, using learned scene representations as initialization.

[Original lecture, 23:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1394s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={29} caption="Adapting to new tasks with few demonstrations" />

These demonstrations illustrate researchers starting from the released model. Approximately fifty refers to the scale of the cited application. Required demonstrations depend on task precision and relevance of pretraining data.

## What can be shared across different embodiments?

A new force–torque sensor requires an input module converting its readings into tokens. Joint-target output requires an appropriately formatted head. Octo places most parameters in the shared Transformer and fine-tunes these representations together with new interfaces. The lecture contrasts starting from an entire policy pretrained on robot actions with starting from a backbone pretrained only on visual representations. Spot and Japanese-company humanoid examples are downstream adaptations to particular equipment, not evidence of unchanged-checkpoint zero-shot control on those robots.

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={30} caption="Generality beyond a single arm" />

Wheeled navigation, quadruped locomotion, and drone flight differ greatly in observations and control. Testing such embodiments probes how much scene-reading representation can be shared.

[Original lecture, 25:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1532s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={31} caption="Combining manipulation, navigation, walking, and flight" />

CrossFormer combines about 900,000 trajectories across embodiments in one policy architecture. Recognizing the same obstacle does not make an arm's avoidance movement equivalent to a drone's altitude change.

[Original lecture, 26:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1594s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={32} caption="A shared backbone with embodiment-specific outputs" />

CrossFormer shares its Transformer backbone while assigning output heads by embodiment. A head converts shared representations into the equipment's control vector. This permits joint backbone training without forcing drones and dual-arm robots to have identical action dimensions. [CrossFormer project](https://crossformer-model.github.io/)

The lecture's examples control WidowX at 5 Hz and a dexterous dual-arm system at 50 Hz. Predicting one second therefore requires five versus fifty commands. Embodiment-specific heads can accommodate temporal resolution as well as control-vector dimension.

[Original lecture, 27:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1625s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={33} caption="Multiple executions from one model" />

The videos use shared representations from the same checkpoint but different connected controllers. Distinguish execution on supported equipment from immediate control of unseen equipment.

[Original lecture, 29:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1751s)

CrossFormer's videos do not combine separately selected best checkpoints for each robot: they select embodiment-specific heads from one checkpoint. Shared parameters are used extensively, including shared encoders for matching camera types. Navigation experience may help visual obstacle representations, but this is not copying wheel speeds directly into quadruped joint torques. Separating shared representations from embodiment-specific action transformations clarifies the benefit of joint learning.

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={34} caption="Compare against specialists on each embodiment" />

Embodiment-specific bars alongside averages matter. Large gains on some equipment can hide regressions elsewhere. Interpretation requires matched baselines, data, and trial counts.

[Original lecture, 30:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1822s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={35} caption="Demonstration savings from pretraining" />

TRI compared pretrained policies with policies trained from scratch on 29 tasks. Some settings involving environmental variation required three to five times less new-task data. This does not mean success rates multiplied by that factor on every task. [TRI source](https://toyotaresearchinstitute.github.io/lbm1/)

In blind A/B evaluations, operators judged outcomes without knowing the policy identity. Pretraining helped particularly when post-training data were scarce, while normalization choices also affected outcomes. Data preprocessing must therefore be recorded among experimental conditions. [TRI study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05331)

[Original lecture, 30:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=1857s)

## Transferring web-learned representations to robots

The TRI research introduced here studies diffusion-Transformer policies using about 1,700 hours of pretraining experience in real manipulation and simulation. Long dual-arm tasks such as peeling an apple offer more failure points than a short grasp. Blind evaluation and repeated trials separate pretraining effects from this variability. The observation that normalization can matter more than architectural changes underscores matching action units and preprocessing before comparing models. [TRI study](https://toyotaresearchinstitute.github.io/lbm1/)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={36} caption="Using Internet-learned representations for control" />

Learning only from robot data may require demonstrations even to understand a new object name. Reusing web-learned image–word relationships can help locate objects, while robot records teach grasping motions.

[Original lecture, 33:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2030s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={37} caption="LLaVA image tokens" />

An image encoder converts a photograph into vectors, and a projection layer maps them into a dimension the language model can read. The model reads these alongside text in the same sequence. [LLaVA project](https://llava-vl.github.io/)

[Original lecture, 34:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2098s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={38} caption="Can actions also become tokens?" />

Connecting continuous motion to next-word prediction requires a rule mapping it to indices. Execution also requires a rule decoding those indices back into physical units.

[Original lecture, 35:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2132s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={39} caption="From VLM to VLA" />

Adding action learning to a VLM produces a VLA predicting actions from instructions and images. Even with the same image input, descriptive text and physical controls require different training targets and data.

[Original lecture, 36:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2164s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={40} caption="Clip by quantiles, then assign indices" />

One extreme outlier can widen the full range and reduce resolution for common small movements. Set lower and upper references per action dimension, clip the range, then divide it into equal bins. The equation below simplifies the lecture's 256-bin example. [OpenVLA](https://openvla.github.io/)

<Math display tex={String.raw`q=\min(255,\lfloor256\,\mathrm{clip}((a-l)/(u-l),0,1)\rfloor)`} label="Dividing continuous actions into 256 bins" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e03" />

[Original lecture, 36:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2205s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={41} caption="Index accuracy versus physical error" />

Choosing the bin next to the correct one still counts as a token error. Also evaluate the physical discrepancy after decoding. The equation below gives mean absolute error; when combining dimensions with different units, report per-dimension values or normalization conventions as well.

The lecturer's approximately 95% is an empirical rule of thumb for monitoring autoregressive-model training. It is computed with teacher forcing, supplying correct preceding tokens, unlike rollout success under the model's own predictions and actual sensors. This motivates examining decoded control error at the same checkpoint too.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\hat a_i-a_i\rvert`} label="Mean absolute command error" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e04" />

Translate token accuracy into motion: a slightly-too-far leftward prediction counts as one token error. The same discrepancy has different consequences far from an object and immediately before contact with a thin glass cup. Examine token accuracy, per-dimension L1/L2 errors, and actual task success together. If rotations and translations were normalized to the same numerical range, their physical-axis errors should remain interpretable after decoding.

Teacher forcing may feed correct preceding action dimensions into later ones. Autonomous generation instead reuses preceding predictions. Thus a checkpoint's training metrics and actual execution can differ. Treat the lecturer's 95% guideline as a convergence clue while checking full object-transfer trajectories separately.

[Original lecture, 37:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2260s)

## Training heterogeneous records together

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={42} caption="Padding and masks" />

Empty positions can align a one-camera sample with a three-camera sample. A mask marks those positions as unobserved rather than measured. Standard dense computation still pays some cost for padding.

For example, padding samples with 100 and 300 camera tokens to length 300 gives 600 positions, of which 200 are empty. Attention masks prevent references but, depending on implementation, storage and computation remain. Padding simplifies formats; packing reduces empty positions but requires strict sample-boundary handling.

[Original lecture, 39:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2340s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={43} caption="Reducing empty positions through packing" />

Concatenating variable-length samples within a batch reduces padding. Record each sample's start and end to prevent unrelated images and instructions from mixing.

<Math display tex={String.raw`b_0=0,\qquad b_i=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\ell_j`} label="Boundaries in a concatenated sequence" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e05" />

[Original lecture, 40:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2436s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={44} caption="Blocking attention across sample boundaries" />

The diagonal blocks permit token references only within the same sample. Position indices restart for each sample too. Add causal constraints if the task also requires restricting temporal direction.

<Math display tex={String.raw`M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0 & s_i=s_j\\-\infty & s_i\ne s_j\end{cases}`} label="Blocking attention between samples" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e06" />

Concatenating lengths 3 and 5 gives boundaries 0, 3, and 8. Position indices are 0,1,2 for the first sample and restart at 0,1,2,3,4 for the second. Add a temporal causal mask if future actions must be hidden within each example. Packing must prevent examples from different homes and robots from leaking targets to one another.

[Original lecture, 41:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2492s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={45} caption="Reading throughput and mixing quality" />

Sequential reads suit storage devices, but a small buffer can crowd batches with neighboring frames from one demonstration. Random reads improve mixing while increasing storage seeks. Measure both throughput and embodiment/task proportions within batches when choosing a strategy.

Mees recalls poor generalization in early Octo runs using a roughly 20,000-sample buffer and trajectory-level shuffling. The team mixed frames across trajectories before image decoding and expanded the buffer to about 500,000 samples. Since both changes were introduced together, the result cannot be attributed solely to buffer size.

A shuffle buffer temporarily stores streamed data for random sampling. If one long demonstration fills most of it, different batches may repeatedly see almost the same table and arm poses. Large buffers of decoded images are expensive, so mixing compressed frames first and decoding only sampled images can help.

Building a full-dataset index and reading randomly provides direct access across the dataset. Slow seeks and small-file reads can leave GPUs waiting for batches. A compromise randomly selects large chunks and shuffles within them. The data-loader experience shows that the sequence of experience reaching an unchanged model can affect outcomes.

[Original lecture, 42:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2569s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={46} caption="Multiple output heads versus one action module" />

The left uses separate equipment-specific heads. The right uses one module outputting a common-length vector, with each embodiment selecting valid components. Equal vector lengths still require separately defined physical semantics per dimension.

Consider WidowX at 5 Hz and a dual-arm robot at 50 Hz. The same ten-step action chunk spans two seconds for the former and 0.2 seconds for the latter. Separate heads, as in CrossFormer, can specify different action dimensions and chunk lengths. A shared action module requires a maximum-size vector, valid-dimension masks, and equipment-specific mappings. Always examine chunk length alongside its physical time horizon.

[Original lecture, 46:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2767s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={47} caption="How does the model identify the embodiment?" />

The same photograph and instruction may require different commands for different connected arms. Embodiment can be specified explicitly or inferred from joint states and images.

[Original lecture, 48:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2898s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={48} caption="Specify equipment or infer it from observations" />

The left selects the specified embodiment's head. In the single-expert design on the right, images, proprioceptive states, and task instructions provide equipment clues. Whether these clues produce correct outputs on unfamiliar hardware needs separate evaluation.

[Original lecture, 48:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=2932s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={49} caption="Joint learning with FAST tokens and continuous actions" />

FAST supplies supervision through tokens compressing motion across time. A continuous action module uses flow matching to generate executable numerical chunks, while a stopped-gradient path prevents its loss from directly changing the backbone. Token and web-data losses still train the backbone. [Knowledge Insulation](https://www.pi.website/research/knowledge_insulation)

The paper's action expert reads VLM observation representations but not the ground-truth FAST action tokens used as training targets. Reference restrictions and gradient blocking serve different roles: one controls information available for prediction; the other controls which parameters a continuous-action loss updates. [Research team's explanation](https://www.pi.website/research/knowledge_insulation)

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\rm token}(\theta)+\lambda\mathcal L_{\rm act}(\phi,\operatorname{sg}(h_\theta))`} label="Stopping gradients from the continuous-action loss" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl09-e07" />

Read gradient insulation as two training paths. The backbone reads images and instructions and learns through FAST action-token and web-text prediction losses. The action expert reads backbone features and learns to predict continuous action chunks. Web cotraining helps preserve semantic representations, while the separated gradient path restricts how action learning changes the backbone. Sharing representations differs from letting every loss update every layer.

## Comparing simulation scores with physical robots

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={50} caption="Repeat evaluation across environments too" />

Scaling data and models also expands the evaluation fleet. Beyond a few illustrative videos, vary object positions or backgrounds and repeat matched conditions to make comparisons.

[Original lecture, 54:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dtofzDY9zuo&t=3250s)

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={51} caption="Changing conditions in physical evaluation" />

A bumped camera or shifted object can change results from the same checkpoint. Simulation makes initial conditions easier to repeat; real evaluation captures actual contact and friction.

Another lecture example replaces a broken motor. Changed joint zeros or sensor readings can make a policy interpret the same arm pose differently. As with camera shifts, record hardware settings and calibration before interpreting checkpoint comparisons.

<LectureSlide lecture={9} page={53} caption="When relative rankings matter more than absolute success rates" />

Each scatter point links real and simulated results for the same policy and task. Similar rankings can help shortlist candidates, but ranking transfer to new tasks and actual success rates require real-world verification.

For example, simulation might give A 70% and B 50%, while reality gives A 55% and B 40%. The numbers differ but A still outranks B. If new friction conditions reverse the ranking, simulation-based selection needs reevaluation there. Repeat policies from matched initial arrangements and check corresponding real tasks. These percentages illustrate rank correlation rather than report experimental measurements.

## Success rate and execution time in the invited talk

Quan Vuong compares success and execution time for the same tasks. Two policies may each succeed eight times out of ten, yet one takes thirty seconds and the other fifteen, changing throughput. The graph's horizontal axis is execution time and its vertical axis success rate. These figures illustrate reading the axes, rather than quote measurements. [Quan Vuong's invited talk, 3:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pzolgvyWEFY&t=223s)

Gato, an official reading, combines different observations and actions into sequences. RECAP in the π∗0.6 paper reuses experience from execution and human corrections to improve policies. Even the same pretrained policy changes according to which subsequent experiences are collected and used.
