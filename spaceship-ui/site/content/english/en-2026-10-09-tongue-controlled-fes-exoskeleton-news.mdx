> **Study stage:** A device-assisted task study with five participants who had cervical spinal cord injuries. It assessed performance while using the device. It did not demonstrate neurological recovery or long-term therapeutic benefit.

## The finding in one sentence

An intraoral tongue sensor let participants command finger flexion and extension. A tendon-driven hand exoskeleton combined with functional electrical stimulation (FES) helped people with cervical spinal cord injuries grasp objects.

## Why it matters

A person with severe motor paralysis needs an input channel compatible with their available movements. The study uses tongue input as an alternative to voice or electromyography and separates how the fingers close and open. It addresses control accessibility and muscle fatigue in assistive engineering and human–robot interaction (HRI).

## How the design differs

Fifteen inductive coils at the palate sense tongue input. Tendons flex the fingers to grasp an object. A brief burst of FES assists extension to release it. The study compared FES-assisted opening with passive extension.

### Does the hand move as soon as the tongue touches the input?

The user selects an on-screen command with their tongue. The device executes the selection without reading thoughts. Under the Methods protocol a grasp command activates after a hold of 0.8 seconds. Opening requires a one-second hold. This helps reject brief accidental touches and unintended opening during a grasp. The hold time is distinct from sensor latency and total time to complete a task.

After the command is confirmed, the sequence depends on the action. In the FES-opening condition, a grasp command first triggers two seconds of FES to prepare the open hand while the user aligns it. Pointing, which does not require grasping an object, omits that initial stimulation. The release command relaxes tendon tension and then applies two seconds of FES. In the passive condition, elastic wires on the back of the hand extend the fingers when tendon tension is released. Command hold, stimulation duration, and task completion time measure different intervals.

The actuator does not pull indefinitely. Fingertip contact force detects the grasp. For grasp types that are difficult to assess from fingertip sensing alone, a preset tendon force supplies an additional stopping criterion. Stopping here means ending the pulling motion. This criterion does not certify safety for every object and user. Separating selection, force generation, and stopping clarifies how the screen in Figure 2 connects to the device on the hand.

**Check your understanding.** Does the 0.8-second command hold mean every object takes only 0.8 seconds to grasp? Hand positioning, actuation, and contact take additional time. Mean completion time and input hold time cover different intervals.

## Tongue localization and hand-task success measure different things

The supplement tested input-plate localization separately. A precision positioning device held the activation unit (AU) fixed in place of a moving human tongue while the input plate moved in 0.5 mm increments in two directions. At each position the authors compared the one-second mean position estimate with the true position. The AU is the component detected by the plate. It does not measure hand force.

Root-mean-square (RMS) position errors were 1.2 mm for the nTRI and 1.7 mm and 1.6 mm for the mouse and keyboard regions of the comparison plate, respectively. An RMS across positions does not bound every instantaneous error at 1.2 mm. The edges and corners were excluded because the AU could not make proper contact there. The reported value therefore does not represent real-time accuracy throughout the mouth.

Activation error is the fraction of samples with too little signal for a position estimate. The authors counted those samples among roughly 30 readings per one-second interval at each position and summarized the results. This differs from the fraction of objects dropped or commands selected incorrectly. The supplement identifies its weighted-average-of-neighbors method as WAN but does not derive its weighting formula. The method name alone does not specify an equation to attribute to the paper.

## Evidence and comparison conditions

The study enrolled **five** people with cervical spinal cord injury. Across device-assisted daily-living tasks involving five grasp types, success was **96%** with FES-assisted opening and <strong>89%</strong> with passive opening.

Mean completion times were **12.7 ± 7.2 seconds** and **13.0 ± 7.6 seconds**, respectively. The participants wore the exoskeleton for these tasks. Depending on the participant, an experimenter helped with positioning, and some failed attempts were retried. The results do not measure recovery of unaided hand function.

Success rates use all attempts, including retries, as the denominator. Completion time was measured for successful attempts from the on-screen instruction to grasp detection, including positioning the object, reacting, and approaching. It does not include holding for five seconds and releasing. Success did require maintaining a stable grasp for those five seconds. Before the task, staff could position the object or stabilize the arm or wrist, but they did not support the object during the five-second hold.

Figure 5 reports a separate force experiment in which the experimenter controlled the exoskeleton with a gamepad while measuring participants’ pinch and grasp forces. Those results do not directly establish accuracy or independent usability of tongue control.

## The unaided baseline and the two assisted conditions

The paper describes an unaided hand baseline, an exoskeleton with passive hand opening, and an exoskeleton with FES-assisted opening. The three were not assessed on a single common clinical scoring scale.

The unaided baseline was assessed qualitatively from video. Some participants could lift objects using compensatory wrist extension, and available arm movement and assistance varied. The 96% and 89% results are attempt-level success rates under the two exoskeleton conditions. Subtracting an unaided observation from these percentages would not measure neurological recovery.

Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 include ISNCSCI motor examinations, part of the standardized classification of motor function after spinal cord injury. They report strength on a 0–5 scale and include dates of prior examinations. They describe the participants’ baseline histories. They do not compare strength before and after device use or show that the device increased strength.

**Check your understanding.** If an assisted grasp succeeds, can the person perform the same action after removing the device? Separate testing is needed. Assisted performance, residual motor capacity, and change after device removal are different outcomes.

## Main limitations and failure conditions

The sample comprised five participants and some tasks required experimenter assistance. The repeated-measures ANOVA and adjusted pairwise tests compared grasp and pinch forces. The force difference between exoskeleton conditions was not significant. These tests did not establish significance for the 96% versus 89% success rates. The percentages alone cannot establish broad clinical superiority.

Questionnaire responses on fatigue describe subjective experience. The study did not objectively quantify muscle fatigue using electromyography or a decline in force.

Testing took place on two consecutive days in sessions of up to three hours. The first day was for training and familiarization. Reports of comfort during the study do not establish comfort during months of home use. The questionnaire was exploratory and tailored to the study device. It was not administered as a standardized, validated usability scale. See “Experimental design” and “Questionnaire design” in the Methods.

Use may be limited if a participant cannot move the tongue reliably or FES does not generate sufficient finger extension. Portability of the control and stimulation equipment outside the laboratory requires further testing. Effectiveness, fatigue, and adverse effects during long-term independent home use remain unestablished.

## Reproducibility and interests

The authors state that data needed to evaluate their conclusions are in the paper and supplement. Code is available on reasonable request to the corresponding author. This review did not identify a public code repository or independent archive of individual participants’ raw data.

L. N. S. Andreasen Struijk disclosed being an inventor on related patent application WO/2023/046252 owned by Aalborg University. The other authors declared no competing interests.

## Why the candidate was held and what would prompt another review

The tongue input and hybrid actuation design merited attention. The original selection retained the article as a candidate pending evidence beyond the small, laboratory-assisted study.

Larger multicenter usability studies, several months of home use, fully wearable wireless equipment, objective fatigue and adverse-event outcomes, or anonymized trial-level data would support a fresh review.
