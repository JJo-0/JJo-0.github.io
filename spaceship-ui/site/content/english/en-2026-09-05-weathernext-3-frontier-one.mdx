## 1. What changed

<strong>WeatherNext 3 adds low-latency satellite observations to a global forecast pipeline that previously waited for numerical weather analyses. This lets it initialize a new forecast every hour.</strong>

## 2. Why the problem is difficult

In operational forecasting, <strong>the age of the observations</strong> matters as much as accuracy. Many global AI systems start from an analysis produced by numerical weather prediction (NWP). The WeatherNext 3 paper explains that analyses are produced every six hours. Once release latency is included, the newest available state may lag the atmosphere by six to twelve hours.

That delay matters most for rapidly changing conditions, including precipitation, surface temperature, and storms. Combining high spatial and temporal resolution with a global 15-day probabilistic ensemble also increases the computational load and the range of data the system must handle.

### Issue time and observation time can differ

A forecast published at 9 a.m. can include observations taken earlier. Gathering measurements from different instruments around the globe, checking their quality, and estimating a consistent atmospheric state take time. The resulting initial state is an analysis. Even a fast forecasting model can miss recent changes if its starting analysis is old.

Record issue time, observation time, and the time for which the prediction is valid. Lead time is the interval between initialization and that valid time. Better skill at a fixed lead time and a fresher forecast available to an operator at a fixed time are related but distinct outcomes. This is why WeatherNext 3 emphasizes latency. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### How new observations complement an existing analysis

The model combines recent satellite imagery with an analysis. Satellites do not measure every atmospheric variable at every altitude, so one fresh observation type cannot specify the whole atmospheric state. The architecture uses the complementary information in the two sources. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Geostationary satellites can watch a region continuously, but coverage and sensor characteristics vary. Image brightness and radiances are indirect observations of temperature or precipitation. Identify which input is mapped to each forecast variable, which analysis supplied training data, and what served as the evaluation target.

## 3. Limits of the earlier approach

WeatherNext 2 used a Functional Generative Network (FGN) and showed strong medium-range probabilistic performance. Its basic structure relied on a 0.25° grid and six-hour steps. Higher temporal resolution required a separate upsampler.

WeatherNext 3 extends the same FGN family. Its design <strong>handles analyses, satellite imagery, precipitation, station data, and tropical cyclone data at different resolutions within a single model.</strong>

## 4. The method in three steps

### ① Ingest recent satellite observations directly

The model receives an 11-channel geostationary satellite mosaic on a 0.1° grid at one-hour intervals. Operational latency for the latest frame is about an hour, letting the system incorporate recent atmospheric conditions sooner than if it waited for the next analysis alone.

![Inputs and outputs of WeatherNext 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig1_alt2.png)

*Figure 1. WeatherNext 3 inputs and outputs. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 1. Paper licensed under CC BY 4.0.*

### ② Combine resolutions on a common mesh

The 0.25° and 0.1° data have their own encoders and decoders and interact on a common icosahedral processor mesh. WeatherNext 3 generates a 64-member ensemble out to 15 days. For selected surface variables it directly predicts hourly outputs at 0.1° resolution.

### ③ Predict observations at off-grid locations

A sparse output head for stations uses elevation and land–sea information alongside latitude and longitude. It supports queries for 2 m temperature and dew point at arbitrary locations and times. The authors tested generalization at stations excluded from training.

### A grid cell and a station represent space differently

A resolution of 0.1° or 0.25° is an angular interval in latitude and longitude. The physical east–west distance between longitudes varies with latitude. A cell-average atmospheric state may differ from a station reading on a hillside or in the middle of a city.

Station forecasting addresses that distinction. A model that accepts position and elevation can produce a point estimate beyond the center of a grid cell. Querying an arbitrary coordinate does not independently validate the prediction there. This gives the held-out station results particular value. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

An ensemble samples possible futures. Its 64 outputs approximate a distribution; only one atmosphere is observed. Check the forecast spread against actual observations. A good ensemble mean can coexist with a distribution that is too narrow or too wide.

## 5. Experiments and results

In a held-out 2024 evaluation, medium-range upper-air variables showed roughly a <strong>5% improvement in continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)</strong> over WeatherNext 2. The authors interpreted this as about <strong>six extra hours of forecast lead time</strong> at comparable skill.

At held-out METAR stations and short lead times, CRPS for 2 m temperature was up to <strong>30%</strong> lower than for WeatherNext 2 and up to <strong>40%</strong> lower than for ECMWF ENS. Lower CRPS indicates a better probabilistic forecast.

![Evaluation at METAR stations excluded from training](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig2_station_eval_standalone.png)

*Figure 2. Evaluation at held-out METAR stations. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 3. CC BY 4.0.*

For early precipitation lead times, Figure 4a compares WN3's PARDIG head with the global probabilistic forecasting baselines WN2 and ECMWF ENS. It reports CRPS reductions as large as <strong>60%</strong> when evaluated against IMERG, <strong>30%</strong> against MRMS, and <strong>10%</strong> against gauges. The 2024 forecasts initialized at 00 and 12 UTC use 6-hour accumulations for IMERG and MRMS and 24-hour accumulations for gauges. The maxima do not represent universal gains at the same location and lead time. Hourly initialization yielded roughly <strong>two to three additional hours</strong> of operational lead time compared with six-hourly initialization.

![WeatherNext 3 precipitation evaluation and hourly initialization](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig3_combined.png)

*Figure 3. Precipitation CRPS, Brier scores, hourly initialization, and reliability. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 4. CC BY 4.0.*

In a separate announcement, Google said it had begun integrating WeatherNext 3 into Search, Gemini, Google Maps, the Google Maps Platform Weather API, and Earth Engine. This is <strong>Google’s account of its own rollout</strong>. Peer review of forecast performance addresses a different question.

### A probabilistic score measures confidence as well as the outcome

Predicting rain with 100% probability every day may look impressive on wet days and produce large errors on dry ones. Indistinct probabilities every day can in turn fail to distinguish risky conditions. The amount of confidence matters.

CRPS compares a forecast cumulative distribution with an observation, taking the location and spread of the distribution into account. Lower scores are better. Specify the variable, baseline, and lead time before citing an improvement. Scores across incompatible units cannot simply be added, and a maximum improvement does not apply at every time and place. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Calibration asks whether, over many comparable cases, events assigned 70% probability occur about 70% of the time. This differs from asking whether a point forecast was close to the observation. The precipitation evaluation reports CRPS, event-specific scores, and reliability separately.

### What each precipitation target observes

Satellites, radar-based products, and ground rain gauges observe precipitation differently, with distinct coverage and error characteristics. Gains against one target need not carry over to another. Comparing multiple sources is useful without constituting independent, long-term validation in the field. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

IMERG is also a WN3 training target. The paper notes that joint training with IMERG may indirectly influence the PARDIG head. MRMS primarily covers the contiguous United States, and gauges are unevenly distributed. Interpret the results in terms of target independence, coverage, and accumulation period. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 6. Limitations and counterevidence

Three limitations qualify these results.

First, this report was an <strong>arXiv v1 preprint</strong>. Second, the authors left extreme precipitation to future study because there were too few true positives. The event-based Brier scores cover <strong>moderate thresholds up to 4 mm per 6 hours</strong>. That bound concerns selected event thresholds. It neither makes the accumulation periods in the CRPS datasets identical nor implies that larger observed totals were discarded across the board. Heavy-rain performance remains open.

Third, individual ensemble samples exhibit hexagonal artifacts that trace the model mesh, along with temporal discontinuities at six-hour outer-step boundaries. The authors report that these effects are especially apparent in precipitation and station outputs.

![Spatial and temporal artifacts in WeatherNext 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig5_combined.png)

*Figure 4. Mesh artifacts in precipitation and a temperature discontinuity at a six-hour boundary. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 7. CC BY 4.0.*

The 2026 quasi-real-time comparison covered only about six weeks, from July 1 to August 11. Small differences over this window cannot establish long-term performance. The review also did not confirm the release of full WN3 weights or complete reproduction code.

### Better average skill leaves rare hazards to be tested

Very heavy precipitation provides few examples, and observation quality varies geographically. An improvement on ordinary days is insufficient to determine emergency-warning performance. Report missed events and false alarms separately, with thresholds and regions specified.

Spatial patterns and time-boundary artifacts in the paper show that the model architecture can leave a visible signature in its outputs. Ensemble averages can disguise features present in single members or at time-step transitions. Wind and solar operators would also need to test the downstream effects of those discontinuities. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Fast inference measures one stage. Collecting, preprocessing, and distributing the data also contribute to service latency. Reduced model compute and reliable end-to-end operations need separate measurements.

## 7. What could change in practice?

An important competitive question for AI weather forecasting may become <strong>how quickly a model can use real observations</strong>, alongside how well it reproduces an existing analysis.

A conventional system separates observation, data assimilation, forecasting, and post-processing. Connecting these steps more directly in a probabilistic model could change the architecture of an operational forecast system beyond simply providing a surrogate for NWP.

Outputs such as 100 m wind, cloud cover, and solar radiation could support wind and solar operations. The material reviewed contained <strong>no independent operational evidence</strong> of a reduction in power-generation forecast error or grid costs.

### The value of a faster update depends on the decision

Tomorrow's total generation and a sharp change one or two hours ahead present different operational decisions. Solar output depends on panel orientation, temperature, and curtailment as well as clouds and irradiance. A given reduction in weather error therefore need not produce the same reduction in generation forecast error.

Logistics and outdoor work likewise require a rule that translates probabilities into action. Underpredicting and overpredicting rain can carry different costs. A weather score is informative, while an economic or safety claim requires evaluation in the relevant decision system.

Hourly updates allow earlier responses to new observations. If consecutive forecasts swing widely, operators also need to know which one to act on. Useful operational evidence includes consistency across updates, delivery latency, fallback forecasts, and reliability in extreme events. <strong>Update frequency and decision quality each need a measurement before the two can be linked.</strong>

Track the version running in a service separately from the version evaluated in the paper. A product update and a fixed research result are separate records.

## 8. What evidence to watch for

The following developments would strengthen the case:

- Peer-reviewed publication
- At least a year of independent comparison in real-time operations
- Separate validation for extreme precipitation and hazardous weather
- Full model weights and code, or a publicly reproducible implementation
- Measured operational outcomes in renewable energy, logistics, or disaster response

Such evidence would support treating WeatherNext 3 as a <strong>change in weather infrastructure</strong> as well as a new AI model.
