## 1. What changed

<strong>WeatherNext 3 adds low-latency satellite observations to a global forecast pipeline that previously waited for numerical weather analyses. This lets it initialize a new forecast every hour.</strong>

## 2. Why the problem is difficult

In operational forecasting, <strong>the age of the observations</strong> matters as much as accuracy. Many global AI systems start from an analysis produced by numerical weather prediction (NWP). The WeatherNext 3 paper explains that analyses are produced every six hours. Once release latency is included, the newest available state may lag the atmosphere by six to twelve hours.

That delay matters most for rapidly changing conditions, including precipitation, surface temperature, and storms. Combining high spatial and temporal resolution with a global 15-day probabilistic ensemble also increases the computational load and the range of data the system must handle.

## 3. Limits of the earlier approach

WeatherNext 2 used a Functional Generative Network (FGN) and showed strong medium-range probabilistic performance. Its basic structure relied on a 0.25° grid and six-hour steps. Higher temporal resolution required a separate upsampler.

WeatherNext 3 extends the same FGN family. Its design <strong>handles analyses, satellite imagery, precipitation, station data, and tropical cyclone data at different resolutions within a single model.</strong>

## 4. The method in three steps

### ① Ingest recent satellite observations directly

The model receives an 11-channel geostationary satellite mosaic on a 0.1° grid at one-hour intervals. Operational latency for the latest frame is about an hour, letting the system incorporate recent atmospheric conditions sooner than if it waited for the next analysis alone.

![Inputs and outputs of WeatherNext 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig1_alt2.png)

*Figure 1. WeatherNext 3 inputs and outputs. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 1. Paper licensed under CC BY 4.0.*

### ② Combine resolutions on a common mesh

The 0.25° and 0.1° data have their own encoders and decoders and interact on a common icosahedral processor mesh. WeatherNext 3 generates a 64-member ensemble out to 15 days. For selected surface variables it directly predicts hourly outputs at 0.1° resolution.

### ③ Predict observations at off-grid locations

A sparse output head for stations uses elevation and land–sea information alongside latitude and longitude. It supports queries for 2 m temperature and dew point at arbitrary locations and times. The authors tested generalization at stations excluded from training.

## 5. Experiments and results

In a held-out 2024 evaluation, medium-range upper-air variables showed roughly a <strong>5% improvement in continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)</strong> over WeatherNext 2. The authors interpreted this as about <strong>six extra hours of forecast lead time</strong> at comparable skill.

At held-out METAR stations and short lead times, CRPS for 2 m temperature was up to <strong>30%</strong> lower than for WeatherNext 2 and up to <strong>40%</strong> lower than for ECMWF ENS. Lower CRPS indicates a better probabilistic forecast.

![Evaluation at METAR stations excluded from training](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig2_station_eval_standalone.png)

*Figure 2. Evaluation at held-out METAR stations. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 3. CC BY 4.0.*

For precipitation at early lead times, the WN3 PARDIG head reportedly reduced CRPS by up to approximately <strong>60%</strong> against IMERG, <strong>30%</strong> against MRMS, and <strong>10%</strong> against rain gauges. Initializing every hour instead of every six hours provided about <strong>two to three additional hours</strong> of operational lead time.

![WeatherNext 3 precipitation evaluation and hourly initialization](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig3_combined.png)

*Figure 3. Precipitation CRPS, Brier scores, hourly initialization, and reliability. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 4. CC BY 4.0.*

In a separate announcement, Google said it had begun integrating WeatherNext 3 into Search, Gemini, Google Maps, the Google Maps Platform Weather API, and Earth Engine. This is <strong>Google’s account of its own rollout</strong>. Peer review of forecast performance addresses a different question.

## 6. Limitations and counterevidence

Three limitations qualify these results.

First, the report was an <strong>arXiv v1 preprint</strong>. Second, too few true positives were available to evaluate extreme precipitation reliably. The authors <strong>limited the evaluation to moderate precipitation up to 4 mm per six hours</strong> and left extremes to future work. Performance in heavy rain remains an open question.

Third, individual ensemble samples exhibit hexagonal artifacts that trace the model mesh, along with temporal discontinuities at six-hour outer-step boundaries. The authors report that these effects are especially apparent in precipitation and station outputs.

![Spatial and temporal artifacts in WeatherNext 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03582v1/Figures/fig5_combined.png)

*Figure 4. Mesh artifacts in precipitation and a temperature discontinuity at a six-hour boundary. Source: Rasp et al., arXiv:2609.03582v1, Figure 7. CC BY 4.0.*

The 2026 quasi-real-time comparison covered only about six weeks, from July 1 to August 11. Small differences over this window cannot establish long-term performance. The review also did not confirm the release of full WN3 weights or complete reproduction code.

## 7. What could change in practice?

An important competitive question for AI weather forecasting may become <strong>how quickly a model can use real observations</strong>, alongside how well it reproduces an existing analysis.

A conventional system separates observation, data assimilation, forecasting, and post-processing. Connecting these steps more directly in a probabilistic model could change the architecture of an operational forecast system beyond simply providing a surrogate for NWP.

Outputs such as 100 m wind, cloud cover, and solar radiation could support wind and solar operations. The material reviewed contained <strong>no independent operational evidence</strong> of a reduction in power-generation forecast error or grid costs.

## 8. What evidence to watch for

The following developments would strengthen the case:

- Peer-reviewed publication
- At least a year of independent comparison in real-time operations
- Separate validation for extreme precipitation and hazardous weather
- Full model weights and code, or a publicly reproducible implementation
- Measured operational outcomes in renewable energy, logistics, or disaster response

Such evidence would support treating WeatherNext 3 as a <strong>change in weather infrastructure</strong> as well as a new AI model.
