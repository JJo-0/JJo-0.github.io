**Start with the basic idea.** Raising the charging voltage can store more energy but can shorten lifetime by distorting the material. The researchers made **small rod-shaped crystals that recover their structure well after charging**. The image below is a micrograph from the paper.

<BeginnerGuide lang="en" title="Start with the diagrams if cathodes, voltage and the H2–H3 transition are unfamiliar">

<HighNickelCathodePrimer lang="en" />

The <strong>cathode</strong> releases lithium ions on charging and accepts them again on discharge. LiNiO₂, with layers of lithium, nickel and oxygen, is the simplest end member of this high-nickel cathode family.

- <strong>Cutoff voltage</strong> is the upper voltage at which charging stops. Raising it can extract more lithium but places greater stress on the material.
- <strong>Specific capacity</strong> is charge stored or released per gram of active material, usually in mAh/g.
- <strong>H2 and H3 phases</strong> are layered crystal states reached at different states of charge. The spacing along the c-axis can shrink abruptly during the H2-to-H3 transition.
- <strong>Capacity retention</strong> is later discharge capacity as a fraction of that in a reference cycle. Always identify that reference.
- A <strong>nanorod</strong> is an elongated nanoscale structure. The name alone does not guarantee performance.

H3 still forms. The authors report that the nanorods undergo H2↔H3 but recover their bulk structure better on discharge. The study asks how reversible a transition can be.

</BeginnerGuide>

## 1. A different way to tolerate high voltage

A September 26, 2026 *Nature Communications* paper reports that high-nickel layered cathodes with nanorod morphology, particularly LiNiO₂, can cycle more stably at high cutoff voltage than conventional microcrystalline material. **The authors report bulk structural reversibility up to 4.95 V while the H2–H3 transition still takes place.**<Cite n={1} />

This is a materials and laboratory-electrode cycling study. It does not establish a commercial 4.95 V battery. Automotive pouch cells, full cells at high areal loading, hundreds to thousands of cycles, abuse safety, gas generation and manufacturing yield each need separate testing.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

The design addresses a longstanding high-nickel cathode problem by asking **how much of the structure returns after the transition** and changing morphology to improve that recovery. Public source data and transparent peer-review material let readers trace the reported numbers.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={3} />

## 2. Why increase nickel content and voltage?

For a simple energy estimate, multiply mean voltage by the usable charge. Reversibly extracting more lithium and using a higher voltage could increase energy per mass of active material. This is one reason for interest in nickel-rich layered oxides.

High nickel content and a high state of charge also put stability under pressure. As lithium leaves, transition-metal oxide layer spacing changes, oxygen and surface reactions become more important, and stress changes within and around particles. Abrupt c-axis contraction during H2→H3 can produce different strains in different parts of a particle.<Cite n={1} />

Repeated contraction and expansion can produce microcracks, electrolyte infiltration, new reaction surfaces and loss of electrical contact. Degradation depends on morphology, surface chemistry, electrolyte, temperature and charging protocol. The mere appearance of H3 does not guarantee immediate failure.

LiNiO₂ is the high-nickel end member without cobalt or manganese, making this problem particularly clear. Synthesis, storage and surface stabilization are also difficult. High-voltage reversibility in LiNiO₂ is a strong test of a design principle, but does not establish the same result in every commercial NCM cathode.

## 3. What question does this study add?

One research direction moderates H2–H3 or shifts it to a higher voltage using compositional doping, surface coatings, concentration gradients, single crystals or electrolyte design. These approaches aim to reduce abrupt lattice changes and side reactions.

Could a material form H3 yet avoid severe capacity fade if it recovers sufficiently on discharge? The team compared nanorod high-nickel materials with conventional microcrystals. Diffraction and spectroscopy tracked bulk and surface changes as a function of state of charge.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

The authors' interpretation goes beyond short diffusion length. They propose that strain accommodation in rod-shaped particles, crystallographic orientation and surface response help restore the structure after H2↔H3. In this account **morphology changes the reversibility of the phase transition**.<Cite n={1} />

Morphology also changes surface area and electrolyte contact. These experiments do not independently isolate size, shape and surface chemistry one variable at a time. Copying the external rod shape into another composition or process does not guarantee the same result.

## 4. What are H2 and H3, and how is reversibility measured?

H1, H2 and H3 name different hexagonal crystal states reached during charging. **The numbers do not specify the number of layers in a repeat unit.** This convention differs from stacking labels such as O3. The important observation for a beginner is that **X-ray diffraction peaks and lattice parameters change with state of charge**.

The c-axis contracts as H2→H3 progresses near the end of charge. On discharge, the material passes through H3→H2 and lower states of charge. How closely diffraction patterns and lattice parameters return indicates bulk reversibility. The authors connect operando and ex situ structural characterization with cycling.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

Reversible means that the predominant bulk structure returned at the measured spatial and temporal resolution. Surface reconstruction over a few nanometers, electrolyte interphases, small defects and long-term accumulated damage can persist. Bulk reversibility and long-term electrochemical stability require separate evidence.

A 4.95 V upper limit is demanding for a cathode. Reaching it once differs from sustaining capacity and efficiency across many cycles. The paper reports cycling data, but its cycle count, loading and cell configuration should be interpreted on their own terms.

**Finish with i.** This test raises the cutoff to 4.95 V for two nanorod compositions. Panels b and e stop at 4.7 V. Panel i has no directly matched microcrystal curve at 4.95 V. Three formation cycles at 0.1 C preceded charging at 0.5 C and discharging at 1 C at 25 ± 1°C. This study defines 1 C as 180 mA/g. The retention denominator matters. Low-rate formation followed by higher-rate cycling means the first and fourth cycles may use different conditions.

At 2.7–4.95 V, nanorod LiNiO₂ gave 196.62 mAh/g at cycle 4 and 181.64 at cycle 100, or about 92.4% by the same definition. It retained capacity at high voltage through that test. Behavior beyond cycle 100 and the lithium inventory of a full cell remain separate questions.<Cite n={3} />

At cycle 1 within 2.7–4.7 V, n-LNO discharged 226.42 mAh/g and m-LNO 187.89 mAh/g. Formation conditions and initial coulombic efficiency also matter. For the longer comparison, follow trajectories within the same rate interval alongside structural analysis.<Cite n={3} />

High capacity at cycle 100 even at 4.95 V merits attention. A single capacity curve cannot establish voltage efficiency, electrolyte oxidation, impedance growth or thermal stability. The lithium source of a cathode half-cell also differs from the limited inventory in a commercial full cell.

## 7. What separates these results from a commercial battery?

<NewsFigure media="sep28-linio2-s9" lang="en" />

### Reading Supplementary Figure S9: does every dark line mark a fractured particle?

Rows from top to bottom show n-LNO, n-Ni75, m-LNO and m-Ni75. The left shows initial states, the middle shows states after charging to 4.7 V and after cycling, and the right shows discharge after 100 cycles. Surface changes remain even on the nanorods. **The authors attribute the patterns in b and c to cracks in the surface SEI layer. They do not identify fractures of the nanorod crystals themselves.** A single photo cannot establish crack composition or depth.<Cite n={2} />

The final column also uses different windows by row. The nanorods cycled at 2.7–4.95 V, m-LNO at 2.7–4.1 V and m-Ni75 at 2.7–4.4 V before discharge. The column therefore cannot rank four materials under identical conditions. Read the residual surface changes alongside the bulk reversibility results.

First consider loading and density. Nanorods can differ from conventional secondary particles in surface area and packing density. Good gravimetric capacity at low areal loading may coexist with poorer volumetric energy or transport in a thick electrode.

Second, consider the electrolyte and voltage window. A 4.95 V cutoff stresses common carbonate electrolytes and interfaces. Even if the cathode lattice returns, electrolyte oxidation, gas, collector or binder instability, and transition-metal dissolution may limit system life.

Third, consider full-cell balance. A lithium-metal counterelectrode in a half-cell differs from graphite or silicon in a full cell in available lithium, N/P ratio and formation loss. Cathode capacity from the workbook cannot directly specify automotive cell energy density.

Fourth, consider manufacturing. Uniform large-scale nanorod synthesis must manage residual lithium, moisture sensitivity and particle breakage. Morphology must survive slurry mixing, coating and calendering. Compatibility with existing equipment and production costs also matter.

## 8. Which statements are general background?

<NewsFigure media="sep28-linio2-cover" lang="en" />

The editorial diagram simplifies the **design rationale** behind the micrographs and cycling curves. It does not measure atomic positions or add experimental results. Figures 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figure S9 are included under CC BY 4.0 with panels, axes and scales retained. PDF figures were converted to PNG after excluding surrounding prose. Measurement curves and colors were preserved.

The descriptions of cathodes, voltage and capacity are general battery science. H2–H3 transitions and high-nickel degradation are established research topics. Stable cycling of these LiNiO₂ nanorods up to 4.95 V, greater bulk reversibility and the cycle-100 source-data numbers are **findings of this paper**.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={3} />

The authors link H2↔H3 reversibility with morphology. The article's 96.3%, 44.0% and 92.4% values were recalculated from public data using cycle 4 to match rates. Changing the reference changes the retention.

The authors provide an open-access article and data and declare no competing interests.<Cite n={1} /> Data availability enables scrutiny but is distinct from independent experimental replication. Check the DOI page for final publication status and subsequent corrections. This review used the accepted peer-reviewed article's permanent DOI, supplement and source data available at the time.

## 9. What to check before the next voltage record

Test areal capacity and volumetric energy at high loading. Do thick nanorod electrodes retain ionic and electronic transport and mechanical integrity? A second test is practical full-cell lithium balance with a graphite or silicon-containing anode.

Third, record hundreds or more cycles at 4.8–4.95 V alongside impedance, gas and thermal response. Fourth, use postmortem microscopy and surface spectroscopy to ask whether bulk reversibility carries over to surface and interfacial stability. Batch size and electrode manufacturing yield also matter.

If these tests succeed, the design could influence high-nickel cathode development. **The morphology accommodates a transition and allows recovery.** The present evidence establishes a materials principle under aggressive voltage conditions, with commercial qualification still open.
