## The finding in one sentence

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) lists **$967,104,372** in total allocations across jurisdictions for FY2025 and FY2026. The DOE page explains that funding for this application round comes from allocations under the appropriations acts for those fiscal years. The table total therefore does not represent newly authorized funding, money already paid or spent, completed installations or measured outage reductions. What this article verifies for FY2025 and FY2026 is the allocation table and the application routes. Evaluating the actual effect requires knowing what recipients installed and measuring customer and grid outcomes on a consistent basis.

## How does electricity reach a customer?

Power from a generating station travels over high-voltage transmission lines and through substations before it reaches distribution lines and feeders. Transformers and wiring complete the final connection to buildings. The extent of an outage and how power can be restored depend on where a failure occurs, whether on a line, at a substation or in a protection and control device.

A feeder is a distribution circuit that carries electricity from a substation to customers. Transferring customers to a different circuit requires isolating the faulted section and confirming that a connected route has capacity to spare. To evaluate a “grid strengthening” project, we need to know the equipment, the area it serves and the type of failure it addresses.

> **Basic concept.** Resilience investments can (1) make faults and outages less likely or (2) reduce how many customers lose service, or how long restoration takes, after a fault. These related aims require different measurements.

## What DOE announced about allocations and applications

DOE’s FY2025–FY2026 notice covers the fourth and fifth years of a five-year formula grant program. A formula grant allocates a funding limit to each jurisdiction according to statutory criteria. The program description reviewed lists factors such as population, area, disaster probability and severity, and past expenditure on mitigation. The announcement does not print the full allocation equation, so this article neither recalculates allocations nor estimates individual weights.

Existing recipients and new applicants follow different procedures. Organizations funded in FY2022–FY2024 request their FY2025 and FY2026 allocations. Eligible organizations that did not apply during that period submit new applications. The amended announcement provides a 120-day window for both routes and lists a deadline of <strong>February 17, 2027, at 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time</strong>. New applicants need to check prerequisites including SAM registration, a UEI and FedConnect. Required documents for existing recipients may depend on whether their project plan has changed.

It combines the FY2025 and FY2026 amounts allocated across jurisdictions, including states, territories and Tribal governments. The row cannot identify final local project selections, contracts awarded or payments made. **Allocation, application or request, project selection, spending and installation, and outcome measurement** are distinct stages.

## Connecting physical equipment to measurable results

DOE advises recipients to select metrics that fit the project’s purpose, scope and beneficiaries. **Build metrics** describe what was installed or changed. **Impact metrics** track changes in outages and restoration. **Equity and community benefit metrics** track how benefits are distributed and how residents participate. Miles of underground line and the number of automated switches document equipment. Measuring outage reductions requires additional data. An improvement in the average also leaves open how vulnerable areas, critical loads and customers facing long outages fared.

## Two kinds of investment: preventing faults and limiting their impact

### 1. Reinforcing lines and equipment to prevent outages

Projects can reinforce vulnerable parts of lines, poles and substations, reconductor existing lines, move exposed overhead sections underground or manage vegetation. DOE’s metrics guide links these physical improvements to outage prevention. Their effectiveness depends on local causes of failure. Trees falling in high winds and a substation flooded by rising water call for different measures. Undergrounding can reduce exposure above ground while introducing different conditions for flooding, access and repair.

Build records can identify the number and length of lines or poles reinforced, equipment types and exposed sections. Impact measures include outages by cause, customer interruptions and outage duration attributable to the hazard. A year with fewer severe weather events can have fewer outages without any project effect, so exposure and comparison periods also matter.

### 2. Isolating the fault and restoring some customers by another route

FLISR stands for Fault Location, Isolation, and Service Restoration. It locates a fault, isolates the damaged section and, where another circuit is available, reroutes power to some customers. DOE explains that automation requires communications and operating software as well as monitors and sectionalizing switches. The fault has already occurred when FLISR responds. If there is no alternative circuit or spare capacity, or communications or control fail, both the number of customers that can be resupplied and the restoration speed are limited.

Build metrics for FLISR record the installation. Impact metrics track changes in the number of interrupted customers, outage duration and restoration speed. For a single-feeder installation, DOE suggests examining feeder reliability and considering comparison with the entire service territory. Report the scopes separately. Mixing a territory-wide average with the direct feeder measurement can dilute or misrepresent the project effect.

### Matching conditions before comparing investments

Using identically named metrics is insufficient to compare preventive upgrades with FLISR. Identify the targeted feeder or area first and show the utility-wide value as a separate reference. Use the five years before the project for the baseline, then apply the same metric definitions and comparable periods after operation starts. State whether all outages are included and how Major Event Days (MEDs) are defined and reported separately.

Storm severity, season, customer numbers and loads, other equipment investments and maintenance practices can all affect observed changes. When only before-and-after averages are available, record these confounders and, where possible, examine similar comparison areas or results by cause.

## Two outage averages: SAIDI and SAIFI

Table A.2 of the DOE guide defines **SAIDI** as total customer interruption duration in customer-minutes divided by the total number of customers. The summation symbol Σ means to add the values for all customers. In symbols, `SAIDI = Σ(outage minutes for each customer) ÷ total customers`. Its unit is minutes per customer in the reporting period, and the denominator includes customers who never lost power. A SAIDI of 20 minutes per customer describes an average. Individual customers need not have experienced 20 minutes of interruption.

**SAIFI** divides the sum of customer interruptions by the total number of customers: `SAIFI = Σ(interruptions for each customer) ÷ total customers`. Its unit is interruptions per customer in the reporting period. This measures frequency across the customer population, distinct from the percentage of customers who lost power. Together, SAIDI and SAIFI distinguish outage duration from frequency.

### A worked example: the same customers interrupted for different durations

Suppose 20 of 100 customers each lose power once for 120 minutes. Total interruption time is 20×120=2,400 customer-minutes. SAIDI is `2,400÷100=24 minutes/customer`, and SAIFI is `20÷100=0.20 interruptions/customer`.

Now suppose automatic switching restores 15 customers after 10 minutes and the remaining five after 120 minutes. Total interruption time becomes `(15×10)+(5×120)=750 customer-minutes` and SAIDI falls to `750÷100=7.5 minutes/customer`. All 20 affected customers still experienced one interruption, so SAIFI remains 0.20 interruptions per customer. These are hypothetical examples of how averages behave. They are neither observations from this funding round nor estimates of its effect.

### The same SAIDI can conceal very different customer experiences

Consider two areas with 100 customers each. In area A, one customer loses power for 2,400 minutes while the other 99 remain connected. In area B, every customer loses power for 24 minutes. Both areas total 2,400 customer-minutes and have SAIDI of 24 minutes per customer. Yet one person in A endured a 40-hour outage, while everyone in B endured 24 minutes. The average hides both the longest outages and the distribution of benefits.

SAIFI differs, at 0.01 interruptions per customer in A and 1 in B. Even these two averages cannot reveal whether outages interrupted critical loads such as hospitals, fire services or water supplies, repeatedly affected particular communities, or left individuals without power for more than 24 hours. Customer-level distributions, counts of long interruptions, outage causes, critical services and geographic and socioeconomic patterns can better explain what resilience investments deliver to the public.

CAIDI, the Customer Average Interruption Duration Index, divides total customer interruption duration by the total number of customer interruptions. It has units of minutes per interruption, whereas SAIDI includes all customers in its denominator. In our first example CAIDI is 2,400÷20=120 minutes per interruption. With automatic switching it is 750÷20=37.5 minutes per interruption. Both calculations use the hypothetical conditions above.

Alongside CAIDI, the guide lists time to restore 50%, 90% and 100% of customers, individual customer interruptions of 24 hours or more, critical-service interruptions, failures by cause and recovery cost. Time to the three restoration thresholds need not change in step. The last 5–10% of customers may wait much longer because homes were destroyed or crews cannot reach them. An overall average cannot characterize the most difficult part of restoration.

## Why a five-year baseline and event definitions matter

DOE recommends five years of baseline data before implementation and consistent metrics throughout the project’s performance period. Comparisons require a start date, the feeder and customer population, and the aggregation period. A project entering service in January contributes more operating months to the first annual reporting period than one starting in December. If boundaries or customer counts change, check that before-and-after denominators and coverage remain comparable.

Major Event Day classification is also important. Table A.2 mentions the IEEE 1366 2.5 Beta method and explains that utilities and regulators may use different criteria. If a subrecipient uses a different definition, recording it and reporting MED results separately helps prevent extreme events either from overwhelming routine reliability trends or from disappearing from the analysis. Saying only “all outages included” obscures these choices.

The observed before-and-after difference cannot by itself establish a causal effect. Storms, fires, cold spells, asset replacement, investments in other circuits, maintenance cycles and utility measurement practices may all change during the period. Comparison areas should resemble the project area in hazards, customer composition and network structure. Costs and the regional distribution of benefits also matter, even when aggregate observations improve.

## What to look for in subsequent public records

When follow-up records become available, check the following in sequence.

1. **Disbursement.** After allocation to jurisdictions, do the records identify project selections, contracts, spending and dates?
2. **Construction.** How many miles of line or units of what equipment were installed on which feeder? When did operation begin?
3. **Population and scope.** Does the sample cover the service territory or the target feeder? What customer count and denominator are used?
4. **Performance baseline.** Do the five pre-project years and the follow-up use common definitions of SAIDI, SAIFI, outage counts and restoration time?
5. **Extreme events.** Are MED criteria and coverage of outages stated? Are MED results shown separately?
6. **Beneficiaries.** Is there evidence on vulnerable areas, critical loads, customers with long outages, employment and community participation?
7. **Costs and alternatives.** Are installation, maintenance, damage repair and recovery costs available, along with results under comparable conditions?

DOE’s guide says it will collect and analyze implementing agencies’ data and release results, while withholding identifiable project information without recipient permission. The detail available publicly may therefore vary by project. Document what can be verified, what may remain private and the difference between aggregate and project-level data.

## Limits of the evidence at the time of publication

The documents describe allocations, applications and performance measurement. They do not demonstrate a specific project’s spending, amount installed, completion date or post-project outage metrics. The sources below identify what was reviewed.

The SAIDI and SAIFI values here are arithmetic teaching examples. The comparison table specifies how future evidence could be matched between approaches. Equipment installed, stated program goals and measured outcomes are different observations. Any claim that this funding reduced outages must await actual recipient data.
