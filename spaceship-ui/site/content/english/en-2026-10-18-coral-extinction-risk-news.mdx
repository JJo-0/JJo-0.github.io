> **Species-level assessment of relative extinction risk.** This account was prepared as a review draft. The 44% figure is the share of species with sufficient data that fell into a threatened category. It is neither a coral mortality rate nor a 44% extinction probability for each species.

## Main finding

Of 892 coral species reassessed, 774 had sufficient data. Of those, 340 (44%) fell into an IUCN threatened category. The assessment combines observations, proxies, and climate scenarios to classify relative risk. It does not predict when each species will go extinct.

## Why the reassessment matters

The authors updated the 2008 assessment using revised taxonomy, changes in coral cover, projected severe bleaching over 30 years, and species-specific susceptibility. During bleaching, stressed corals lose symbiotic algae and turn pale. Bleaching and death are separate events. SSP2-4.5 is the socioeconomic and emissions scenario used for the projections. The assessment gives conservation planners a species-level basis for priorities and budgets.

## Figures and comparison conditions

Among 774 assessable species, 33 were Critically Endangered, 251 Endangered, and 56 Vulnerable. Together these categories account for 340 species, or 44%. The official study page describes supplementary data on range, depth, coral cover, heat stress, trait scores, and final classification for the 892 species.

## How were the categories assigned?

The main method, IUCN Criterion A, considers observed or estimated population reductions over the longer of ten years or three generations. Lacking better generation-length estimates, the authors retained the ten-year assumption and used a 30-year window. Under A2, A3, and A4, the reduction thresholds begin at 30% for Vulnerable, 50% for Endangered, and 80% for Critically Endangered. **These percentages refer to population reduction over the assessment window.** They do not estimate extinction probability. This describes the main route used in the study, which is only one part of the IUCN criteria (accepted manuscript, Methods, PDF p. 15 / printed p. 14).

Most species lacked 30 years of population data across their entire range. The authors used coral cover, the share of seabed area occupied by live coral, as a proxy and adjusted it for susceptibility traits. A decrease in cover and a directly counted decline in a particular species' population are different measurements.

When species-specific trait data were missing, the authors assigned the intermediate susceptibility score of 2. Scores 1, 2, and 3 represent low, medium, and high susceptibility. An assigned score should be distinguished from an observed trait. The forward analysis also incorporates projected recurrent severe bleaching during 2020–2050 and assumptions about adaptation (Methods, printed pp. 14–15).

**Reader check.** Does a 50% decline threshold for Endangered mean a 50% probability of extinction? No. For each species, check which criterion and evidence support the category. Relative risk, extinction probability, and time to extinction are distinct quantities.

## Which years does the 2026 paper represent?

Supplementary p. 2 describes a 2024 reassessment. Atlantic assessments appeared in the 2022 Red List update and Indo-Pacific assessments in the 2024 update. Publication in 2026 does not establish a new survey of all corals in that year. How subsequent mass bleaching events enter the assessment requires a separate check.

Historical comparisons need a consistent method. Table 1 recalculates a time series for 1998, 2008, and 2024 using the current approach. On that basis, risk increased for 100 species in 1998–2008 and 210 in 2008–2024. Only genuine changes in risk enter the Red List Index. Reclassifications due to new data, taxonomy, or methods are treated separately (Table 1 and supplementary pp. 12–15).

The originally published 2008 assessment used a different method and denominator. Supplementary Table SI5 gives 145 threatened species out of 774 for 2008 recalculated under the current method, compared with 231 out of 704 in the original assessment. Subtracting those counts cannot measure recovery or a change in extinction probability.

**Reader check.** Does publication this year date all the field observations to this year? Check the publication date, assessment date, and observation window separately.

### What did the researchers observe in place of species counts?

Supplementary pp. 5–6 report global species-specific population estimates for only 15 species. For the others, the analysis draws on the GCRMN 2020 report, which pooled coral cover observations from 1978 to 2019 and modeled gaps across space and time. The past-decline calculation used a 30-year window ending in 2019. A “2024 reassessment” therefore includes observations from earlier years.

For a species distributed across several regions, the authors weighted regional changes in cover by each region's share of its range. These dimensionless weights sum to one. Areas with more monitoring stations do not automatically count more. The assumption that a region's share of range approximates its share of the population was not directly verified. Depending on susceptibility score, the supplement selects the 20th, 50th, or 80th percentile of estimated decline. A score of 3 selects a value within the uncertainty distribution. It does not mean an 80% decline.

## Limits and contrary evidence

### Is deeper water always a refuge?

Supplementary p. 7 explains that the forward calculation considers the share of a species' depth range within 0–30 m and the part of its geographic range expected to experience annual severe bleaching before 2050. A widespread species need not experience equal heat stress everywhere in the same year.

Depth is a proxy for exposure, not a survival rate measured by counting every individual. The treatment of habitat below 30 m as less exposed to surface heat stress is a modeling assumption. It cannot guarantee a refuge for every species and location. Depending on evidence and depth, the analysis assumes either no adaptation or 1°C of adaptation. It did not measure every population as already having evolved a 1°C tolerance.

**Reader check.** Does including the proportion of deeper habitat establish that survival was observed there? Check the modeled exposure and field observations independently. Conflating them can overstate the extent of a refuge.

The 118 species between the total of 892 and the 774 assessed with sufficient data cannot simply be counted as unthreatened. Nor does the study support changing the denominator of 44% to 892 or assigning the data-deficient group the same risk profile.

Most species lacked direct population trends. Global coral cover decline and projected future severe bleaching under SSP2-4.5 served as proxies. The 44% does not identify imminent extinctions or their timing. Regional sampling bias, recovery, local adaptation, or a different future emissions pathway can change classifications.
