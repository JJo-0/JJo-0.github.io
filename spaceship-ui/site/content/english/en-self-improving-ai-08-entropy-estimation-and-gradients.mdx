The upper half reads a given token sequence as context and sharpens the distribution at each position. The lower half generates several output paths and trains the model to select paths with lower costs more often. The arrows and multiplication signs are schematic. Use the exact derivatives in the paper when implementing them. [Figure 1 and equations (8)–(9)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08186v1#page=3)

Keep four questions distinct as you read these five slides.

| Quantity | Question |
|---|---|
| Objective value | What is the entropy over all answers the model can produce? |
| Sample estimate | How can one generated answer estimate that quantity? |
| Fixed-sample gradient | What do we get if we fix the sampled tokens and differentiate the loss? |
| Full objective gradient | Have we included the effect of sampling different answers as the parameters change? |

We use <Math tex={String.raw`x`} /> for the input, <Math tex={String.raw`Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_T)`} /> for the random token sequence and <Math tex={String.raw`y`} /> for one sampled sequence. <Math tex={String.raw`p_\theta(y\mid x)`} /> is the probability that the model generates that sequence. We use natural logarithms and measure entropy in <strong>nats</strong>.

We start with a fixed finite length and a finite vocabulary. We can then sum over all outputs and check the derivatives directly if the probabilities are positive and differentiable. Variable-length sequences with an end-of-sequence token (EOS) require additional conditions, discussed at the end of slide 37.

## Slide 37 — Computing sequence entropy one token at a time

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-08-entropy-estimation-and-gradients/ntu-self-improving-37-en.png"
  alt="Original slide 37 relating full sequence entropy to the expected sum of next-token entropies over the prefixes visited."
  caption="P037 — Sequence entropy and the sum of token entropies."
  source="https://speech.ee.ntu.edu.tw/~hylee/ml/ml2026-course-data/Self-Improving.pdf#page=37"
  credit="Hung-yi Lee / NTU ML 2026."
  width={1800} height={1013}
/>

<strong>In the video · [36:33–39:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s06mSAGN4gM&t=2193s).</strong> The lecturer contrasts a classifier's finite outputs with the large output space of text generation. He calculates the next-token distribution and sums the token entropies along a generated path. The relationship between the expectation of this sum and full entropy is our starting point.

### What exactly is infeasible to compute?

With vocabulary size V and length T, there are V to the power T possible sequences of fixed length. Entropy is well defined. What becomes impractical for a large model is enumerating all sequences exactly. In a small model, we can list every output and calculate entropy directly.

The entropy of the full output is

<Math tex={String.raw`L(\theta;x)=H_\theta(Y\mid x)
=-\sum_y p_\theta(y\mid x)\log p_\theta(y\mid x)
=\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[-\log p_\theta(Y\mid x)].`} display />

An autoregressive model assigns a probability equal to the product of token probabilities conditioned on the preceding tokens.

<Math tex={String.raw`p_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),\qquad
-\log p_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log p_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).`} display />

Turning this product into a sum does not imply that <strong>the tokens are independent</strong>. It follows from the chain rule for conditional probabilities. Different preceding tokens can give different distributions for later tokens.

### H on the slide describes a distribution before the token is selected

Given a prefix s, calculate entropy over the whole next-token distribution.

<Math tex={String.raw`h_\theta(s;x)=
-\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}p_\theta(v\mid x,s)\log p_\theta(v\mid x,s).`} display />

The slide's <Math tex={String.raw`H(\phi)`} /> corresponds to the initial empty prefix. The H terms below it use prefixes containing previously sampled tokens. Here we denote the empty prefix by <Math tex={String.raw`\varnothing`} />. Having already selected A does not set this entropy to zero. We are evaluating <strong>the distribution before choosing the next token in that context</strong>.

Define the sum of entropies over the prefixes visited on one path as follows. It corresponds to the slide's surrogate loss, with a subscript to show its dependence on the parameters.

<Math tex={String.raw`g_\theta(y;x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}h_\theta(y_{<t};x).`} display />

This value generally differs from the negative log probability of that path. Their expectations coincide when we sample paths from the model's own distribution.

<Math tex={String.raw`L(\theta;x)
=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb E_{Y_{<t}}\!\left[h_\theta(Y_{<t};x)\right]
=\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}\!\left[g_\theta(Y;x)\right].`} display />

This is <strong>unbiasedness of the value estimate</strong>. Averaging over many paths drawn from the same distribution estimates the full entropy. Neither the value on a single path nor its derivative must equal the full entropy or its gradient, respectively. [Paper §3.1, equations (3)–(7)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08186v1#page=2)

The Stanford notes decompose the joint entropy of two random variables into the entropy of one and the conditional entropy of the other. Conditional entropy <strong>also averages over the possible values of the conditioning variable</strong>. The notes use base-2 logarithms by default. The identity is the same with natural logarithms, but converting the numerical values requires a change of units. [Definitions and logarithm base](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee376a/files/2017-18/lecture_3.pdf#page=1) · [Chain rule](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee376a/files/2017-18/lecture_3.pdf#page=5)

### Worked example — A small model with four possible answers

Consider a model that generates two tokens. The first is A or B with probability 0.5 each. After A it generates 0 or 1 with probabilities 0.9 and 0.1. After B the two probabilities are 0.5 each. After two tokens it terminates with probability 1, so termination contributes no further entropy.

| Output path | Path probability | Sum g of entropies of the token distributions visited |
|---|---:|---:|
| A0 | 0.45 | 1.018230 nat |
| A1 | 0.05 | 1.018230 nat |
| B0 | 0.25 | 1.386294 nat |
| B1 | 0.25 | 1.386294 nat |

Writing h for binary entropy gives

<Math tex={String.raw`h(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p),\qquad
h(0.5)=0.693147,\quad h(0.9)=0.325083.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`g(A0)=g(A1)=h(0.5)+h(0.9)=1.018230,\qquad
g(B0)=g(B1)=2h(0.5)=1.386294.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathbb E[g(Y)]
=0.5(1.018230)+0.5(1.386294)
=1.202262\ \mathrm{nat}.`} display />

Computing the full entropy directly from the probabilities of the four paths gives the same result.

<Math tex={String.raw`H(0.45,0.05,0.25,0.25)=1.202262\ \mathrm{nat}
\approx1.734498\ \mathrm{bit}.`} display />

For the single path A0, g is 1.018230, while the full entropy is 1.202262. Greedy decoding always picks the most likely path A0 here and therefore cannot stand in for the expectation under the original distribution.

<strong>Conditions for variable length.</strong> The paper proves unbiasedness of the token estimator for finite sequences ending in EOS when the entropy is finite. An implementation should include EOS and exclude subsequent padding. Truncation at a maximum length or averaging by token count can change the objective. Also, <strong>the conditions for an unbiased value do not by themselves justify exchanging differentiation and an infinite sum.</strong> We first derive the gradient for a fixed, finite output space. [Theorem 1 and proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08186v1#page=3)

## Slide 38 — What does differentiating with a fixed sample leave out?

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-08-entropy-estimation-and-gradients/ntu-self-improving-38-en.png"
  alt="Original slide 38 sampling an answer and differentiating token entropy while holding its prefixes fixed."
  caption="P038 — Computing the token entropy gradient with a fixed sample."
  source="https://speech.ee.ntu.edu.tw/~hylee/ml/ml2026-course-data/Self-Improving.pdf#page=38"
  credit="Hung-yi Lee / NTU ML 2026."
  width={1800} height={1013}
/>

<strong>In the video · [39:55–41:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s06mSAGN4gM&t=2395s).</strong> The lecturer first samples a token sequence and feeds its prefixes back into the model to compute the token entropy gradients. A different path is sampled each time, so the average update is expressed as an expectation.

The bottom of the figure feeds previously sampled tokens in as input. Automatic differentiation treats these token IDs as fixed. It differentiates the logits, probabilities and entropy with respect to the parameters given those tokens as context.

The resulting gradient for one sample is

<Math tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta g_\theta(y;x)\qquad\text{with sampled }y\text{ held fixed}.`} display />

Let A be the average of this gradient over samples.

<Math tex={String.raw`A(\theta)=\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta g_\theta(Y;x)\right].`} display />

The gradient of the actual objective, however, is

<Math tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta L(\theta;x)
=\nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}\!\left[g_\theta(Y;x)\right].`} display />

The two expressions appear to differ only in the position of the gradient. The important difference is that <strong>the distribution defining the expectation also depends on the same parameters</strong>. A weight change alters both the entropy at a given prefix and the probability of reaching it.

The expectation contains the product of two parameter-dependent terms.

<Math tex={String.raw`L(\theta;x)=\sum_y
\underbrace{p_\theta(y\mid x)}_{\text{sampling probability}}
\underbrace{g_\theta(y;x)}_{\text{cost on this path}}.`} display />

More samples can reduce the sampling error in estimating A. Even a perfect estimate of A still omits part of the full gradient. <strong>Repeating the calculation cannot remove the bias from the missing term.</strong>

Discrete sampling does not preclude computing the gradient. Instead of differentiating the sampled ID directly, we can use <strong>the gradient of its selection probability</strong>. The log-derivative identity in the next section gives the connection.

## Slide 39 — The product rule gives two terms

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-08-entropy-estimation-and-gradients/ntu-self-improving-39-en.png"
  alt="Original slide 39 comparing the objective loss, the surrogate loss and the two gradient terms."

  source="https://speech.ee.ntu.edu.tw/~hylee/ml/ml2026-course-data/Self-Improving.pdf#page=39"
  credit="Hung-yi Lee / NTU ML 2026."
  width={1800} height={1013}
/>

<strong>In the video · [41:11–42:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s06mSAGN4gM&t=2471s).</strong> The lecturer emphasizes that although the computable surrogate loss equals the objective in expectation, moving the derivative inside the expectation can drop a term.

### Deriving the gradient line by line

In our finite output space, assume positive, differentiable probabilities and fix the input x. Start with the product rule.

<Math tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta L
&=\nabla_\theta\sum_y p_\theta(y\mid x)g_\theta(y;x)\\
&=\sum_y p_\theta(y\mid x)\nabla_\theta g_\theta(y;x)
+\sum_y g_\theta(y;x)\nabla_\theta p_\theta(y\mid x).
\end{aligned}`} display />

The first sum already weights each term by its probability and is therefore an expectation. To turn the second sum into an expectation under the same distribution, multiply and divide by the probability.

<Math tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta p_\theta(y\mid x)
=p_\theta(y\mid x)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y\mid x).`} display />

The exact decomposition is

<Math tex={String.raw`\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta L
&=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta g_\theta(Y;x)]}_{A:\ \text{direct entropy term}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}[g_\theta(Y;x)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Y\mid x)]}_{B:\ \text{sampling-distribution term}}.
\end{aligned}}`} display />

A directly changes the cost of a visited path. B changes how often different paths are selected. Interpreting cost as negative reward connects B to the policy gradient. The decomposition follows by applying the product rule to the equality of values. Equation (8f) of the paper likewise contains the gradient of the log probability. [Paper, PDF p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08186v1#page=3)

### A notation trap — ∇p in the sum, ∇log p in the expectation

The final term in the original image takes the following form.

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}
[g_\theta(Y;x)\nabla_\theta p_\theta(Y\mid x)]\qquad\text{(as printed on the slide)}.`} display />

<strong>This form is incorrect in general.</strong> The gradient of the probability belongs in the <strong>sum</strong> without probability weights. To express the same term as an <strong>expectation</strong> under p, use the gradient of the log probability.

<Math tex={String.raw`\sum_y g_\theta(y;x)\nabla_\theta p_\theta(y\mid x)
=\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}[g_\theta(Y;x)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Y\mid x)].`} display />

The expectations on slides 39 and 40 omit `log`. Use the log-probability gradient as on the right side of the identity above.

  alt="Berkeley lecture slide explaining how the derivative of a probability equals the probability times the derivative of its logarithm."

/>

The equation in the blue box is the log-derivative trick we just used. Berkeley maximizes reward, whereas here we minimize an entropy cost. <strong>Mixing the signs for reward and cost reverses the update direction.</strong> Also, unlike the reward in the diagram, g here depends directly on the parameters. We must retain A as well as B. [Original PDF, p. 5](https://rail.eecs.berkeley.edu/deeprlcourse/static/slides/lec-5.pdf#page=5)

### A probability tree with a correct value estimate and a zero gradient

Return to the two-token model on slide 37. This time only q, the probability of A as the first token, can change. Keep 0.9 after A and 0.5 after B fixed. Let q be the sigmoid of a logit z.

<Math tex={String.raw`q=\sigma(z),\qquad
L(z)=h(q)+q\,h(0.9)+(1-q)h(0.5).`} display />

The exact derivative is

<Math tex={String.raw`\frac{dL}{dz}
=q(1-q)\left[\log\frac{1-q}{q}+h(0.9)-h(0.5)\right].`} display />

At q = 0.5 the entropy gradient of the first token itself is zero. But the distribution after A has lower entropy than the one after B. This difference contributes to the full gradient.

<Math tex={String.raw`A_z=0,\qquad
B_z=0.25\,[0.325083-0.693147]
\approx-0.092016.`} display />

| Calculation | Gradient with respect to z | Effect of gradient descent |
|---|---:|---|
| Differentiate full entropy over all four paths | −0.092016 | Increases z, making A more likely |
| Use only the fixed-sample token entropy gradient A | 0 | Cannot change the first branch probability |
| Include both A and the correct B | −0.092016 | Matches the derivative of full entropy |
| Use the slide's expectation term without the logarithm | +0.017725 | Moves the first branch in the opposite direction in this example |

These are exact calculations over all four paths. The positive sign in the last row is not a rounding artifact. Using the probability gradient inside an expectation already weighted by p changes the weights. A finite-difference check of the full objective also gives −0.092016.

<strong>Limit of this interpretation.</strong> The model does not specify whether answers on path A are factually correct. This calculation determines the entropy gradient's direction. Improving accuracy is a separate question. As Part 6 noted, a model can become more confident in an incorrect answer.

The red arrows mark the path used for the calculation. They do not identify a correct answer. A gives a gradient that sharpens distributions at fixed prefixes. On its own it does not account for the selection probabilities of all paths.

The slide speaks of the gradient as an update direction. When minimizing, parameters actually move in the <strong>negative gradient direction</strong>.

<Math tex={String.raw`\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\eta\,\widehat{\nabla_\theta L},\qquad \eta>0.`} display />

### How does token entropy change the logits?

For a given context, write z for the logit vector and p for the softmax probabilities. The derivative of token entropy with respect to each logit is

<Math tex={String.raw`\frac{\partial H(p)}{\partial z_j}
=-p_j\,[\log p_j+H(p)].`} display />

Because probabilities are normalized, each logit affects the other probabilities too. The gradient is zero at a uniform distribution. Reducing entropy therefore need not strengthen the best answer. This also explains why A cannot change the first branch in our probability tree when the initial distribution is exactly 0.5 versus 0.5.

### Further reading D — Building a surrogate loss for automatic differentiation

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-08-entropy-estimation-and-gradients/berkeley-policy-gradients-p20.png"
  alt="Original Berkeley slide constructing a surrogate loss so automatic differentiation gives the desired policy gradient."
  caption="UC Berkeley CS 185/285 — A surrogate loss for computing policy gradients with automatic differentiation (PDF p. 20)."
  source="https://rail.eecs.berkeley.edu/deeprlcourse/static/slides/lec-5.pdf#page=20"
  credit="Sergey Levine / UC Berkeley CS 185/285, Policy Gradients."
  width={1800} height={1013}
/>

The slide builds the computation graph to produce the gradient we want. The surrogate loss passed to backpropagation can have a different numerical value from the original evaluation metric. Its purpose is <strong>to give the required gradient estimate when differentiated</strong>. [Berkeley PDF, p. 20](https://rail.eecs.berkeley.edu/deeprlcourse/static/slides/lec-5.pdf#page=20)

Hold the sample y fixed during differentiation. Write sg for stop-gradient, which preserves a value but blocks differentiation along that route. The implementation loss corresponding to equation (9) is

<Math tex={String.raw`\ell_{\mathrm{sur}}(\theta;y)
=g_\theta(y;x)+\operatorname{sg}\!\left(g_\theta(y;x)-b\right)\log p_\theta(y\mid x).`} display />

Here b is a fixed baseline independent of the sampled answer. Start with zero. Differentiating one sample gives

<Math tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta\ell_{\mathrm{sur}}
=\nabla_\theta g_\theta(y;x)
+(g_\theta(y;x)-b)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y\mid x).`} display />

The baseline leaves the expected gradient unchanged because the probabilities sum to one.

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathbb E_{Y\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(Y\mid x)]
=\sum_y\nabla_\theta p_\theta(y\mid x)=\nabla_\theta 1=0.`} display />

Substituting a batch mean that includes the current sample, however, can make the baseline depend on that sample. Claiming unbiasedness then requires a separate design or correction. The code below uses a fixed scalar baseline.

### The essential PyTorch calculation

The excerpt below is the core of a function that validates input shapes, data types and masks. `logits` contains logits recomputed at each sampled prefix, `sampled_tokens` contains the sampled token IDs, and `valid_mask` is true through EOS and false for padding.

```python
# logits: [batch, time, vocabulary]
# sampled_tokens: [batch, time], dtype=torch.long
# valid_mask: [batch, time], dtype=torch.bool
logp = torch.nn.functional.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)
token_entropy = -(logp.exp() * logp).sum(dim=-1)
token_logp = logp.gather(-1, sampled_tokens.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)

entropy_sum = (token_entropy * valid_mask).sum(dim=1)
sequence_logp = (token_logp * valid_mask).sum(dim=1)
loss = (entropy_sum + (entropy_sum.detach() - baseline) * sequence_logp).mean()
```

`detach()` makes the cost in the second term act only as a weight. Omitting detach there introduces an extra derivative of the cost multiplied by the log probability. Detaching the first `entropy_sum` as well would remove A. The signs here assume <strong>cost minimization</strong>. [Equation (9)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08186v1#page=3) · [PyTorch detach documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.Tensor.detach.html) · [log_softmax documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.functional.log_softmax.html)

If the function returns one loss per sequence, the caller should take the mean before backpropagating.

<strong>Implementation conditions.</strong> The policy that generates samples must match the distribution used in the loss. Arbitrary top-k or top-p filters, different temperatures, or samples from a stale model can invalidate the unbiasedness claim. Keep the model fixed between sampling and recomputation and handle stochastic behavior such as dropout consistently. `model.eval()` disables dropout. `no_grad()` separately disables gradient tracking.

Keep input prompt tokens separate from generated answer tokens and sum only over the generated portion, including EOS. Averaging by length can change the objective. Low-precision probabilities can be numerically unstable, so the full function performs the calculation in float32 for half and bfloat16 logits. These checks clarify the conditions of the calculation. Accuracy and stable adaptation still require evaluation.

## What these five slides establish

<strong>Under appropriate sampling conditions, the sum of token entropies can be an unbiased estimate of sequence entropy. Differentiating only that sum with the sample held fixed can omit changes in the distribution that selects the path.</strong>

Both terms on slide 39 follow from one objective. Differentiating the expectation accounts for changes to the cost and to the probabilities. Even the missing logarithm on the original slide can reverse the gradient direction, as the worked example shows. Checking the equations, the implementation and a small numerical example together catches this error.
