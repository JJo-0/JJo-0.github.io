> **BACKLOG_CANDIDATE** · Reviewed October 15, 2026 (KST) · Rank 3 · **79/100** · Evidence category **INDUSTRY_TRIGGER**. This review covers the DOE announcement and order plus selected PJM records of October 14 operations. Operating hours for designated resources, dispatch of data center backup generation, electricity produced, and outages avoided are separate quantities.

## Main finding

The US Department of Energy's October 13–16 emergency order gave PJM a last-resort option to call on backup generation during a period of large loads. The order itself documents authorization. Evidence of a shortage or of an outcome requires operating records.

The dates covered by the announcement have passed. An extension or subsequent order would require its own verification before treating the same authority as current.

The full order distinguishes its operating period from reporting and compliance duties, some of which continue afterward. It expressly excludes backup generation under the order from treatment as a capacity resource. Temporary dispatch authority therefore does not establish dependable year-round capacity.

## Why it matters

Autumn maintenance can take generators and transmission lines out of service at the same time. Rising demand can then narrow the reserve margin. The order supports short-term reliability by permitting dispatch of designated generators and the use of backup generation immediately before or during an EEA3 emergency.

## What is different about the order?

This is a four-day operational measure. It allows on-site backup generation to be called as a grid resource after existing demand response and reserves, on a timescale very different from transmission investment or market redesign.

## Where does the backup power go?

The order includes large customers supplying themselves on site. That reduces the load seen by the network. A generator exporting electricity to the network has a different effect. Running an on-site generator does not imply an equal injection into the grid. The order covers both grid-synchronized and non-synchronized resources.

Assessing the contribution requires the change in net grid demand, on-site generation, operating hours, and constraints at the relevant location. Adding nameplate capacity does not identify when or where a bottleneck eased.

Section D excludes resources serving critical reliability and backup needs, including hospitals, emergency response, and water treatment. It does not make all standby power available for grid support. The section defines the scope of the order and cannot determine whether an individual facility's operation complies with it.

## Forecasts, alerts, orders, and outcomes

Demand and maintenance estimates on pp. 1–2 are forecasts available when PJM applied. A narrow projected reserve margin does not measure an actual shortage or an outage.

An Energy Emergency Alert (EEA) signals grid stress. The criteria quoted on pp. 4–5 allow the appropriate level to be declared as needed, without necessarily progressing through every lower level. Issuing an order, declaring EEA3, and dispatching backup generation each require a separate record.

**Reader check.** Does issuing the order establish that EEA3 or an outage occurred? It grants conditional operating authority. The occurrence of an alert, dispatch, or interruption is a separate operational question.

## What later operating records confirm

[PJM's October 15 daily compliance report](https://www.pjm.com/-/media/DotCom/documents/other-fed-state/20261015-pjm-daily-compliance-report-doe-order-no-202-26-50.pdf) identifies units that operated under the order's allowances on October 14: CORDOVA 3 CC for 24 hours, ELGIN CT 1–4 for eight hours each, and BC WAGNER CT for five hours. Generators supplied these data to PJM and the report allows for corrections. There is direct evidence of operation for these units.

The column records hours, not MW of output or MWh of energy. A 24-hour entry does not establish full-rated output throughout the day or quantify avoided outages. Nor is this a list of data centers that switched to on-site backup.

[PJM's October 14 operational update](https://insidelines.pjm.com/operations-update-extends-maximum-generation-alert-calls-on-demand-response/) reports pre-emergency load management on October 13 and 14 and says that large-load backup generation had not been needed by the time of the update. The statement covers neither the entire period through the 16th nor later conditions. Adjusting demand, operating a designated generator, and switching a data center to backup power are three distinct events.

## Order dates and the nationwide estimate

- Effective dates: October 13–16, 2026.
- DOE's estimate of unused backup generation nationwide: more than 35 GW.
- The 35 GW is not immediately available PJM capacity. It does not account for fuel, emissions permits, grid location, synchronization, or communications constraints.

## Limits and contrary evidence

Selected operating hours have been verified. This review did not cover the complete EEA3 and backup dispatch records, generated MWh, avoided outages, costs, or emissions for the order period. An emergency order and a daily report cannot provide a full retrospective assessment of risk, causes, and results. Local distribution constraints and environmental limits restrict how much distributed backup generation can be used together.

## Code, data, and interests

The announcement reviewed here supplies no code or analytical dataset. DOE issued the order and announced the policy. PJM applied on October 11, 2026. Evaluating performance requires subsequent PJM data and independent regulatory review.

## Checking the interpretation

Does a nationwide estimate above 35 GW mean 35 GW was delivered to PJM? A national resource estimate and a regional dispatch result answer different questions. To establish generation, look for unit-level dispatch and operating records, including energy in MWh and the time interval, alongside capacity in GW.
