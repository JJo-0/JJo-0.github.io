> <strong>Study stage: nonrandomized, single-arm phase 1/2 clinical trial in humans.</strong> This article explains the clinical evidence. Individual treatment decisions are outside its scope.

## A historical benchmark answers a different question from a concurrent control

CHORUS tested adding canakinumab to the treatment pathway for unresectable stage III non-small cell lung cancer. The denominators differ: 41 participants enrolled, 37 received treatment, and 32 were included in the efficacy analysis. Estimated progression-free survival (PFS) at 24 months was 67% in the efficacy population, above a prespecified historical benchmark. <strong>The study made no randomized comparison with a concurrent treatment group.</strong> [Original paper][primary]

## Why add a drug to an existing treatment pathway?

When a new drug is added to a sequence such as chemoradiotherapy followed by immunotherapy, both the potential for better outcomes and added toxicity matter. The biological rationale for modulating an inflammatory pathway must be tested against actual patient outcomes and safety.

An early trial can assess feasibility and look for signs of activity. Without a group receiving the existing treatment alone under the same conditions, however, the effects of patient selection, care setting, and follow-up are hard to separate from a treatment effect.

## Why were 41 people enrolled but only 32 analyzed for efficacy?

Some enrolled participants may never receive treatment or may not meet eligibility requirements for a particular analysis. Record the denominators for efficacy and safety separately. Using the same convenient number for every percentage can misrepresent the trial.

Censoring makes the survival calculation more complex. The 67% estimate cannot be converted back into a count by assuming that all 32 people were followed for 24 months. The estimate uses the risk set and events observed over time.

## What does it mean to exceed a prespecified benchmark?

Let p₀ be the historical reference and p the estimated proportion at 24 months. A prespecified analysis can assess whether p clears that reference. A concurrent randomized comparison asks a different question.

The paper used a one-sided 90% lower confidence bound for its primary decision and reported intervals at other confidence levels in descriptive analyses. A one-sided 90% bound and a two-sided 95% interval are not interchangeable decision rules. [Prespecified criterion and results][primary]

## Response, progression-free survival, and overall survival

ORR is the proportion meeting defined tumor response criteria. PFS measures time without a specified event such as progression or death. A high response rate alone does not show how long responses lasted. Overall survival measures another outcome.

When a graph overlays a historical curve for standard therapy, identify the patient populations and time periods. A line sitting higher on the plot cannot by itself establish a comparative treatment effect. Long-term estimates can become less certain as the numbers at risk fall.

## Reading the safety results

The paper reports safety outcomes including pneumonia- and pneumonitis-related events. When radiation, immunotherapy, and an additional drug are given, check attribution, grade, and treatment discontinuation. Also check whether the safety denominator differs from the one used for efficacy.

The paper discloses drug and research support relationships. Independent follow-up and controlled trials therefore carry particular weight. A plausible mechanism cannot determine the balance of harm and patient benefit without sufficient data.

## What should the next report show?

Look for randomized comparisons, longer follow-up, standard treatment delivered under comparable conditions, and markers that can guide patient selection prospectively. <strong>The trial identified an early treatment signal. Exceeding the historical benchmark does not yet establish a replacement for the current standard of care.</strong>
