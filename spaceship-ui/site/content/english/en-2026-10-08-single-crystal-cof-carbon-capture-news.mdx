## What the study found

The team made gas-separation membranes by mixing single crystals of a covalent organic framework (COF) into polymers. The highest filler fraction, <strong>75.5 vol%</strong>, came from a 6FDA-DAM sample. The headline combination in the abstract, **about 74,800 Barrer** permeability and **20.8** CO₂/N₂ selectivity, came from a PIM-1 sample with **59.7 vol%** filler. These are different specimens.

## How the design differs

Carbon-capture membranes generally face a trade-off between permeability and selectivity. Increasing the free volume to let gas pass faster can make it harder to distinguish CO₂ from N₂.

Mixed-matrix membranes incorporate a porous filler into a polymer to improve these properties. At high loading, however, particles can aggregate and voids can form at the polymer–filler interface, reducing the actual selectivity.

The team used high loadings of **single-crystal COFs with controlled shape and interfaces**. The aim was to increase the number of gas transport channels while suppressing nonselective defects that can accompany ordinary powder fillers.

## Why stronger adhesion is only part of the story

A COF connects organic building blocks through covalent bonds, forming regular open channels. In a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM), these particles are dispersed in a continuous polymer phase. A loading of 75.5 vol% is a fraction of the material volume, distinct from a weight fraction or a carbon-capture percentage.

The abstract emphasizes weak-interaction inlaying. The authors adjusted the balance between polymer–particle and particle–particle interaction energies to maintain dispersion and compatible interfaces even at high loading. They compare surface functional groups using control samples and molecular dynamics. OH groups strengthened both adhesion to the polymer and attraction between particles, leading to aggregation and settling. For the CH₃, H, and F variants, polymer–particle interactions favored dispersion relative to particle–particle attraction. The relative strengths of the two interactions matter more than maximizing or minimizing either one in isolation.

Gas separation also involves two distinct questions. Permeability measures how readily a material transports a gas. Selectivity measures its preference for one gas over another. Relating either number to whole-membrane throughput or a power plant’s capture fraction also requires membrane thickness and area, feed composition, and pressures. The abstract alone cannot specify system performance.

### Check your understanding

Does 75.5 vol% mean 75.5% of the CO₂ was removed? The denominator here is the volume of membrane constituents. A capture fraction uses the amount of CO₂ supplied as its denominator.

## Comparing specimens under matched conditions

Figure 4d compares membranes using 6FDA-DAM at 25°C and 2 bar with a 15/85 CO₂/N₂ mixture. The neat polymer gave about 969 Barrer and a selectivity of 25.9. At 60.9 vol% COF-CH₃, the mixed membrane gave about 22,900 Barrer and 20.6. Permeability rose substantially while selectivity fell.

At 75.5 vol% filler, the COF-CH₃/6FDA-DAM sample reached about 40,000 Barrer and 14.4. With PIM-1 and 59.7 vol% filler, the corresponding pair was about 74,800 Barrer and 20.8. Keep polymer identity and loading attached to each value. Supplementary Table 5 reports 87,022.0 ± 1,458.7 Barrer and 17.9 ± 1.2 selectivity for COF-H/PIM-1. Thus 74,800 Barrer is the value paired with 20.8 selectivity for COF-CH₃/PIM-1, rather than the largest permeability in the entire study. Ranking membranes on permeability alone would miss that distinction.

## Scope of the reported results

- COF filler volume fraction: **up to 75.5 vol%**
- CO₂ permeability: **74,800 Barrer**
- CO₂/N₂ selectivity: **approximately 20**
- all-organic mixed-matrix architecture
- A polyimide-based membrane retained **about 85% of its initial permeability** after six months of room-temperature aging.

This list brings together results from multiple specimens. Maximum loading, the representative permeability–selectivity pair, and long-term stability should not be assigned to a single specimen.

Supplementary Table 3 gives the storage conditions and reference values. After room-temperature vacuum storage, permeability for 60.9 vol% COF-CH₃/6FDA-DAM changed from 22,864.3 ± 1,180.2 Barrer on day 1 to 19,263.4 ± 406.1 Barrer on day 180. The ratio of the means is about 84.3%. Each time point reports a mean and standard deviation from three independently measured membranes. The experiment did not continuously operate one membrane for 180 days. Selectivity also changed, from 20.6 ± 1.3 to 23.6 ± 0.4. Retained permeability alone does not describe the whole change. [Supplementary Table 3, PDF p. 75, printed p. 74.]

## Interpreting the numbers

The Barrer is a unit of gas permeability for a membrane material. Plant throughput also depends on membrane thickness, effective area, pressure difference, module packing, feed composition, and compression energy.

Consequently, 74,800 Barrer cannot be converted on its own into a percentage of a power plant’s CO₂ removed or a cost of energy.

Supplementary Figures 40–44 include pure- and mixed-gas comparisons, concentration and pressure changes, and humidity and NOx/SOx tests. The study did examine contaminants and humidity. Its tests do not cover every constituent or long-term operating condition in actual flue gas.

## The main limitations

Supplementary Notes 7 and 9 simulate a two-stage separation process and estimate costs. These are process-model outputs. Table 3 likewise measures membranes after 180 days of room-temperature vacuum storage. Neither result represents continuous flue-gas plant operation over that period.

High-loading COF membranes also depend on material cost and scalable crystal synthesis. High-yield production of single-crystal filler and uniform dispersion in a polymer matrix must be reproduced at industrial scale.

The authors declared no competing interests.

## Why follow this approach?

A longstanding challenge in carbon capture is **achieving high filler loading in a working membrane while suppressing interfacial defects**. If the structure survives module-scale fabrication and real-gas operation, it could expand the available designs for post-combustion carbon capture.

## What do the equations in the Methods measure?

The Methods contain five numbered equations. Equation (1) is a semiempirical estimate of mean void radius from positron annihilation lifetime, distinct from a microscopic measurement of a pore. Equation (2) calculates permeability from measured pressure rise, specimen thickness and area, temperature, and composition. Equation (3) gives a separation factor by dividing the ratio of two gas fractions on the permeate side by their ratio in the feed. Equation (4) gives solubility from uptake divided by pressure. Equation (5) divides permeability by solubility to obtain diffusivity.

Together they distinguish transport driven by greater solubility from transport driven by faster diffusion. In a numerical use of Equation (5), account for the 10⁻¹⁰ conversion implicit in a value stated in Barrer. Dividing raw numbers without this conversion gives the wrong scale and units. A complete symbol-and-unit table and a visual cross-check of the printed equations remained open in the original review.

There is also an unresolved link between Equation (2) and the apparatus description. The Methods describe a constant feed, argon sweeping away permeate, and steady-state mass spectrometry. The printed equation contains a downstream volume and a pressure rise over time. Their relationship in this apparatus needs clarification. In Equation (3), the printed denominator is the feed composition ratio, but the adjacent description repeats the permeate symbol. The review leaves the source equation and wording intact.

## Reading the supplementary equations

Equation (2) of Note 6 converts each mass to a volume using density, then calculates the filler volume fraction. Weight fraction and volume fraction therefore need not match. The equation and density notation on printed page 67 were checked visually, but the conversion from 5 wt% to the reported 11.8 vol% there remained unresolved. Without confirming the mass basis and specimen density, retain the reported value as such.

Distinguish Maxwell model predictions from measurements. The 100 vol% COF row of Table 6 is a prediction, not a measured pure COF membrane. In Table 3 the 180-day value is a mean and standard deviation from three independent membranes after vacuum storage at room temperature. It describes aging in storage.

### The lifetime assumed in the cost model

Note 9 compares the membrane with adsorption using the porous material CALF-20. Adsorption captures molecules on surfaces or in pores and later releases them. Membrane separation uses a pressure difference and differences in transport rates. The CALF-20 side cites pilot data from another source. The membrane figures of 90.0% CO₂ recovery, 91.3% purity, 1.52 GJ/tCO₂, and $38/tCO₂ come from the two-stage simulation. It uses room-temperature measurements for a 15/85 CO₂/N₂ mixture. Figure 57 specifies a dry feed of 1,000 kmol/h. The cited CALF-20 pilot received 19–20% CO₂. Feed composition, product purity, and type of evidence differ, so the comparison cannot establish cheaper capture at matched purity in an actual plant. This review did not independently verify the pilot raw data. [Supplementary Note 9, PDF pp. 70–71.]

Scale-up poses another question. Note 8 reports an 81.5 wt% yield over three days for this single-crystal COF, while identifying productivity per reactor volume and time as a challenge for large-scale capture. Yield measures the fraction recovered from a starting amount, distinct from a factory’s daily output. The tonne-scale production, $50/kg, and five-minute synthesis mentioned in the same note describe advances in other COF studies. Those conditions were not demonstrated for this COF-CH₃ membrane. [Supplementary Note 8, PDF p. 70.]

The process and economic analysis in Note 7 assumes a 20-year plant life and a five-year membrane life. Spreading membrane costs across a given number of years affects annual costs. Five years is a modeling assumption. The 180-day storage experiment does not verify that service life.

Equations (3)–(8) in Note 7 discretize the module, enforce overall and component balances, and compute permeation. Equation (9) allocates membrane-related cost annually. The printed expression adds a Membrane/5 term to labor, maintenance, and utilities. The 5 is consistent with the assumed membrane life, but that page does not fully define the scope of the Membrane cost term or the numerical units of every input. Replacing 5 with the 20-year plant life, or claiming to reproduce actual capture cost from this expression alone, would be unjustified. Material permeability and commercial cost answer different questions.

**Check your understanding.** What if actual membranes require replacement sooner than five years? The annual replacement burden must be recalculated. The increase cannot be determined without the remaining cost and operating assumptions.

### Is J in the process model a rate per unit area?

Note 7 divides a module into 100 computational segments, each with one hundredth of the total area. This is a discretization of flow, not an experiment on 100 physical membranes. Equation (4) divides incoming flow into retentate and permeate. Equation (5) applies the same conservation relationship to each component. The compositions in Equations (6) and (7) each sum to one, accounting for all components in each stream.

Read J in Equation (8) carefully. The left side is incoming component flow minus retained component flow. The right side includes the segment area A_u. J therefore corresponds to the component flow through that segment, rather than the usual **permeation flux per unit area**. If F is molar flow, J must also be molar flow, while Z is a dimensionless mole fraction. The page does not state all numerical units for F, pressure, and L. The review does not supply missing values or assume a value for L.

The bracket on the right takes the difference between total pressure times component mole fraction on the two sides. The driving force is the **component partial-pressure difference**. This explains how the model connects permeation to the material balance. Long-term performance of a flue-gas plant still requires operating evidence. Superscript n denotes the segment number, rather than exponentiation. This reading was checked against the text and page image of Equations (3)–(8) on PDF page 69, printed page 68.
