Will supporting a plant that can produce hydrogen cheaply also create demand? Prospective users look beyond any one production plant when deciding whether to switch fuels. They consider local supply chains, transport and storage, whether neighboring firms are switching and whether they can rely on a continuing fuel supply. This study brings one of those relationships into policy design: <strong>a subsidized switch by one offtaker can influence decisions at nearby facilities.</strong><a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<small>Rumpelnik et al., published September 17, 2026 in Nature Energy. The study combines empirical analysis of historical natural gas power plant adoption with a network of potential hydrogen users and forward simulations. “Up to 41%” and “13–15%” compare modeled policies through 2100. They are not measured savings from policies in operation today.</small>

## 1. The cheapest project and the project that brings more users on board

Imagine two industrial facilities that could use the same amount of hydrogen. One can switch at slightly lower cost but has few potential users nearby. The other costs a little more to convert but connects several plants and transport hubs. The first may need a smaller subsidy today. Supporting the second might make it easier for others to switch later. These hypothetical facilities illustrate the choice the study examines.

Minimizing the cost of supporting one project and encouraging subsequent adoption involve different criteria. As neighbors start using hydrogen, they may develop shared infrastructure, supply chains, experience and information. A **spatial spillover** occurs when one user’s decision affects nearby users. The researchers model how such spillovers could influence the spread of hydrogen demand in Europe.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

That mechanism does not imply that the most central plant always merits support. If hydrogen is far from cost-competitive, the subsidy may be too expensive, and potential connections may never become actual supply chains. The question is <strong>how a policy should weight cost and connectivity when scoring projects.</strong>

The paper does not report a 41% improvement in electrolyzer efficiency or an immediate 41% fall in hydrogen production cost. It calculates how choosing different early recipients changes the ratio of policy spending to additional demand over the long run. Device performance and policy cost-effectiveness have different units.

## 2. The three levels in Figure 1 use different evidence

Read Figure 1 from the top. The first stage in blue models historical adoption empirically as a function of cost, spatial spillovers and distance to infrastructure. The second, in red, represents prospective European hydrogen users as a network and calculates centrality, a measure of their positions of influence. The third, in green, simulates future cost and adoption and compares policies for allocating support.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The multiplication sign in the first row denotes an interaction between cost and spatial spillovers. The adjacent plus sign denotes a separate term, such as distance to infrastructure. The diagram shows <strong>which relationships enter the model</strong>. Multiplying two prices would not give actual hydrogen demand.

The data also differ by stage. The empirical analysis uses historical adoption of natural gas power plants. The network analysis and simulation of future hydrogen adoption use a separately constructed set of <strong>14,108 potential offtakers</strong>. These are facilities that might switch to hydrogen or hydrogen-derived fuels. The count is not a tally of current green-hydrogen customers.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

That distinction identifies the basis for the long-run projection. The model assumes that relationships observed during the spread of natural gas carry over to some degree to hydrogen. Historical observations help evaluate the model. They cannot directly show how hydrogen adoption will develop.

## 3. Why look at the spread of natural gas?

Before widespread hydrogen use, there is little long-run evidence on hydrogen adoption. The authors chose natural gas power plants as a historical analogue because cost competitiveness, energy transport, storage and supply chains mattered in their expansion. The point of comparison is how one facility’s conversion affects nearby infrastructure and other firms’ decisions. The two fuels belong to different industrial contexts.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Cost competitiveness and nearby adoption were both positively associated with adoption, and the two effects appeared to reinforce each other. The authors report that the influence of nearby adoption can become larger when cost competitiveness is very poor. A large multiplier on a marginal effect describes how the estimated adoption relationship changes under specified cost and neighborhood conditions. It is not a multiplier on observed hydrogen demand.

The mechanism is familiar. A firm contemplating a switch may wait for supply and infrastructure, while suppliers wait for sufficient demand. Early support could reduce uncertainty for neighbors facing this coordination problem. Nearby firms do not necessarily share a supply chain, however. Industrial processes, required hydrogen quality, transport constraints and contract terms affect actual connections.

The model therefore constructs a spatial network from observations and assumptions. It uses estimated associations to compare policy scenarios without claiming separate causal identification of every pathway. It is a policy simulation, distinct from a randomized trial of real-world implementation.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 4. Having many neighbors and connecting different regions

The study uses two centrality measures. **Degree centrality** summarizes a facility’s direct connections to other facilities. A dense cluster might develop into a hydrogen valley. **Betweenness centrality** measures how often a facility lies on paths between other nodes. It can identify links in a potential hydrogen corridor between regions.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Consider a chain of three facilities, A–B–C, with no direct link between A and C. B has two neighbors, while A and C have one each. The only path from A to C also passes through B. B scores highly on both measures in this example. In larger networks, a local hub with many neighbors can be distinct from a site connecting regions.

Subsidizing B does not guarantee two more adopters. Centrality signals potential spillovers. Actual adoption also depends on cost, feasibility and infrastructure. The simulation separately tests whether prioritizing well-connected facilities produces the largest increase in hydrogen demand.

High centrality was geographically concentrated. Half the offtakers in the top 10% by degree centrality were in five regions of Belgium and Germany. A spatial policy designed for efficiency may therefore concentrate support in existing industrial clusters. Regional fairness and equity in industrial transition are policy objectives that require separate evaluation.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 6. Which denominator gives the 41% improvement?

Total subsidies can rise even as their effect per euro improves. The researchers divide cumulative policy spending by cumulative additional hydrogen demand attributable to the policy. Using all hydrogen demand as the denominator would give the policy credit for adoption that would have occurred anyway. Defining additional demand therefore requires a baseline.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<Math tex={String.raw`C_{\mathrm{policy}}`} /> is cumulative policy cost over the analysis period, <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta H`} /> is the cumulative hydrogen demand added by the policy, and <Math tex="K" /> is the cost of inducing one more kilogram of demand. With costs in euros and demand in kilograms, the resulting unit is €/kg. This interpretation requires a positive denominator. Both policies must use the same discounting and accounting conventions.

For illustration, suppose policy 0 spends €100 million to create 50 million kg of additional demand, giving <Math tex={String.raw`K_0=100/50=2.00\,\mathrm{EUR/kg}`} />. Suppose policy 1 spends €118 million to create 100 million additional kg, giving <Math tex={String.raw`K_1=118/100=1.18\,\mathrm{EUR/kg}`} />. The factors of one million cancel in each division.

The improvement in cost-effectiveness is <Math tex={String.raw`(2.00-1.18)/2.00\times100\%=41\%`} />. Subtracting new unit cost from baseline unit cost gives the absolute saving. Dividing it by baseline unit cost gives the relative reduction. Total spending rose 18%, yet cost per additional kilogram of demand fell 41%. This hypothetical example illustrates the denominator. It does not reconstruct the study’s underlying budgets or demand.

For the same reason, the claim that “hydrogen prices fell 41%” would be incorrect. A price belongs to a transaction. This metric measures policy spending per unit of additional demand attributable to the policy. Production cost, market price, subsidy expenditure and additional demand may be related, but each measures something different. Identifying the numerator and denominator makes it possible to tell a policy outcome from a change in device performance.

The temporary decline in cost-effectiveness early in the simulation also matters. Changing which facilities receive support can increase costs before follow-on conversions appear. Long-run cumulative gains leave the initial fiscal burden intact. This helps explain the authors’ emphasis on sustained policy commitment. Whether a budget authority accepts the long-term assumptions is another question.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

## 7. Where is the simulation most vulnerable?

The central assumption is that the empirical relationship for natural gas diffusion offers a useful analogue for hydrogen. Both fuels depend on infrastructure and clustered demand, but their switching costs, electricity prices, competing technologies and transport characteristics differ. As hydrogen adoption data accumulate, the model can be re-estimated and the assumption tested. Its 2100 result is conditional on that assumption.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The authors identify future hydrogen cost competitiveness and the range of end uses as major uncertainties. A market limited to hard-to-convert sectors such as chemicals and steel would differ in scale and trajectory from one that also served transport and power. A favorable combination of low costs and broad end uses cannot stand for all scenarios.

Spatial indicators also have limits. Geographic proximity may miss constraints imposed by pipelines, contracts and transport networks. A plant closure or revised investment plan can change potential demand and centrality. Linking actual supply chains with company decisions would help test whether high centrality predicts strong policy effects.

Sensitivity to auction weights illustrates the trade-off. The paper reports that, in the EHB-style setting, raising the weight on centrality above 20% can sacrifice more in cost competitiveness than it gains from spillovers. The result at 10% therefore gives no basis for assuming that 50% or 100% would be better. Connectivity supplements the cost criterion.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 8. What would an actual policy need to establish?

The practical proposal is to extend existing cost evaluation with information about location and connections among offtakers. Alongside a project’s unit cost, an application might document prospective connected users, shared infrastructure and conversion schedules, and plausible routes to later adoption. This is a policy implication from the model. It does not establish that existing auctions have already changed.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-4" data-news-citation="4" aria-label="Go to reference 4" data-astro-reload>[4]</a>

Implementation would begin with an updated list of offtakers. Potential sites and binding purchase agreements must be distinguished, and the feasibility of building infrastructure and timing constraints on electricity and hydrogen supply need checking. A later evaluation would ask whether scoring centrality actually produced additional adopters or subsidized firms that would have switched anyway. This is how the simulation’s additional-demand denominator could be tested in practice.

Initial spending and long-run outcomes also need to be reported together. Presenting the cumulative advantage through 2100 as a saving in today’s budget removes the time dimension that defines the comparison. Separate cost and demand trajectories for 2030, 2040, 2050 and beyond would show when benefits arise under which assumptions. Such long projections are most informative as comparisons between policies.

Regional equity also deserves its own evaluation. Concentrating support in established industrial clusters with high centrality may reduce opportunities in areas with few existing connections. Maximizing efficiency and promoting balanced development are different objectives. A policy scoring well on one measure need not best serve every social goal.

The question thus extends beyond which facility can produce hydrogen at the lowest cost to <strong>which early conversions make others possible</strong>. The authors examine it with a network of 14,108 prospective offtakers and long-run simulations. An enduring contribution beyond the 41% figure is the inclusion of costs and spatial spillovers in the same policy comparison, with its denominator and time horizon stated explicitly. The authors declared no competing interests.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>
