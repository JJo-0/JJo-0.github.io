## 1. In one sentence

The multi-immersion oblique plane microscope (miOPM) accommodates organelles, cleared tissue and 384-well plates on one instrument. The operator exchanges oil, silicone oil, water or air objectives of equal focal length and adjusts a zoom relay without rebuilding the entire light-sheet microscope for each sample.

### What does it mean to use one instrument for different samples?

Fine intracellular structures demand resolution and enough collected fluorescence to distinguish nearby features. Scanning a large region of thick tissue puts a premium on clearance between objective and specimen, field of view and accessible depth. No objective maximizes every quantity at once. miOPM addresses the burden of rebuilding downstream optics when the sample calls for a different trade-off.

Three questions clarify the design claim. Why can changing objectives blur the image? Which mapping does adjusting magnification restore? And why is accessible depth finite even after the match? Each question separates an instrument capability from a remaining limit.

## 2. Why this is difficult

Light-sheet fluorescence microscopy illuminates a thin plane for fast three-dimensional imaging with low phototoxicity. High-resolution cell imaging calls for a high-numerical-aperture (NA) oil objective, whereas large organisms or cleared tissue can require a long-working-distance water or air objective. Conventional oblique plane microscopy (OPM) uses one primary objective for illumination and detection. Its secondary and tertiary optics relay a tilted image to the camera and are matched to the primary objective. Exchanging that objective upsets the magnification and refractive-index match, introducing aberrations.

## 3. Basic concepts

NA combines the acceptance angle of the objective with the refractive index of its medium. Higher NA helps resolve finer structures, but narrows the focus range and increases sensitivity to alignment, coverslip and immersion errors. Remote focusing reproduces the specimen focal plane in a second optical space so a tilted plane can be imaged on the camera. The mapping of angles from specimen to remote space is central.

## 4. Limits of earlier methods

Lattice light-sheet microscopy and Field Synthesis offer high resolution and low phototoxicity but require dedicated mounting and a separate illumination objective. Earlier high-NA OPM designs accommodate standard sample holders, yet changing the primary objective requires downstream realignment. Large-field, low-NA OPM trades intracellular resolution and light collection for volume. A single instrument could not readily switch among resolution, field of view and working distance.

## 5. Three steps in the new approach

First, the authors derived the Any Immersion Remote Refocusing (AIRR) condition to calculate the required remote magnification across a family of 40× objectives with equal focal lengths but different immersion media. Second, a zoom lens adjusts this magnification while keeping its front and rear focal planes in place, avoiding extensive relay reconstruction. Third, the oil, silicone oil and water objectives operate at a 40° oblique angle, and the air objective at 50°. A galvanometer mirror and motorized stage scan the sheet and specimen.

### Follow the light through three objectives

The first objective, O1, forms a thin, tilted illumination plane in the specimen and collects fluorescence emitted from it. Illuminating only this plane suppresses light from other heights. Sweeping it sideways builds a volume from individual camera frames, each of which captures a plane.

O2 reimages the information collected by O1 in remote space. The specimen itself stays put while its light forms a corresponding image elsewhere. O3 relays the tilted image to the camera. A sharp image at one plane says little about planes in front of or behind it, so remote focusing requires a mapping across a volume.

Here magnification determines how lengths, angles and out-of-focus positions are relayed into remote space. The miOPM zoom matches this mapping to the specimen refractive index while keeping the front and rear focal planes fixed. Merely reproducing a similar image size with a fixed relay would not ensure that mapping. The original optical layout appears in [Figure 1c–d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41592-026-03245-0/figures/1).

## 6. Equations and system boundaries

The lateral magnification condition matches specimen and remote spaces with different refractive indices. It is explained in the Foundations section and around Equations (5)–(9) of Supplementary Note 1. The notation below restates that explanation.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="miopm-airr-lateral-magnification" title="How should magnification match the refractive indices of the two spaces?" tex={"M = \\frac{n_s}{n_r}"} >

`M` denotes lateral magnification from specimen to remote space, `n_s` the specimen refractive index and `n_r` the remote refractive index. All are dimensionless. Here the remote space is air, so `n_r=1`. Changing the specimen index requires adjusting the zoom ratio as well as exchanging the objective. The authors define the remote focus range as the axial interval where the Strehl ratio `S` is at least 0.8. Higher NA shortens that interval.

</CandidateEquation>

### Why do 1.33 and 0.75 both appear as magnifications?

Lateral magnification describes **how an image length changes** between the two spaces. Pupil magnification describes how the optical pupil is relayed. The water-to-air pupil magnification of 0.75 in the paper cannot be substituted for lateral magnification in the equation above.

With water at 1.33 and air at 1, the lateral magnification is 1.33. This example checks the orientation of the ratio. It supplies neither an additional measurement nor evidence that aberrations vanish at all depths.

**The boundary condition matters.** The supplement expands the phase terms with the specimen interface at the nominal focal plane. Terms that remain away from focus limit imaging depth. Moving the interface away from its intended position can introduce additional aberration even at focus. Alignment and focus control still matter after the magnification is set.

Check: Will the same quality persist deeper into the specimen once magnification matches? Image-scale matching and the range of acceptable aberration impose different conditions.

### Why does depth remain limited after magnification is matched?

Supplementary Note 1 combines the phase introduced by the remote optics with the contribution of the specimen index boundary in Equation (7). Some terms cancel under the matching conditions. For equal effective focal lengths, an on-axis analysis and the source's approximate expansion, setting M=ns/n2 and placing the interface at the nominal focus leaves the term in Equation (8).

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="miopm-si-eq8" title="Which phase term remains away from focus?" tex={String.raw`\Delta\Psi=-\frac{k n_1\rho^2\sin^2\alpha}{2f}\,z_1^2\left(1+\frac{n_1}{n_2}\right)`} >

ΔΨ is phase error, k the vacuum wavenumber (inverse length), n1 and n2 the refractive indices of the two imaging units, ρ the normalized pupil radius and α the maximum semiaperture angle of the first unit. Both units have effective focal length f, and z1 is axial displacement in the first. The refractive indices and ρ are dimensionless. So is k·z1²/f, consistent with phase. This α denotes a different angle from the tilt of the illumination plane.

The dependence on z1² matters. In this approximation, holding other factors fixed, doubling displacement quadruples the magnitude of the residual phase term. This proportionality does not quantify a fourfold change in usable imaging depth or Strehl ratio. The final assessment uses residual phase after piston and defocus are removed.

</CandidateEquation>

The equation distinguishes matching magnification and boundary conditions after an objective change from eliminating aberration over depth. Equation (9) rewrites the same residual in specimen-index and angle coordinates. Additional terms arise if magnification is mismatched or the interface leaves the focal plane.

### Why refocus before evaluating aberration?

Wavefront error contains several modes. Piston adds the same phase to every ray without changing the focal shape. Defocus can be corrected by shifting the focus. The team removed both components before assessing the remaining error. Equation (15) in Supplementary Note 1 gives the expression below.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="miopm-si-eq15" title="What is subtracted before comparing focal quality?" tex={String.raw`\Delta\Psi' = \Delta\Psi - a\Psi_p - \delta z\Psi_d`} >

This expression computes **residual phase before evaluation**. `ΔΨ` is the original phase error, `Ψp=1` the piston mode and `a` its coefficient. `Ψd` is a defocus mode appropriate to high NA and `δz` the axial shift coefficient that removes its contribution. Phase and `a` are dimensionless (radians), `δz` has units of length, and `Ψd` as defined in the paper has units of inverse length. All subtracted terms therefore have matching dimensions. Equations (13)–(14) obtain the coefficients from inner products with the modes over the pupil.

</CandidateEquation>

Equation (16) calculates the Strehl ratio from residual phase. This dimensionless measure compares the concentration of light at the focus with an ideal reference. The axial range reaching the 0.8 threshold defines the theoretical imaging depth. That depth differs from an observed depth of sharp imaging in tissue. The interpretation assumes small phase error, an axial analysis, the objective sine condition and the interface position described above. The calculation links magnification match to residual phase, focal quality and finally a finite depth range.

## 7. Experiments and results

The reported refractive-index range of 1.33–1.51 describes operation with the zoom adjusted to the specimen. Quality also depends on depth, field position, the finite remote focus range, the coverslip and immersion alignment. Supplementary Table 1 gives calculated remote focus ranges of 128.4, 85.3, 70.1 and 69.3 µm for air (NA 0.95), water (1.15), silicone oil (1.25) and oil (1.3), respectively. At 488 nm and the maximum available excitation NA, calculated light-sheet thickness/length ranges from 1.9/113 µm with air to 0.39/3.6 µm with oil. A table note says an adjustable slit reduced the excitation NA in most acquisitions. Obliquity and illumination aperture thus matter alongside objective selection. The tabulated calculations are not measured sheets used in every experiment.

The authors tested resolution and focus range with 100 nm fluorescent beads and imaged fixed cells and tissues, living cultured cells, cleared mouse brain and expanded tissue. Their living-organism example was an amoeba. The study did not establish in vivo imaging in a living mammal. Figure 4's 384-well experiment used fixed U-2 OS cells. A separate three-hour live-cell migration experiment followed SU8686 cells in collagen in an eight-well chamber. They represent different throughput and imaging conditions. The data record provides about 50 GB supporting main Figures 1–5. The paper also reports public numerical code. This review did not independently reproduce all repositories and computing environments.

### How is resolution measured?

A point spread function (PSF) describes how a small, point-like emitter spreads after passing through the optical system. Even an ideally tiny object acquires a width in the image. Imaging small fluorescent beads helps distinguish optical blur from specimen structure. The physical bead size and acquisition conditions also enter the interpretation.

The figure below illustrates validation in an earlier OPM study. Panels D–E show how spot width changes away from focus under different illumination conditions. Panel H compares raw data with deconvolution, a computational reconstruction that accounts for optical spreading. The narrower post-reconstruction spot must be identified as such before comparing it with a raw optical measurement. Use the same distinction when reading miOPM Figure 1e and the supplementary table.

“Diffraction-limited” refers to proximity to the ideal response for a given wavelength and aperture. The field edge, out-of-focus position, specimen interface and use of reconstruction can all alter the comparison. That is why the theoretical S≥0.8 range and actual observation depth in tissue are reported separately.

## 8. Evidence that qualifies the conclusions

The public peer-review file records both early criticism and later assessments. Reviewers initially questioned the comparisons and wording below (PDF pages 8–9). After revision, reviewer 1 found substantial improvements in the discussion and design explanation, and reviewer 2 considered the work ready for publication (pages 10–11). The initial objections should be read together with those assessments. Supplementary Note 2 separately compares collection efficiency and photobleaching at 30° and 40°.

Reviewers questioned resolution claims based on deconvolution instead of raw images, the fairness of the lattice light-sheet comparison, spatially variable blur in some images, and the risk of presenting existing OPM applications as exclusive to miOPM. In supplementary experiments, the 40° oil configuration had a calculated 8% lower collection efficiency and 1.3 times the volume of the 30° setup. Photobleaching was similar to Field Synthesis, which performed slightly better in this comparison. The authors did not consider the measured data conclusive evidence for the predicted 8% difference.

## 9. Limitations

### How does the quality of individual components combine?

Supplementary Note 3 distinguishes remote-focus aberrations from those contributed by other lenses. In the authors' **approximate example**, three objectives with Strehl ratios of 0.95 and independent aberrations give a system ratio around 0.86, the product of three factors of 0.95. This illustrates how system quality can fall below that of each component. The authors did not measure 0.86 for the actual instrument. Correlated aberrations can partly cancel, limiting the usefulness of multiplication as a universal predictor.

The same note cites the wide usable field and alignment tolerance as reasons for choosing 40× objectives. Downstream optics can limit the actual field, so nominal objective field gains need not translate directly into imaged area. AIRR theory also assumes a coverslip of the appropriate type and thickness, corrected for the objective and introducing no further aberration. Optical magnification matching addresses one part of the instrument and sample error budget.

Even under AIRR, aberration accumulates away from the nominal focal plane. High-NA objectives are more sensitive to coverslip thickness, tilt and immersion medium. With an air objective viewing a water specimen, the upper coverslip surface must be held within ±5 µm of nominal focus to avoid potentially substantial global spherical aberration, making focus lock practically necessary. This is a complex custom instrument. Maintenance and alignment costs and independent laboratories' success in building it remain unverified.

## 10. What changes in practice?

The advance concerns combining capabilities that previously required multiple instruments. A core imaging facility could potentially move between sensitive live cells, large tissues and high-throughput plates on the same platform. Magnification correction, angle selection, focus lock and specimen preparation continue to shape performance after the objective is exchanged.

## 11. What to watch next

Independent builds, long-term stability, alignment times, total cost, raw resolution metrics, matched-sample and matched-light-dose comparisons with conventional OPM, lattice light-sheet and confocal microscopy, and independent runs of the public code would strengthen the evidence. The present study supplies a versatile design and demonstrations across several sample types.
