# The Mind's Eye: From Light to Meaning

Open your eyes in the morning and color, shape, and motion enter awareness almost at once. Seeing feels so immediate that the work behind it is easy to overlook.

## Main findings
- The brain interprets and reconstructs retinal input in stages to produce visual experience.
- The report follows visual information through the retina, the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), V1, and higher visual areas, then examines how researchers decode neural activity.
- The interactive guide illustrates the function of each stage.

## Reading order
1. Compare rods and cones to understand the input stage.
2. Follow the operations at successive stages of the visual pathway.
3. Explore neural decoding and the research questions it raises.

## 🧠 A journey through the visual system

The human visual system performs remarkably sophisticated information processing. Follow a ray of light through the stages that contribute to conscious sight.

    function initV1Simulator() {
        const canvas = document.getElementById('v1-canvas');
        const ctx = canvas.getContext('2d');
        const slider = document.getElementById('orientation-slider');
        const valueSpan = document.getElementById('orientation-value');
        function drawFilteredImage() {
            const orientation = parseInt(slider.value);
            valueSpan.textContent = orientation;
            ctx.clearRect(0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);
            ctx.fillStyle = '#f8f8f8';
            ctx.fillRect(0, 0, canvas.width, canvas.height);
            ctx.strokeStyle = '#000';
            ctx.lineWidth = 3;
            const targetAngle = (orientation * Math.PI) / 180;
            const tolerance = Math.PI / 12;
            for (let i = 0; i < 15; i++) {
                const x = Math.random() * canvas.width;
                const y = Math.random() * canvas.height;
                const length = 40 + Math.random() * 40;
                const angle = Math.random() * Math.PI;
                const angleDiff = Math.abs(angle - targetAngle);

        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Stage 1 | Eye and retina: sensing the visual world</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            Light passes through the eye before reaching the retina. The cornea and lens refract the light to focus an image on the retina. Neural processing already begins there. The retina is a specialized neural circuit extending from the central nervous system into the eye. Through phototransduction it converts physical light energy into electrical signals and begins extracting visual information.
        </p>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-green-50 to-emerald-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-green-200 mb-8">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-green-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-green-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">

                    </svg>
                </div>
                From light to an electrical signal: rods and cones
            </h3>
            <p class="text-green-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                The retina has two classes of photoreceptors that detect light: rods and cones.<sup>3</sup> Their different sensitivities and functions provide a combination analogous to a high-resolution color sensor for daylight and a highly sensitive monochrome sensor for night.
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-gray-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">R</span>
                        Rods
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-gray-700">
                        <p><strong>Count:</strong> Approximately 92 million rods provide high sensitivity to weak light.<sup>4</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> Rods are concentrated away from the retinal center. They support vision in dim light (scotopic vision) and help detect movement.<sup>3</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> Rods alone cannot discriminate colors. When they dominate vision in very dim light, the scene appears largely monochrome.</p>
                        <p><strong>Pigment:</strong> Rods use rhodopsin, a visual pigment whose light-sensitive component derives from vitamin A. Vitamin A deficiency can impair vision in dim light and cause night blindness.<sup>4</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-blue-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">C</span>
                        Cones
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-gray-700">
                        <p><strong>Count:</strong> About 6 million cones support fine central vision and color vision under bright (photopic) conditions.<sup>3</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> Cones are concentrated in the macula, particularly its central fovea. We direct our gaze toward an object to place fine detail on this region.<sup>3</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Color perception:</strong> The three cone types have overlapping sensitivities to short, medium, and long wavelengths, often described as blue, green, and red. Their combined responses allow us to distinguish many colors.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 p-4 bg-green-100 rounded-lg">
                <p class="text-green-900 text-sm font-medium">
                    📋 Parallel input channels give vision both the fine shape and color information available in daylight and sensitivity to movement under dimmer conditions.

                The retina's first computations: receptive fields, edges, and contrast
            </h3>
            <p class="text-blue-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                Photoreceptors signal through bipolar cells to retinal ganglion cells. Horizontal and amacrine cells modulate the signals within this network of retinal interneurons.<sup>4</sup> Substantial processing therefore takes place before the signals even leave the eye.
            </p>
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 mb-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-blue-900 mb-4">How a receptive field works</h4>
                <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-4">
                    A ganglion cell's receptive field is the area of the retina in which light can change that cell's activity.<sup>8</sup> In the 1950s, Stephen Kuffler's experiments helped establish that many ganglion cells have a central region and a surrounding region that exert opposing effects. This arrangement is called center–surround organization.<sup>10</sup>
                </p>
                <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4">
                    <div class="bg-blue-50 rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h5 class="font-medium text-blue-900 mb-2">ON-center/OFF-surround cell</h5>
                        <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm">Light in the center excites the cell and increases its firing rate. Light in the surround inhibits the cell and reduces its firing rate.<sup>9</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-blue-50 rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h5 class="font-medium text-blue-900 mb-2">OFF-center/ON-surround cell</h5>
                        <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm">Light in the center inhibits the cell, while light in the surround excites it.<sup>9</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-blue-100 rounded-lg p-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-blue-900 mb-4">[Visualization: responses of a retinal ganglion cell's receptive field]</h4>
                <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-4">
                    The diagram illustrates how an ON-center cell responds to different patterns of illumination.
                </p>
                <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-blue-800">
                    <p><strong>Center only:</strong> Illuminating only the center strongly excites the cell, increasing its firing rate.</p>
                    <p><strong>Surround only:</strong> Illuminating only the surround strongly inhibits the cell, reducing its firing rate.</p>
                    <p><strong>Center and surround together:</strong> Under uniform illumination, excitation from the center and inhibition from the surround can largely cancel. The firing rate may remain near its spontaneous level.<sup>9</sup></p>
                    <p><strong>Edge stimulus:</strong> When a light–dark boundary crosses the receptive field, a bright center beside a dark surround can produce a strong response.</p>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 p-4 bg-blue-100 rounded-lg">
                <p class="text-blue-900 text-sm font-medium">
                    📋 <strong>Mechanism:</strong> Lateral inhibition contributes to this arrangement. Signals from neighboring retinal regions influence one another through circuits that include horizontal cells.<sup>7</sup> This organization makes ganglion cells sensitive to spatial contrast, the difference in brightness across locations.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="mt-8 p-6 bg-gray-50 rounded-lg">
            <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4">Data compression and efficient coding</h4>
            <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                The retina contains nearly 100 million photoreceptors, but only about a million ganglion cells transmit its output toward the brain. Uniform regions produce redundant signals. Spatial contrast and edges convey information critical for identifying contours, so center–surround circuits emphasize these changes. This is an example of efficient coding under limited transmission capacity. Retinal output already describes relationships between locations in the image.
            </p>
        </div>

        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Stage 2 | Optic nerve and thalamus: transmission and relay</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            Processed signals travel along the optic nerve to the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) in the thalamus. This journey supports more than transmission. Contrasts extracted in the retina are routed, sorted, and modulated before cortical interpretation.
        </p>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-purple-50 to-violet-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-purple-200 mb-8">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-purple-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-purple-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M12 19l9 2-9-18-9 18 9-2zm0 0v-8"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                The optic chiasm connects each visual hemifield to the opposite hemisphere
            </h3>
            <p class="text-purple-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                The axons of retinal ganglion cells come together in the optic nerve, which contains roughly a million nerve fibers.<sup>10</sup> Some fibers cross at the optic chiasm on the underside of the brain. This arrangement delivers information about the opposite visual hemifield to each cerebral hemisphere.
            </p>
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 mb-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-4">Which fibers cross?</h4>
                <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                    <div class="space-y-4">
                        <div class="p-4 bg-purple-50 rounded-lg">
                            <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-2">Nasal retinal fibers</h5>
                            <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross at the chiasm. They carry information about the temporal (outer) part of that eye's visual field.<sup>11</sup></p>
                        </div>
                        <div class="p-4 bg-purple-50 rounded-lg">
                            <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-2">Temporal retinal fibers</h5>
                            <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Fibers from the temporal half of each retina continue on the same side of the brain. They carry information about that eye's nasal (inner) visual field.<sup>11</sup></p>
                        </div>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-purple-100 rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-3">An integrated visual field map</h5>
                        <div class="space-y-2 text-sm text-purple-800">
                            <p><strong>Right hemisphere:</strong> Receives the left visual hemifield from both eyes where their views overlap</p>
                            <p><strong>Left hemisphere:</strong> Receives the right visual hemifield from both eyes where their views overlap</p>
                            <p><strong>Significance:</strong> Bringing the two eyes' views of the same region to one hemisphere makes their signals available for estimating depth through binocular disparity.<sup>11</sup></p>
                        </div>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="p-4 bg-purple-100 rounded-lg">
                <p class="text-purple-900 text-sm font-medium">
                    📋 <strong>Clinical significance:</strong> Damage at the optic chiasm can produce a characteristic pattern of visual field loss. For example, compression by a pituitary tumor can cause bitemporal hemianopia, a loss of the outer visual field in each eye.<sup>12</sup>
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-indigo-50 to-blue-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-indigo-200">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-indigo-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-indigo-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M19 11H5m14 0a2 2 0 012 2v6a2 2 0 01-2 2H5a2 2 0 01-2-2v-6a2 2 0 012-2m14 0V9a2 2 0 00-2-2M5 11V9a2 2 0 012-2m0 0V5a2 2 0 012-2h6a2 2 0 012 2v2M7 7h10"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                The lateral geniculate nucleus: an organized visual relay
            </h3>
            <p class="text-indigo-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                Signals from the optic nerve reach the LGN of the thalamus. This structure processes and regulates incoming visual information. In primates it has six distinguishable principal layers, with different layers carrying signals with different response properties.<sup>13</sup>
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-3 gap-6 mb-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-green-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">M</span>
                        Magnocellular pathway
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> LGN layers 1–2 (the two lower layers)</p>
                        <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Magnocellular neurons have large cell bodies.</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> These neurons carry signals important for motion, depth, and low spatial frequencies, which describe broad patterns with relatively little fine detail.<sup>14</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> High sensitivity to changes over time supports the detection of rapid movement.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-red-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">P</span>
                        Parvocellular pathway
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> LGN layers 3–6 (the four upper layers)</p>
                        <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Parvocellular neurons have smaller cell bodies.</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> They carry color, texture, and high-spatial-frequency signals that support fine detail.<sup>14</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> Their sensitivity to spatial detail contributes to precise form recognition and color discrimination.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-blue-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">K</span>
                        Koniocellular pathway
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> Interlaminar zones between the six principal LGN layers</p>
                        <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Koniocellular neurons have very small cell bodies.</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> This heterogeneous pathway includes blue–yellow opponent signals, sensitivity to low spatial frequencies, and other specialized visual functions.<sup>15</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> Identified more recently than the M and P pathways, K cells account for roughly 10% of LGN neurons in the estimate used here.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4">Additional roles of the LGN</h4>
                <div class="space-y-4 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                    <div class="flex items-start">
                        <div class="w-3 h-3 bg-indigo-400 rounded-full mt-2 mr-3 flex-shrink-0"></div>
                        <div>
                            <strong>Attentional modulation:</strong> Signals from cortex and brainstem alter the transmission of visual input. Depending on attention, the LGN can strengthen relevant signals and suppress less relevant ones.<sup>17</sup>
                        </div>
                    </div>
                    <div class="flex items-start">
                        <div class="w-3 h-3 bg-indigo-400 rounded-full mt-2 mr-3 flex-shrink-0"></div>
                        <div>
                            <strong>Sleep and wakefulness:</strong> Changes in thalamic transmission during sleep can reduce the influence of external visual input on awareness, including during dreaming.<sup>18</sup>
                        </div>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="mt-8 p-6 bg-gray-50 rounded-lg">
            <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4">Sorting and modulating visual information</h4>
            <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                In the LGN, retinal contrasts are organized into parallel channels with differing sensitivities to motion, color, and fine form. This division of labor helps the brain analyze a complex scene. Attention and arousal also regulate how readily these signals pass on to cortex.
            </p>
        </div>
<div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-purple-50 to-violet-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-purple-200 mb-8">
    <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
        <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-purple-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
            <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-purple-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M12 19l9 2-9-18-9 18 9-2zm0 0v-8"></path>
            </svg>
        </div>
        The optic chiasm connects each visual hemifield to the opposite hemisphere
    </h3>
    <p class="text-purple-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
        The axons of retinal ganglion cells come together in the optic nerve, which contains roughly a million nerve fibers.<sup>10</sup> Some fibers cross at the optic chiasm on the underside of the brain. This arrangement delivers information about the opposite visual hemifield to each cerebral hemisphere.
    </p>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 mb-6">
        <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-4">Which fibers cross?</h4>
        <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
            <div class="space-y-4">
                <div class="p-4 bg-purple-50 rounded-lg">
                    <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-2">Nasal retinal fibers</h5>
                    <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Fibers from the nasal half of each retina cross at the chiasm. They carry information about the temporal (outer) part of that eye's visual field.<sup>11</sup></p>
                </div>
                <div class="p-4 bg-purple-50 rounded-lg">
                    <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-2">Temporal retinal fibers</h5>
                    <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Fibers from the temporal half of each retina continue on the same side of the brain. They carry information about that eye's nasal (inner) visual field.<sup>11</sup></p>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-purple-100 rounded-lg p-4">
                <h5 class="font-medium text-purple-900 mb-3">An integrated visual field map</h5>
                <div class="space-y-2 text-sm text-purple-800">
                    <p><strong>Right hemisphere:</strong> Receives the left visual hemifield from both eyes where their views overlap</p>
                    <p><strong>Left hemisphere:</strong> Receives the right visual hemifield from both eyes where their views overlap</p>
                    <p><strong>Significance:</strong> Bringing the two eyes' views of the same region to one hemisphere makes their signals available for estimating depth through binocular disparity.<sup>11</sup></p>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
    <div class="p-4 bg-purple-100 rounded-lg">
        <p class="text-purple-900 text-sm font-medium">
            📋 <strong>Clinical significance:</strong> Damage at the optic chiasm can produce a characteristic pattern of visual field loss. For example, compression by a pituitary tumor can cause bitemporal hemianopia, a loss of the outer visual field in each eye.<sup>12</sup>
        </p>
    </div>
</div>
<div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-indigo-50 to-blue-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-indigo-200">
    <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
        <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-indigo-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
            <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-indigo-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M19 11H5m14 0a2 2 0 012 2v6a2 2 0 01-2 2H5a2 2 0 01-2-2v-6a2 2 0 012-2m14 0V9a2 2 0 00-2-2M5 11V9a2 2 0 012-2m0 0V5a2 2 0 012-2h6a2 2 0 012 2v2M7 7h10"></path>
            </svg>
        </div>
        The lateral geniculate nucleus: an organized visual relay
    </h3>
    <p class="text-indigo-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
        Signals from the optic nerve reach the LGN of the thalamus. This structure processes and regulates incoming visual information. In primates it has six distinguishable principal layers, with different layers carrying signals with different response properties.<sup>13</sup>
    </p>
    <div class="grid md:grid-cols-3 gap-6 mb-6">
        <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
            <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-green-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">M</span>
                Magnocellular pathway
            </h4>
            <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                <p><strong>Location:</strong> LGN layers 1–2 (the two lower layers)</p>
                <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Magnocellular neurons have large cell bodies.</p>
                <p><strong>Function:</strong> These neurons carry signals important for motion, depth, and low spatial frequencies, which describe broad patterns with relatively little fine detail.<sup>14</sup></p>
                <p><strong>Property:</strong> High sensitivity to changes over time supports the detection of rapid movement.</p>
                <p><strong>Color sensitivity:</strong> Relatively low color sensitivity, with responses chiefly to luminance.</p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
            <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-red-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">P</span>
                Parvocellular pathway
            </h4>
            <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                <p><strong>Location:</strong> LGN layers 3–6 (the four upper layers)</p>
                <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Parvocellular neurons have smaller cell bodies.</p>
                <p><strong>Function:</strong> They carry color, texture, and high-spatial-frequency signals that support fine detail.<sup>14</sup></p>
                <p><strong>Property:</strong> Their sensitivity to spatial detail contributes to precise form recognition and color discrimination.</p>
                <p><strong>Temporal resolution:</strong> Responses to rapid changes are relatively slow.</p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
            <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-blue-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">K</span>
                Koniocellular pathway
            </h4>
            <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-indigo-800">
                <p><strong>Location:</strong> Interlaminar zones between the six principal LGN layers</p>
                <p><strong>Cell size:</strong> Koniocellular neurons have very small cell bodies.</p>
                <p><strong>Function:</strong> This heterogeneous pathway includes blue–yellow opponent signals, sensitivity to low spatial frequencies, and other specialized visual functions.<sup>15</sup></p>
                <p><strong>Property:</strong> Koniocellular neurons account for about 10% of LGN neurons in the estimate cited here. Many of their functions remain under investigation.</p>
                <p><strong>Evolutionary significance:</strong> This pathway has been proposed to be evolutionarily ancient.</p>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-indigo-100 rounded-lg p-6 mb-6">
        <h4 class="font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4">LGN receptive fields: luminance and color contrast</h4>
        <p class="text-indigo-800 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-4">
            Many LGN neurons, like retinal ganglion cells, have antagonistic center–surround receptive fields. More specialized contrasts are also evident here. For instance, color opponency in the parvocellular pathway provides sensitivity to differences in chromatic input as well as to luminance contrast.<sup>16</sup>
        </p>
        <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-4">
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                <h5 class="font-medium text-indigo-900 mb-2">Red–green opponent cells</h5>
                <p class="text-indigo-800 text-sm">The center may favor a long-wavelength (red) signal and the surround a medium-wavelength (green) signal, or the preferences may be reversed. This opposition emphasizes subtle red–green differences.<sup>16</sup></p>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                <h5 class="font-medium text-indigo-900 mb-2">Blue–yellow opponent cells</h5>
                <p class="text-indigo-800 text-sm">Opposing short-wavelength cone signals and combined longer-wavelength signals helps distinguish bluish hues, such as those of the sky, from yellowish hues, such as those of some fruits.<sup>16</sup></p>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
        <h4 class="font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4">Additional roles of the LGN</h4>
        <div class="space-y-4 text-sm text-indigo-800">
            <div class="flex items-start">
                <div class="w-3 h-3 bg-indigo-400 rounded-full mt-2 mr-3 flex-shrink-0"></div>
                <div>
                    <strong>Attentional modulation:</strong> Signals from cortex and brainstem alter the transmission of visual input. Depending on attention, the LGN can strengthen relevant signals and suppress less relevant ones.<sup>17</sup>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="flex items-start">
                <div class="w-3 h-3 bg-indigo-400 rounded-full mt-2 mr-3 flex-shrink-0"></div>
                <div>
                    <strong>Sleep and wakefulness:</strong> Changes in thalamic transmission during sleep can reduce the influence of external visual input on awareness, including during dreaming.<sup>18</sup>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="flex items-start">
                <div class="w-3 h-3 bg-indigo-400 rounded-full mt-2 mr-3 flex-shrink-0"></div>
                <div>
                    <strong>Coordination with eye movements:</strong> Visual processing changes around rapid eye movements (saccades). This coordination contributes to the reduced visibility of disturbances during a saccade, known as saccadic masking.<sup>19</sup>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
</div>
<div class="mt-8 p-6 bg-gray-50 rounded-lg">
    <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4">Sorting and modulating visual information</h4>
    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
        The LGN sorts and modulates the contrast signals it receives from the retina. Parallel channels emphasize motion, color, and fine detail, distributing the work of analyzing a scene. Attention and arousal influence the information passed onward. These organized signals next reach primary visual cortex for further interpretation.
    </p>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="max-w-6xl mx-auto px-8 pb-12">
    <div class="flex items-center mb-8">
        <div class="w-12 h-12 bg-gradient-to-br from-orange-500 to-red-600 rounded-xl flex items-center justify-center text-white font-bold text-xl mr-4">
            3
        </div>
        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Stage 3 | Primary visual cortex: extracting basic features</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            From the thalamic LGN, signals reach primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe.<sup>26</sup> Also known as striate cortex, V1 is the first cortical stage of the principal visual pathway. Many of its neurons encode oriented edges and line segments. This discovery changed our understanding of how cortex represents a scene. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work.<sup>28</sup>
        </p>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-orange-50 to-red-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-orange-200 mb-8">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-orange-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-orange-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-orange-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M9.663 17h4.673M12 3v1m6.364 1.636l-.707.707M21 12h-1M4 12H3m3.343-5.657l-.707-.707m2.828 9.9a5 5 0 117.072 0l-.548.547A3.374 3.374 0 0014 18.469V19a2 2 0 11-4 0v-.531c0-.895-.356-1.754-.988-2.386l-.548-.547z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Hubel and Wiesel's discovery of orientation selectivity
            </h3>
            <p class="text-orange-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                One initial expectation was that cortical neurons would respond to small spots of light like the cells studied in the retina and LGN. While recording V1 neurons in anesthetized cats, Hubel and Wiesel noticed strong firing when the edge of a slide moved across the visual field.<sup>26</sup> This observation led to the discovery that many V1 neurons respond selectively to a line or boundary at a particular orientation.
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-orange-900 mb-4">Simple cells</h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-orange-800">
                        <p>Their receptive fields have elongated, distinguishable excitatory (ON) and inhibitory (OFF) subregions.<sup>25</sup></p>
                        <p>They respond best to a line segment presented at a particular angle and position.</p>
                        <p>For example, a cell tuned to a vertical line may respond weakly or not at all to a horizontal or diagonal line.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-orange-900 mb-4">Complex cells</h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-orange-800">
                        <p>These cells also favor particular orientations but respond to a feature across a wider range of positions than simple cells do.<sup>25</sup></p>
                        <p>They lack clearly separated ON and OFF subregions. An oriented segment can elicit a response at several positions within the receptive field.</p>
                        <p>Some complex cells also respond strongly to a line moving in a particular direction.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 p-4 bg-orange-100 rounded-lg">
                <p class="text-orange-900 text-sm font-medium">
                    📋 One useful model treats V1 as a bank of orientation filters. Many neural populations analyze the same image in parallel, each emphasizing a particular orientation in a particular part of the visual field.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-blue-50 to-cyan-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-blue-200">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-blue-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-blue-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-blue-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M4.318 6.318a4.5 4.5 0 000 6.364L12 20.364l7.682-7.682a4.5 4.5 0 00-6.364-6.364L12 7.636l-1.318-1.318a4.5 4.5 0 00-6.364 0z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Imaging orientation maps and pinwheels
            </h3>
            <p class="text-blue-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                If neighboring V1 neurons prefer certain orientations, how are those preferences distributed over the cortical surface? Optical imaging and fMRI have made that spatial organization visible.<sup>26</sup>
            </p>
            <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 mb-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-blue-900 mb-4">Features of orientation maps and pinwheels</h4>
                <div class="space-y-4">
                    <div class="p-4 bg-blue-50 rounded-lg">
                        <h5 class="font-medium text-blue-900 mb-2">Orientation map</h5>
                        <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm">Neurons with similar preferred orientations tend to cluster into orientation columns. Preferences change progressively across the cortical surface, forming a mosaic-like orientation map.<sup>31</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="p-4 bg-blue-50 rounded-lg">
                        <h5 class="font-medium text-blue-900 mb-2">Pinwheel organization</h5>
                        <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm">Around a pinwheel center, columns are arranged like spokes, sampling orientations across a half turn (0–180 degrees). This organization places units sensitive to a range of orientations close together for a local region of visual space. It has been interpreted as an efficient way to cover locations and orientations.<sup>34</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-blue-100 rounded-lg p-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-blue-900 mb-4">The emergence of feature-based organization</h4>
                <p class="text-blue-800 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                    V1 introduces another organizing principle. The retina and LGN retain spatial relationships in a retinotopic map. V1 preserves a map of where a stimulus occurs and also organizes responses according to properties such as its orientation. Together they encode location and feature.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
</div>
<div class="max-w-6xl mx-auto px-8 pb-12">
    <div class="flex items-center mb-8">
        <div class="w-12 h-12 bg-gradient-to-br from-teal-500 to-cyan-600 rounded-xl flex items-center justify-center text-white font-bold text-xl mr-4">
            4
        </div>
        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Stage 4 | Higher visual cortex: assembling representations of form and motion</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            From V1, signals for local features such as oriented line segments reach surrounding visual areas, including V2, V3, V4, and V5/MT. These areas show different functional specializations. Through their interactions, simple signals contribute to more complex representations of color, depth, form, and motion.<sup>30</sup>
        </p>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-teal-50 to-cyan-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-teal-200 mb-8">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-teal-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-teal-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-teal-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M9 12l2 2 4-4m6 2a9 9 0 11-18 0 9 9 0 0118 0z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Hierarchical processing and functional specialization
            </h3>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-3 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-teal-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-pink-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">V4</span>
                        Color and form
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-teal-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> A key area of the ventral stream</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> Contributes to the perception of color and shape.<sup>23</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> Many neurons respond to curves or complex patterns more selectively than to simple line segments.</p>
                        <p><strong>Color constancy:</strong> Helps support stable perception of an object's color under changes in illumination.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-teal-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-green-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">V5</span>
                        Motion processing
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-teal-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> A key area of the dorsal stream</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> Strongly specialized for visual motion.<sup>38</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> About 90% of neurons show direction selectivity in the estimate presented here.<sup>40</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Motion blindness:</strong> Damage affecting this area can disrupt the perception of continuous motion.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-teal-900 mb-4 flex items-center">
                        <span class="w-6 h-6 bg-blue-600 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center text-white text-xs font-bold mr-3">V2</span>
                        Integration and depth
                    </h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-teal-800">
                        <p><strong>Location:</strong> Adjacent to V1</p>
                        <p><strong>Function:</strong> Combines signals arriving from V1.</p>
                        <p><strong>Property:</strong> Some neurons respond to illusory contours.</p>
                        <p><strong>Depth perception:</strong> Binocular disparity provides a cue to depth.</p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 p-4 bg-teal-100 rounded-lg">
                <p class="text-teal-900 text-sm font-medium">
                    📋 Visual cortex beyond V1 has a hierarchical organization. Along pathways from V1 to V2 and onward to areas such as V3 and V4, receptive fields typically become larger. Neurons can respond to increasingly complex combinations of visual features.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-purple-50 to-pink-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-purple-200">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-purple-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-purple-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M9 19v-6a2 2 0 00-2-2H5a2 2 0 00-2 2v6a2 2 0 002 2h2a2 2 0 002-2zm0 0V9a2 2 0 012-2h2a2 2 0 012 2v10m-6 0a2 2 0 002 2h2a2 2 0 002-2m0 0V5a2 2 0 012-2h2a2 2 0 012 2v14a2 2 0 01-2 2h-2a2 2 0 01-2-2z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Imaging functional specialization with fMRI
            </h3>
            <p class="text-purple-800 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                Functional MRI maps task-related signals associated with blood oxygenation. Comparing stimulus conditions makes it possible to visualize which cortical regions respond more strongly to a particular visual feature.<sup>42</sup>
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-4">Visualizing color responses in V4</h4>
                    <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                        Participants alternate between complex colored patterns, such as Mondrian-like pictures, and a simple black-and-white grating. A comparison can show preferential responses to colored patterns in the occipital region associated with V4.
                    </p>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6 border border-gray-200">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-4">Visualizing motion responses in V5/MT</h4>
                    <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                        Participants view hundreds of randomly moving dots alternating with the same dots held stationary. The moving pattern evokes a strong motion-sensitive fMRI response in V5/MT near the temporal–occipital junction.<sup>38</sup>
                    </p>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 bg-purple-100 rounded-lg p-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-4">Activation and awareness</h4>
                <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                    Activity in one region need not coincide with conscious perception. In a study discussed here, V5/MT remained active during rapid visual stimulation even when participants did not report seeing motion.<sup>38</sup> Such findings suggest that conscious perception involves interactions among several regions, potentially including higher frontal and parietal areas, beyond activity in a single specialized visual area.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
</div>
<div class="max-w-6xl mx-auto px-8 pb-12">
    <div class="flex items-center mb-8">
        <div class="w-12 h-12 bg-gradient-to-br from-pink-500 to-rose-600 rounded-xl flex items-center justify-center text-white font-bold text-xl mr-4">
            5
        </div>
        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Stage 5 | Two streams for perception and action</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            Higher visual signals follow two broad streams. The two-streams hypothesis, proposed in 1982 by Leslie Ungerleider and Mortimer Mishkin from work in monkeys and refined for human vision by Melvyn Goodale and David Milner, has strongly influenced cognitive neuroscience.<sup>41</sup>
        </p>
        <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-8 mb-8">
            <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-purple-50 to-violet-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-purple-200">
                <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                    <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-purple-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                        <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-purple-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                            <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M8.228 9c.549-1.165 2.03-2 3.772-2 2.21 0 4 1.343 4 3 0 1.4-1.278 2.575-3.006 2.907-.542.104-.994.54-.994 1.093m0 3h.01M21 12a9 9 0 11-18 0 9 9 0 0118 0z"></path>
                        </svg>
                    </div>
                    Ventral stream
                </h3>
                <div class="space-y-4">
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-2">Identifying what an object is</h4>
                        <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">The ventral stream supports object identification and recognition. This role gives it the name “what” pathway.<sup>23</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-2">Anatomical route</h4>
                        <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Signals from V1 pass through regions including V2 and V4 and extend along ventral cortex into inferior temporal cortex (IT).<sup>23</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-purple-900 mb-2">Effects of damage</h4>
                        <p class="text-purple-800 text-sm">Damage can produce visual agnosia: a person may retain basic visual acuity yet have difficulty recognizing objects. Prosopagnosia, a difficulty recognizing faces, is one example.<sup>41</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-green-50 to-emerald-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-green-200">
                <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-green-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                    <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-green-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                        <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-green-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                            <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M17.657 16.657L13.414 20.9a1.998 1.998 0 01-2.827 0l-4.244-4.243a8 8 0 1111.314 0z"></path>
                            <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M15 11a3 3 0 11-6 0 3 3 0 016 0z"></path>
                        </svg>
                    </div>
                    Dorsal stream
                </h3>
                <div class="space-y-4">
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-green-900 mb-2">Locating and interacting with an object</h4>
                        <p class="text-green-800 text-sm">The dorsal stream represents spatial location and motion and helps guide bodily action. It contributes to answering “Where is it?” and “How do I interact with it?”<sup>41</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-green-900 mb-2">Anatomical route</h4>
                        <p class="text-green-800 text-sm">From V1, projections involving V2 and V5/MT extend along the dorsal route into posterior parietal cortex (PPC).<sup>23</sup></p>
                    </div>
                    <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-4">
                        <h4 class="font-semibold text-green-900 mb-2">Effects of damage</h4>
                        <p class="text-green-800 text-sm">Damage can impair spatial judgments and visually guided action. Examples of deficits associated with affected networks include optic ataxia and hemispatial neglect.<sup>41</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-gray-50 to-slate-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-gray-200">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-gray-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-gray-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M13.828 10.172a4 4 0 00-5.656 0l-4 4a4 4 0 105.656 5.656l1.102-1.101m-.758-4.899a4 4 0 005.656 0l4-4a4 4 0 00-5.656-5.656l-1.1 1.1"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Complementary networks and unified experience
            </h3>
            <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                The two-streams account describes specializations within an interacting system. Research has documented substantial communication between the pathways.
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-3">Examples of interaction</h4>
                    <div class="space-y-3 text-sm text-gray-700">
                        <p><strong>Tool use:</strong> Selecting an appropriate grip for fine manipulation engages the action system. Knowing whether the target is a hammer or a screwdriver also draws on object identification.<sup>49</sup></p>
                        <p><strong>Object recognition:</strong> Recognizing an object partly obscured by a cluttered background may require information about its spatial outline and position as well as its identity.<sup>47</sup></p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-3">Current perspective</h4>
                    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                        The pathways exchange information as complementary networks. Perception and action together support a unified experience of the visual world.
                    </p>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
    </div>
</div>
<div class="max-w-6xl mx-auto px-8 pb-12">
    <div class="flex items-center mb-8">
        <div class="w-12 h-12 bg-gradient-to-br from-indigo-500 to-purple-600 rounded-xl flex items-center justify-center text-white font-bold text-xl mr-4">
            ♦
        </div>
        <h2 class="text-3xl font-bold text-gray-900">Neural decoding: connecting neural activity with visual experience</h2>
    </div>
    <div class="bg-white rounded-2xl shadow-lg border border-gray-100 p-8">
        <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-8">
            How do patterns of spikes relate to experiences as varied as seeing a red rose, a moving car, or a friend's face? This remains an open question in neuroscience and philosophy. Neural decoding provides a quantitative way to investigate part of it: researchers work backward from a pattern of activity to infer the visual information it represents.<sup>46</sup>
        </p>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-indigo-50 to-purple-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-indigo-200 mb-8">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-indigo-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-indigo-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M9 7h6m0 10v-3m-3 3h.01M9 17h.01M9 14h.01M12 14h.01M15 11h.01M12 11h.01M9 11h.01M7 21h10a2 2 0 002-2V5a2 2 0 00-2-2H7a2 2 0 00-2 2v14a2 2 0 002 2z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                Approaches to decoding
            </h3>
            <div class="space-y-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4">fMRI-based visual decoding</h4>
                    <p class="text-indigo-800 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-4">
                        Researchers have used spatial patterns in fMRI measurements to reconstruct aspects of viewed images. The original report attributes to the Kamitani group an accuracy above 90% for classifying simple characters or digits from V1–V3 voxel patterns. That figure concerns a restricted classification task, not the accuracy of reconstructing an arbitrary image.<sup>21</sup>
                    </p>
                    <div class="bg-indigo-50 rounded-lg p-4">
                        <p class="text-indigo-900 text-sm font-medium">
                            Researchers have also reported partial decoding of dream-related visual content from fMRI signals.<sup>20</sup>
                        </p>
                    </div>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="text-lg font-semibold text-indigo-900 mb-4">High-resolution decoding with electrodes</h4>
                    <p class="text-indigo-800 text-sm leading-relaxed mb-4">
                        Electrodes can record spikes from individual neurons. Simultaneous recordings from dozens of V4 or IT neurons in monkeys can carry enough information to predict an object category or certain visual features of a stimulus.<sup>22</sup>
                    </p>
                    <div class="bg-indigo-50 rounded-lg p-4">
                        <p class="text-indigo-900 text-sm font-medium">
                            A related brain–computer interface (BCI) decodes motor cortex activity during attempted handwriting by a person with paralysis. The system can turn the decoded movement patterns into characters on a screen in real time.<sup>48</sup>
                        </p>
                    </div>
                </div>
            </div>
        </div>
        <div class="bg-gradient-to-r from-gray-50 to-slate-50 rounded-xl p-8 border border-gray-200">
            <h3 class="text-2xl font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-6 flex items-center">
                <div class="w-8 h-8 bg-gray-200 rounded-lg flex items-center justify-center mr-3">
                    <svg class="w-4 h-4 text-gray-700" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" viewBox="0 0 24 24">
                        <path stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round" stroke-width="2" d="M8.228 9c.549-1.165 2.03-2 3.772-2 2.21 0 4 1.343 4 3 0 1.4-1.278 2.575-3.006 2.907-.542.104-.994.54-.994 1.093m0 3h.01M21 12a9 9 0 11-18 0 9 9 0 0118 0z"></path>
                    </svg>
                </div>
                The unresolved question of consciousness
            </h3>
            <p class="text-gray-700 leading-relaxed mb-6">
                Even a highly accurate decoder leaves a fundamental question open: how is objective neural activity related to subjective experience?
            </p>
            <div class="grid md:grid-cols-2 gap-6">
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-3">The hard problem</h4>
                    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                        Philosopher David Chalmers calls this the hard problem of consciousness. We can establish that a V1 neuron responds to a line tilted 45 degrees while still asking why neural processing is accompanied by experience at all.<sup>1</sup>
                    </p>
                </div>
                <div class="bg-white rounded-lg p-6">
                    <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-3">The explanatory gap</h4>
                    <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                        In principle, we could record every neuron's activity in extraordinary detail and still face the question “Why does red feel like this?” The relationship between a physical description and the character of an experience remains unresolved.
                    </p>
                </div>
            </div>
            <div class="mt-6 bg-gray-100 rounded-lg p-6">
                <h4 class="font-semibold text-gray-900 mb-4">Future directions</h4>
                <p class="text-gray-700 text-sm leading-relaxed">
                    Advances in artificial neural networks and brain imaging give researchers finer tools. Philosophical analysis can clarify which questions those tools answer. Together these approaches may help connect neural activity with conscious experience as we work out the operations of the visual system piece by piece.
                </p>
            </div>
        </div>
