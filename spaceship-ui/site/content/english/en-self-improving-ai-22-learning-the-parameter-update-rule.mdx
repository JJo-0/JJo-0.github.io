### Worked example 1. How much loss disappears when a brightness offset increases by one?

A camera reads a reference patch at 100 brightness units. An independent reference instrument measures 104 on the same patch. Suppose we add a correction θ to the camera output to predict the reference reading. This <strong>one-observation, one-parameter example is illustrative</strong>. With θ equal to zero, the prediction is 100, and the signed error, prediction minus reference, is −4. Half the squared error gives a loss of 8. The factor of ½ cancels the factor of 2 when we differentiate the square.

<Math tex={String.raw`\hat y=100+\theta,\qquad L(\theta)=\tfrac12(\hat y-104)^2=\tfrac12(\theta-4)^2,\qquad \frac{dL}{d\theta}=\theta-4.`} display />

The slope here is −4. A small increase in θ decreases the loss. Choose a learning rate η of 0.25, multiply the slope by that rate, and subtract the product from the old θ. Subtracting a negative number moves θ up to 1. The slide uses ∇ to collect one partial derivative per parameter. Our example has just one derivative.

<Math tex={String.raw`\theta'=\theta-\eta\frac{dL}{d\theta}=0-0.25(-4)=1,\qquad \hat y'=101,\qquad L(1)=\tfrac12(101-104)^2=4.5.`} display />

The absolute error falls from 4 to 3, and the loss falls from 8 to 4.5. Keep the data and slope fixed and try a learning rate of 2.5. The new θ is 10, and the prediction reaches 110. It overshoots the reference value of 104, raising the absolute error to 6 and the loss to 18. <strong>A local slope supplies a direction. The choice of step size determines where the update lands.</strong> These numbers describe the particular quadratic loss in this example.

### Worked example 2. If the trained inspector gets seven more images right, whose score increased?

In an <strong>illustrative inspection experiment</strong>, a training agent trains an inspector using images of manufactured parts. An evaluator sets aside 50 images that were never used in training and tests the inspector on the same images before and after the update. Pair the two answers for each image. The inspector originally got 32 right. Training corrects 10 previously wrong answers but introduces errors on 3 images the original inspector got right. Both changes enter the final count.

<Math tex={String.raw`n_{\rm after}=32-3+10=39,\qquad a_{\rm before}=\frac{32}{50}=0.64,\quad a_{\rm after}=\frac{39}{50}=0.78.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\Delta a=\frac{10-3}{50}=0.14=14\ \mathrm{pp}.`} display />

The gain of 14 percentage points belongs to <strong>the trained inspector</strong> on these 50 images. Pairing the outcomes also reveals the 10 corrections and 3 new errors. The training agent itself never classified the images, so its own classification accuracy was not measured. To compare two training methods, start them from identical copies of the base model, give them the same compute budget, and evaluate the resulting copies on the same held-out images. ### Worked example 3. How much of the gap between the base and official models was closed?

In Table 1, the base models score 7.5%, the models post-trained by Claude Code with Opus 4.6 score 23.2%, and the providers' official instruction-tuned models score 51.1%. ### Worked example 4. How much probability did the model assign to the same four bytes?

The vertical axis shows BPB, or bits per byte. It measures the <strong>average number of bits needed to predict a byte of validation text</strong>. If the probability assigned to the observed next token is p, its prediction costs −log₂ p bits. A probability of one half costs one bit, one quarter costs two bits, and one eighth costs three. The code sums the negative log probabilities in natural-log units (nats) and divides by the number of bytes and ln 2. It excludes special tokens with zero byte length. [Official evaluation function](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch/blob/228791fb499afffb54b46200aca536f79142f117/prepare.py#L344-L365)

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{BPB}=\frac{-\sum_t\ln p(x_t\mid x_{<t})}{B\ln 2}=\frac{-\sum_t\log_2 p(x_t\mid x_{<t})}{B}.`} display />

In a <strong>toy example</strong>, two tokens together encode B=4 bytes of validation text. Model A assigns the two observed tokens probabilities of 1/2 and 1/8, for a total cost of four bits. Model B assigns the same tokens probabilities of 1/2 and 1/4, for a total cost of three bits.

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{BPB}_A=\frac{1+3}{4}=1.00,\qquad \mathrm{BPB}_B=\frac{1+2}{4}=0.75.`} display />

The models saw the same text. Model B assigned more probability to the second token and saved 0.25 bits per byte. This is why lower values are better on the slide. The default experiment gives each run <strong>five minutes of training</strong> on one GPU. Startup and compilation fall outside that budget. Faster code can complete more training steps in the same time, so the comparison measures what can be achieved on a specified machine within that budget. [Time budget and comparison conditions](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch/blob/228791fb499afffb54b46200aca536f79142f117/README.md#how-it-works)

In the slide title, 83 counts all log entries and 15 counts all rows marked `KEEP`. We can calculate the <strong>fraction kept among all entries</strong> and the <strong>fraction kept among the subsequent entries</strong>. [NTU Part II, page 44](https://speech.ee.ntu.edu.tw/~hylee/ml/ml2026-course-data/self-evolving-agent.pdf#page=44) · [Baseline and log format](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch/blob/228791fb499afffb54b46200aca536f79142f117/program.md)

<Math tex={String.raw`\frac{15}{83}\approx18.1\%,\qquad \frac{15-1}{83-1}=\frac{14}{82}\approx17.1\%.`} display />

The second fraction means that 14 of the subsequent 82 entries were kept. The analysis notebook's separate keep-rate statistic uses `KEEP + DISCARD` as its denominator and excludes crashes. ### Worked example 6. Does the best learning rate for one task also win on two?

Suppose two inspection cameras each need a calibration correction. In this <strong>illustrative example</strong>, the optimum correction θ is zero for both cameras. At the same error, camera B's loss curve has three times the curvature of camera A's. Write that curvature as a, with a=1 for A and a=3 for B. The candidate rules differ only in their learning rate η. Both cameras start at θ₀=2 and get one update.

<Math tex={String.raw`L_a(\theta)=\tfrac12 a\theta^2,\quad \nabla_\theta L_a=a\theta,\quad \theta_1=\theta_0-\eta a\theta_0=2(1-\eta a).`} display />


| Candidate rule | A's new correction → loss | B's new correction → loss | Mean loss |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| η=0.25 | 1.5 → 1.125 | 0.5 → 0.375 | 0.750 |
| η=0.75 | 0.5 → 0.125 | −2.5 → 9.375 | 4.750 |

Try the larger step on B. The starting gradient is 3×2=6. Subtracting 4.5 from 2 takes the correction past the optimum at zero to −2.5. Its loss now exceeds the initial loss of 6.

<Math tex={String.raw`\theta_1=2-0.75\cdot6=-2.5,\qquad L_3(-2.5)=\tfrac12\cdot3\cdot(-2.5)^2=9.375.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`J(0.25)=\frac{1.125+0.375}{2}=0.75,\qquad J(0.75)=\frac{0.125+9.375}{2}=4.75.`} display />


The inner update produces <strong>a new correction θ₁</strong>. The outer comparison returns <strong>a choice of rule</strong>. A alone favors 0.75. Giving the two cameras equal weight favors 0.25. To measure the quality of a learning rule, specify the tasks and the budget it gets on each.

### Worked example 7. How does ShinkaEvolve score both prediction loss and expert imbalance?

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-22-learning-the-parameter-update-rule/shinkaevolve-fig1-program-search.png"
  alt="A parent program is sampled and modified. The pipeline checks novelty and evaluates the candidate before archiving it."
  caption="Lange et al., Figure 1, left panel. Trace candidate generation and evaluation around the cycle."
  source="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19349v1#page=1"
  credit="Robert Tjarko Lange, Yuki Imajuku and Edoardo Cetin / CC BY 4.0"
  label="Supplementary material"
  width={1801} height={1296}
/>

In a mixture of experts (MoE), a router chooses which experts process each token. In the September 2025 ShinkaEvolve paper, <strong>the code under evolution</strong> computed an auxiliary loss that discouraged unbalanced routing during training. The team evaluated candidates on a 556-million-parameter model with 64 experts, activating eight per token. Inner training added 0.01 times the candidate auxiliary loss to next-token cross-entropy (CE). Outer evaluation measured the trained model's CE and its actual routing imbalance. Follow the arrow from evaluation back to the archive in the supplementary figure to locate this second comparison. [ShinkaEvolve, §4.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.19349v1#page=9)

Work through Equations 4–5 in the appendix. <strong>For this example</strong>, use two experts and send each token to one expert. After training with candidate A, 60 of 100 tokens go to one expert and 40 to the other. The final CE is 2.00. For candidate B the counts are 52 and 48, and CE is 2.05.
