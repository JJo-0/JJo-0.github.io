> **Study stage.** A single-center feasibility study that did not randomize patients to compare survival or treatment effects.

## Three meanings of a better test

Genomic testing in childhood cancer is useful only if the result reaches clinicians in time to inform a decision. Ask three separate questions: **How much faster was it? What additional findings did it detect? How did it affect care?** Faster reporting alone cannot establish longer survival.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) looks for variants across the DNA sequence. Distinguishing tumor changes from inherited characteristics requires sample preparation, sequencing, interpretation, and verification. A fast sequencing instrument and a fast clinical report measure different parts of that process.

## When does the three-day clock start?

The authors defined turnaround time (TAT) from the point when all required specimens were ready until **issuance of a clinically interpretable report**. It includes DNA extraction, sample transport, sequencing, and analysis. It starts after the initial suspicion of cancer and excludes waiting for sample collection.

The paper reports mean turnaround of 42 days for conventional GMS-WGS and three days for rapid WGS (rWGS). A simple average of entries with numerical values in the public supplement gives about 44.2 days across 49 GMS records and 3.14 days across 35 rapid records. A separate output table in Apollo version 2 reports stage means for GMS solid tumors (26) and hematologic malignancies (20). Summing stage means and weighting by group size yields 42.0 days for GMS and about 3.14 for rWGS. Matching identifiers between the Excel inputs showed that the GMS analysis file omitted three of the supplement’s numerical entries. Times matched for all 46 shared GMS records and all 35 rapid records. The review identified the omitted entries but could not determine the reason. Missingness, deliberate exclusion, and aggregation error should not be assumed interchangeable. [Apollo v2, Figure_S1_results.txt.]

No rapid turnaround time was recorded for the 19 retrospective cases. The result is therefore not a paired comparison of both times for all 54 patients. This review checked the arithmetic of public group summaries. It did not independently reproduce the sequencing or complete analysis.

By contrast, Figure_S1_script.R in Apollo version 2 sets error=sd_tat and draws bars by adding and subtracting the standard deviation from cumulative stage means. The article’s formula is not the calculation used by that plotting code. The distinction between the paper’s stated method and the public code remains relevant.

The mean and sample standard deviation describe turnaround in the same time units. The count n is the number of observations with turnaround data in the relevant group, not automatically all 54 participants. The dimensionless t critical value is the 97.5th percentile of a t distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom. The interval leaves a combined 5% in the two tails.

The half-width equals the standard deviation times the critical value divided by the square root of the sample size. Two groups with the same mean can have different intervals because their spread or counts differ. An interval for the mean is not a guarantee for an individual patient’s report time or a range containing 95% of patient waits.

The calculation assumes consistent definitions of turnaround time, independent observations in the relevant group, and an appropriate t-based interval for the mean. Severe skew or outliers in a small group need a separate check. Imputing zero days for missing times or combining the mean, standard deviation, and count from different populations changes the calculation. The formula cannot reconcile exclusion criteria or the use of standard deviations in the plotting script.

Check your understanding: Does a mean of three days and its confidence interval show that every report was available within three days? Individual times and uncertainty about the mean are different. Operational assessment also needs the distribution of delays and a record of missing times.

## What is the denominator of the 95% figure?

### Whole-genome tests can differ in depth

The Results report median tumor sequencing depth of 137× for rWGS versus 97× for GMS-WGS. Germline specimens had median depths of 84× and 42×, respectively. Depth describes repeated reads of DNA positions. It does not count how many tests a patient underwent. Data volume and analysis conditions therefore differed. These observations do not isolate how much of the detection difference arose from depth. [Results, paragraph before Figure 1.]

The comparison in the Results is 147/155 for rWGS and 138/155 for GMS-WGS. The denominator is the set of clinically actionable variants identified by multiple methods in this study. It is neither the probability of diagnosing a patient correctly nor the probability of detecting every possible cancer variant. Distinguish the abstract’s broad reference to standard of care from the specific GMS-WGS comparison in the body.

The difference between the counts is also not the number found only by rapid sequencing. Each method could miss findings that the other detects. The variant-level comparison determines overlap and unique calls.

The authors describe eight variants missed by rWGS and 17 reportable variants identified only by rWGS. This explains why subtracting 138 from 147 to get nine does not count newly detected variants. The difference in the sizes of two baskets cannot tell us how many items belong to just one of them. That analogy describes set membership, not the clinical importance of each finding.

“Whole genome” does not mean every type of molecular information was measured. The authors state that WGS did not include DNA methylation analysis, which was performed separately in central nervous system tumors when indicated. Rapid sequencing did not eliminate all other diagnostic tests. [Results, paragraph before Figure 2.]

## Changes in care and benefits to patients

Among 35 prospectively enrolled patients, 17 had documented changes in care. These included changes to the diagnostic pathway or risk classification, access to precision treatment, or avoidance of unnecessary procedures. In the retrospective group, three clinicians judged that nine of 19 people could potentially have benefited had the findings been available at the time. Documented actions and retrospective judgments constitute different evidence.

A management change alone does not show that it improved survival or quality of life. This small, single-center, nonrandomized study did not compare long-term outcomes between randomized groups of similar patients. It cannot establish a survival improvement.

## Verification after the rapid test

The paper reports that findings unique to rWGS were confirmed with another test when clinically indicated or checked manually against the original GMS-WGS data. It would be inaccurate to say that every finding underwent separate confirmatory testing, or that rapid analysis removed the need for verification.

### Check your understanding

Can 147 detected variants out of 155 and care changes in 17 of 35 patients be compared as two versions of the same rate? The former counts variants and the latter counts people. One patient can have multiple variants. Keep analytical detection, clinical use, and long-term patient outcomes separate.

Outstanding questions in the source revision included inclusion and exclusion criteria across turnaround files, the confidence-interval description versus the code’s standard deviations, variant-level supplementary data, and the public protocol. The source marked this private revision as still undergoing editorial review.
