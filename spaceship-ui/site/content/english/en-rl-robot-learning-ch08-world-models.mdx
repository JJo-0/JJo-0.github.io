## What should an action-conditioned model predict?

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={10} caption="Can a robot anticipate consequences before acting?" priority />

Suppose a robot pushes a cup onto a plate. A command alone cannot reveal whether moving the hand another centimeter will settle the cup on the plate or catch the rim and tip it over. A world model predicts what follows from observations so far and proposed actions. These predictions can support both candidate-action selection and policy training.

The SO-101 projects introduced before the technical lecture illustrate how to test a world model. Difficulty increases from straight pushing to moving around obstacles and handling unfamiliar objects. Even when the model predicts motion, reaching the goal and the number of actions used are evaluated through actual execution. [Project introduction](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=444s)

[Original lecture, 12:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=746s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={11} caption="A batter anticipates where the ball will arrive" />

Time separates the ball's illustrated position from the bat's future position. A batter estimates direction and speed from a few observations and allows time to swing. Likewise, a robot may need different commands for a ball at the same image location depending on whether it arrives from the left or right.

Using the lecture's approximate figures, ball travel takes about 400 ms and visual-signal transmission about 100 ms, leaving roughly 300 ms for judgment and swinging. These are lecture approximations. They explain why a batter anticipates arrival from the early trajectory rather than waiting to react after the ball reaches the plate.

[Original lecture, 13:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=805s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={12} caption="A policy chooses an action; a world model predicts its outcome" />

The left arrow maps a current state and goal to an action: given “move the cup onto the plate,” the policy chooses a hand-motion direction. The right maps a current state and candidate action to a next state. Left and right pushes can be supplied separately to compare outcomes for the same cup. A VLA using image history may also learn motion regularities. The distinction here concerns the inputs and outputs provided externally.

The simulator on the left computes next states from specified masses, friction, and contact rules. The model on the right estimates transition patterns from experience pairing observations with robot commands. It can learn difficult-to-model scenes such as folding cloth, but may predict unfamiliar materials or contacts incorrectly. The approaches can also be combined.

If a rigid box's mass and friction are known, a physics engine can quickly test different pushes. Specifying every contact and material interaction becomes harder for wet cloth folding or soft tissue deforming under pressure. A learned model can acquire such changes from image–action pairs, but may also need substantial data to relearn simple motion laws already known analytically. The lecture's choice depends on the phenomena to represent and the available experience.

## Planning through video

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={16} caption="Predict movement rather than repainting every pixel" />

The left camera frame propagates along the orange path toward the right. Finn and colleagues' CDNA model applies multiple transformations to previous images and uses masks to mix them across regions. The robot arm and cup can move differently while the background remains fixed. Motion alone cannot easily reveal the color and shape of newly exposed surfaces absent from the previous frame. [Finn et al., Video Prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07157)

When a cup moves rightward, predicting where existing pixels go makes it easier to preserve wall patterns and table colors than redrawing them every time. Early convolutional LSTMs read spatial features while remembering motion across frames. Multiple transformations and occlusion handling help address newly visible regions. This motion-centered structure helped learn future video from the relatively limited interaction data available then. [Finn et al., action-conditioned video prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07157)

[Original lecture, 20:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1237s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={17} caption="A point selected on an image becomes the robot's goal" />

As in the cloth and plate examples, users can select an image point to move and its destination. Candidate predictions receive lower cost when that point approaches the target. Alternatively, users can supply a desired final image or a classifier score for “drawer closed.” Different definitions of success allow the same world model to plan different tasks. [Ebert et al., Visual Foresight](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00568)

Visual Foresight trains on interaction videos and actual commands collected autonomously by a robot. Predicting next images does not require human task labels such as “move this object there.” At execution, the user selects goal pixels to rank candidate paths.

[Original lecture, 22:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1334s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={18} caption="CEM narrows candidates; MPC checks again after execution" />

The left side shows model training: collect commands and before-and-after images, using the next frame as a target. The right shows execution. The Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) samples action sequences, selects low-predicted-cost elites, and updates the next sampling distribution. Model Predictive Control (MPC) executes only the beginning of the selected sequence, observes the real scene, and replans. CEM searches for candidates; MPC revises plans through observations during execution.

Pixelwise squared error can blur object outlines by averaging two possible locations. Feeding predicted frames into subsequent predictions accumulates mismatch. Computing many frames for every candidate adds cost, motivating later latent-space methods that compress control-relevant information for comparison.

Berkeley CS285 explains that replanning every few steps reduces reliance on each plan's accuracy and allows shorter horizons. If a cup slides less than expected after the first move, the remaining commands can be recalculated from its observed position. [Sergey Levine, CS285 model-based reinforcement-learning slides](https://berkeleydeeprlcourse.github.io/deeprlcourse/static/slides/lec-11.pdf)

<Math display tex={String.raw`a^\star_{t:t+H-1}=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a_{t:t+H-1}}\sum_{k=1}^{H}c(\hat{s}_{t+k},g)`} label="Selecting the action sequence with low predicted cost" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl08-e03" />

Follow the planning procedure: the user selects a point on the cup and a target point. The planner samples several multistep arm-motion sequences and predicts the images each would produce. It retains candidates approaching the target pixel and adjusts the next candidate distribution. This repetition is CEM. Executing only the beginning of a good sequence and restarting from a new photograph is MPC.

If weak contact moves the cup less than expected, the next photograph captures the discrepancy. Executing the entire original long sequence would instead continue commanding against a mistaken cup position. This is why actual observations are incorporated repeatedly. [Visual Foresight](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00568)

[Original lecture, 22:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1363s)

## Imagining in latent space

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={19} caption="Must every prediction redraw the entire screen?" />

More candidates and longer horizons increase the cost of repeatedly generating full images. A short numerical vector can instead retain information important for action selection, such as cup position and motion. This compressed representation is a latent representation. If compression removes whether the cup is grasped, fast computation can still select a poor plan.

[Original lecture, 25:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1513s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={20} caption="Practicing policies in a compressed state space" />

The encoder on the left maps observations to vectors. The dynamics model in the middle predicts vectors and rewards given actions. The policy selects another action and feeds it back into the dynamics model. The lower loop improves the policy through these predicted experiences. Distinguishing real environment data for model learning from model-generated data for policy learning reveals the figure's two paths.

A <strong>latent code</strong> compresses an image into numbers useful for control. Representing cup and arm positions without every background pixel makes candidate comparison cheaper. But losing a small cup handle or a narrow pre-contact gap can harm grasping even when reconstruction looks plausible. Evaluate visual fidelity and actual control success separately.

[Original lecture, 25:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1546s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={21} caption="V sees, M remembers, and C chooses actions" />

In Ha and Schmidhuber's figure, V compresses the current frame, M remembers previous scenes and actions, and C selects actions from both current vision and memory. Remembering which way a road curved can guide steering even after the bend leaves view. [Ha and Schmidhuber, World Models](https://worldmodels.github.io/)

[Original lecture, 27:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1625s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={22} caption="Reconstruction reveals compression's gains and losses" />

The game frame on the left is compressed by a VAE encoder and reconstructed by its decoder on the right. Corridors and bright objects remain while surface textures blur. This may suffice for avoiding walls but fail if a small wall sign marks the destination. Reconstruction quality and preservation of task-relevant information need separate evaluation.

[Original lecture, 27:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1658s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={23} caption="Do not collapse alternative futures into one mean" />

The RNN carries memory forward. The mixture density network (MDN) above it represents the next latent-value distribution with multiple Gaussian components. If a ball can bounce to either side of an obstacle, a mean-only prediction may send it through the middle. Retaining probability near both outcomes lets those futures be evaluated separately.

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={24} caption="CMA-ES tests candidate policy weights" />

CMA-ES samples controller weights, runs an episode with each controller, and adjusts the sampling mean and covariance to favor higher-reward candidates in the next generation. Like CEM on P018, it evaluates candidates, but optimizes reusable controller weights rather than an immediate command sequence. The original paper uses the original environment or a learned dream environment depending on the task.

The <strong>controller C</strong> can remain small because V and M already represent scenes and temporal changes. C has less need, for example, to estimate speed anew from every frame. The original paper tests candidate C weights with the evolutionary strategy CMA-ES, compares cumulative dream-environment scores, and improves the next candidate population. This optimization is separate from training M to predict next latent states. [Ha and Schmidhuber, World Models](https://worldmodels.github.io/)

[Original lecture, 29:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1774s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={25} caption="Why do corners become harder without memory?" />

The right figure examines memory's role. Even with only part of the road visible, earlier frames showing the bend provide steering evidence. Removing a module and measuring performance change on the same task is an ablation study. These results need not generalize unchanged to other robots whose observations already contain sufficient memory-related information.

[Original lecture, 30:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1858s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={26} caption="Test a dream-trained policy in the original environment" />

The left shows a Doom-style environment and the right CarRacing. In the original Doom experiment, a controller was trained inside the learned environment and then evaluated in the original game. Exploiting flaws missed by the dream model can reduce performance in the original game. The authors varied dream-environment sampling temperature to avoid excessively confident predictions.

[Original lecture, 31:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=1904s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={27} caption="One step's error becomes the next step's input" />

The upper path receives encoded actual frames, while the lower path feeds back model-generated latent values. If a predicted cup drifts sideways, the next prediction starts from that incorrect position. The earlier MDN-RNN already has a transition distribution for sampling futures. The RSSM introduced next separately learns an estimate before observation and an observation-informed estimate for the same time step.

## PlaNet and Dreamer

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={28} caption="RSSM learns predictions alongside observation-informed estimates" />

The orange RNN path carries memory from prior latent states and actions. The green prior estimates before seeing the new image; the purple posterior estimates after seeing it. Remembering that the hand reached forward does not reveal whether grasping succeeded. A photograph showing the lifted cup updates the estimate. Training brings the distributions closer while also explaining observations and rewards. [Hafner et al., PlaNet](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04551)

<Math display tex={String.raw`h_t=f_\phi(h_{t-1},z_{t-1},a_{t-1}),\qquad p_\phi(z_t\mid h_t),\quad q_\phi(z_t\mid h_t,o_t)`} label="RSSM prediction and observation correction" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl08-e05" />

Read the <strong>prior</strong> and <strong>posterior</strong> through cup grasping. A history showing the arm rising does not establish that it lifted the cup. The history-only prediction is the prior. If a new photograph shows the cup still on the table, the observation-informed posterior shifts toward a failed grasp. Training fits reconstructions and rewards while reducing KL between these distributions, teaching plausible history-based state samples even when future images are unavailable during imagination.

The RSSM's deterministic memory path carries history continuously, while stochastic latent variables represent alternative current situations consistent with it. A deterministic RNN path does not eliminate all noise: errors in predicted and sampled inputs still affect it.

[Original lecture, 34:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2076s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={29} caption="Feedback inside imagination versus feedback from the real environment" />

In the lower loop, the policy reads a predicted state to select an action and the model predicts again from that action. This loop closes within imagination. Without new real observations, future latents are sampled from the prior. During real execution, sensors correct the state estimate. Distinguishing these loops explains why RSSMs still accumulate error over long imagined trajectories.

Although a fixed function updates RSSM hidden state, its previous latent input already contains sampling and prediction errors. Training the prior against an actual-observation-informed posterior reduces the imagination–observation gap. Long imagined paths still need checking against real observations.

[Original lecture, 36:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2187s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={30} caption="Dreamer moves repeated planning computation into training" />

PlaNet on the left searches latent-space action sequences with CEM at every new observation. Dreamer on the right trains an actor to select actions and a critic to evaluate future rewards from imagined trajectories. Calling the actor at execution reduces repeated large candidate searches. But the actor may learn actions overvalued by the model, making real-environment validation important. [Hafner et al., Dream to Control](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01603)

<Math display tex={String.raw`\hat{G}_t=\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^k\hat{r}_{t+k}`} label="Discounted rewards along an imagined trajectory" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl08-e06" />

Dreamer first updates a world model to explain images and rewards from real trajectories. It then starts imagined trajectories from latent states of real experience. Model weights are held fixed during this policy update, while the critic estimates cumulative future rewards. Across overall training, fresh real experience continues updating the world model. This differs from permanently freezing a model after its initial creation. [Dreamer V1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01603)

[Original lecture, 37:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2270s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={31} caption="DayDreamer applies the approach to real robots with sensors and motors" />

The four photographs show A1 quadruped locomotion, UR5 object transfer, XArm grasping, and Sphero navigation. Learning to stand and walk from about an hour of real quadruped experience and the arm-manipulation experiments are separate tasks. Motor control and learning must run concurrently on physical robots, so the authors execute collection and training asynchronously. [Wu et al., DayDreamer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14176)

## Large-scale video and robot actions

Another V4 change highlighted in the lecture replaces fixed-size recurrent memory with Transformer context and a KV cache. A KV cache stores keys and values of previously read tokens for reuse in later predictions, so retained information depends on context length and storage cost. Flow matching learns changes in video latents, while action-labeled data connect them to specific commands. Keep action-free pretraining, action conditioning, and imagined policy improvement separate rather than treating them as one training stage. [Dreamer V4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24527)

The Dreamer V4 paper presents a Minecraft diamond task requiring more than 20,000 mouse and keyboard actions from pixel inputs. Intermediate resources and tools must be obtained in sequence over this long task. This length differs from the internal number of flow-integration steps generating one video. [Original Dreamer V4 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24527)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={35} caption="Extending game-specific latent representations through Internet video" />

A game limits the objects and camera movements frequently encountered. Internet video offers more varied objects and motion, but also varied viewpoints, backgrounds, and purposes. This motivates large video models. Useful robot representations must connect Internet-video patterns to robot viewpoints and manipulation data.

[Original lecture, 45:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2715s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={36} caption="Why can ten seconds of video contain 51,200 tokens?" />

Each grid cell is a patch. A 256×256 image divided into 16×16-pixel patches has sixteen patches along each axis. At twenty frames per second for ten seconds, those 256 patches repeat across 200 frames. Standard dense attention compares all token pairs, so doubling tokens roughly quadruples comparison pairs.

Stanford CS231n's ViT explanation projects each patch into a vector for Transformer input. Repeating this per video frame multiplies patches per image by frame count, as below. [Justin Johnson, CS231n, 1:04:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RQowiOF_FvQ&t=3884s) · [Lecture slides](https://cs231n.stanford.edu/slides/2025/lecture_8.pdf)

<Math display tex={String.raw`N=\left(\frac{256}{16}\right)^2(20\cdot10)=51{,}200`} label="Multiply patches per frame by frame count" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl08-e08" />

[Original lecture, 47:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2821s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={37} caption="Compress space and time while allocating tokens to important regions" />

The first column groups neighboring locations spatially, the second groups change across frames temporally, and the third allocates tokens adaptively by scene. A stationary wall may tolerate strong compression, but coarsening a finger's first contact with a cup handle can erase contact timing. The slide's compression numbers illustrate principles.

Using future frames for temporal compression is convenient for recorded video, but real-time control cannot use frames that have not arrived. Commands must be based on tokens formed from frames available so far. Static backgrounds and rapidly changing contact scenes also need not receive equal token budgets.

Three axes clarify compression. <strong>Spatial compression</strong> groups neighboring pixels within a frame. <strong>Temporal compression</strong> groups repeated information across frames. <strong>Adaptive compression</strong> aims to allocate fewer tokens to stationary walls and more to changing hands and objects in contact. These choices can be combined, but token-count calculations must separately account for both spatial axes and time.

The lecture's large compression factors are conceptual approximations. Actual token counts require input resolution, temporal sampling, latent-grid sizes, and token definitions. A label of “8× compression” alone does not support comparing context lengths across models.

[Original lecture, 48:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=2910s)

Consider a hypothetical eightfold reduction along width, height, and time. A 256×256-pixel, 200-frame input becomes a 32×32×25 latent grid with 25,600 positions. That is 1/512 of the original 13,107,200 pixel positions, but only half the previous page's 51,200 patch tokens. Applying pixel-based compression again to an already patched token count is incorrect. This educational calculation treats each latent position as one token and does not claim an actual Cosmos configuration's output length. The transcript's “about 100” cannot be derived from these factors alone without input resolution, latent-grid, and further patching details.

| WAM: DreamZero example | Observations, instructions, etc. | Future video and corresponding actions | Execute jointly generated action chunks |
| VAM: mimic-video example | Video features conditioned on observations/instructions | Commands from an action decoder | Translate visual plans into control values |

WAM means World Action Model and VAM Video Action Model. Later in the lecture, both approaches are sometimes grouped broadly together. Following whether actions enter or leave the model, and whether a separate decoder is used, is more informative than names alone.

Video pretraining can expose a model to many scenes of objects rolling or following a hand. Execution may reuse only the learned features. Separate video-training cost from the latency of issuing one motor command.

[Original lecture, 55:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3315s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={40} caption="Static-image meaning differs from the rules of motion" />

An image and caption can identify a carrot and knife. Consecutive frames more directly reveal pressing order, direction, and where cut pieces move. The lecture's VLA comparison concerns transferring VLMs pretrained mainly on static images and text to robots. If a model already includes video or robot experience, first examine its actual training data.

[Original lecture, 55:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3341s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={41} caption="Learn motion from video and commands from robot data" />

Video–text inputs usually do not include robot joint angles or motor torques. They can still teach temporal cues from objects following hands. Robot-specific actions require data pairing camera observations with commands. Human hand motions therefore cannot simply be copied into robot motor commands.

[Original lecture, 57:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3435s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={42} caption="mimic-video attaches an action decoder to video features" />

The upper path adapts a VLM to robot actions; the lower uses a generative video backbone. In mimic-video, an action decoder produces robot commands corresponding to anticipated visual changes. Inferring actions from resulting changes makes it an inverse dynamics model (IDM). Several joint motions can produce the same visual change, and training data influence which command is chosen. [Pai et al., mimic-video](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15692)

[Original lecture, 58:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3505s)

The graph separates predicted-video inputs from expert-video inputs. Expert video provides an experimental reference containing outcomes unavailable in advance during deployment. Higher success with this input indicates room to improve video prediction. Failures remaining with expert video also require examining action decoding and robot execution. Since the copied figure's bars are not visible, the lecture explanation and paper supplement the comparison and interpretation. [mimic-video](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15692)

The paper's oracle condition supplies latent representations extracted from actual future frames. Near-perfect success under that condition indicates substantial room to improve predictions in that comparison. [mimic-video paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15692)

[Original lecture, 59:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3583s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={44} caption="Use intermediate denoiser features rather than completed video" />

The pink language model handles instructions, while the blue video model handles current images and partially refined future representations. Even noisy intermediate features can contain object-motion and task-direction information. The decoder generates action chunks from them, reducing the need to render visually polished video in the normal control path.

Features from mimic-video's Cosmos-Predict2 can be extracted before reconstructing final RGB frames. How far video denoising proceeds before feature extraction and how many refinement steps generate actions are controlled separately. [mimic-video paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15692)

Video and actions generated through <strong>flow matching</strong> each have their own refinement progress. Video features can indicate where to move an object before final frames are complete. mimic-video conditions action generation on these intermediate features. Adjusting video and action refinement separately allocates computation according to action-generation needs rather than visual polish. [mimic-video paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15692)

The scenes may look similar, but the left results from motor commands and the right from video generation. The slide states that video rendering is omitted during ordinary policy inference. [Original lecture, 63:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3790s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={48} caption="DreamZero jointly predicts video and actions within one model" />

The left diagram adds noise to both video and actions and learns both outputs jointly. On the right, the robot executes an action chunk, receives actual frames, and generates the next chunk. A KV cache stores attention information from earlier frames to reduce recomputation. [Ye et al., DreamZero](https://dreamzero0.github.io/DreamZero.pdf)

For example, the predicted gripper may hold the cup while the actual camera shows it still on the table. Updating the next chunk's context with real frames creates an opportunity to approach again instead of extending the mistaken prediction.

DreamZero trains with actual past context and jointly predicts noise in future video and actions. <strong>Teacher forcing</strong> supplies correct preceding context during training. At inference, it executes a generated chunk and receives fresh observations before the next one. Replacing anticipated scenes with actual scenes reduces compounding error from continuing to assume an imagined cup was grasped.

A KV cache stores keys and values for processed context to reduce recalculation. Real-time operation of large models also requires system design such as cache management and fewer generation steps. Future-video quality alone does not determine actual control speed. [DreamZero paper](https://dreamzero0.github.io/DreamZero.pdf)

[Original lecture, 63:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=3804s)

The lecture emphasizes implementation choices enabling real control rates even with DreamZero's 14-billion-parameter model. Parameter count alone does not guarantee the same speed on another GPU or robot. Examine chunk-generation latency, executed chunk length, and how frequently actual observations update the cache together.

The photographs show moving apples, folding shirts, untying cords, and drawing circles. Familiar scenes can still require different action orders and contacts. Zero-shot means no additional training on that evaluation task; few-shot adaptation adjusts the model with limited additional data. This table groups DreamZero and mimic-video together opposite Dreamer-style methods. P038 separated DreamZero as WAM and mimic-video as VAM. Despite changing categories, compare whether candidate commands can be varied to test outcomes, whether goals directly produce commands, and where robot data are required.

Action-conditioned models can learn what followed each action from failures, successes, and free play alike. Combined with learned reward models, they can support imagined reinforcement learning. Goal-conditioned action models may need annotations linking instructions to relevant segments. General video backbones can first learn motion from Internet video without robot commands, but connecting it to physical commands still requires robot data.

Cross-robot transfer can share video representations while adapting action decoders to equipment. Action-conditioned models likewise need ways to align different command semantics. Both evaluations must explain handling of robot-specific cameras and control units.

## Prediction without drawing pixels

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={51} caption="Must predicting contact also redraw the background?" />

Determining whether a cup is lifted does not require perfectly predicting wall textures. But losing the hand–cup gap during compression can ruin a grasp plan. This motivates JEPA, shifting prediction targets from pixels to task-relevant representations.

[Original lecture, 68:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=4087s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={52} caption="JEPA predicts future-frame representations rather than future images" />

The upper predictor estimates the next representation from the current representation and action; the lower encoder converts the actual next frame into a vector during training. Comparing them avoids RGB reconstruction. If both paths map every scene to the same vector, error can be small but planning impossible: representation collapse. The three boxes illustrate slowly updated target encoders, pretrained frozen encoders, and distribution-spreading regularization. Their retained information and guarantees differ. [V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09985) · [DINO-WM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04983) · [LeWorldModel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19312)

<Math display tex={String.raw`\hat{z}_{t+1}=f_\phi(z_t,a_t),\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\|\hat{z}_{t+1}-z_{t+1}\|_2^2`} label="Prediction error in future-scene embedding space" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl08-e09" />

To picture collapse numerically, map both a lifted-cup image and an empty-table image to the same vector and have the predictor output only that vector. Prediction loss can be small without retaining grasp-success information. V-JEPA uses training designs including slowly changing target encoders. DINO-WM freezes pretrained DINO features and predicts future features. LeWorldModel suppresses constant solutions through Gaussian regularization spreading latent distributions. EMA alone does not automatically solve every representation-collapse problem.

In the learned representation space, action sequences can be selected to reduce distance between predicted future and goal-image features. To place a cup on a shelf, check whether predictions approach the goal, execute the first action, and update the state from the real camera. Final evaluation counts actual placement as well as representation error. [V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.09985) · [DINO-WM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04983) · [LeWorldModel](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19312)

[Original lecture, 68:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=4130s)

<LectureSlide lecture={8} page={53} caption="Compare pixel and latent approaches through the same cup-transfer task" />

Pixel models predict where the cup moves in an image; Dreamer-style models evaluate multistep rewards or actions in compressed states. DreamZero jointly produces future video and commands, while mimic-video derives commands from video features. JEPA-based planning can compare predicted futures and goals as vectors. In every architecture, fresh observations after actual robot motion connect prediction with reality.

[Original lecture, 71:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cTTmUZlOF2s&t=4272s)

## Try the executable example

Suppose a virtual robot starts at position 0 with goal 1. Its model predicts that command 1 moves it by 1, but the example's actual system moves only 0.8. Following the initial plan leaves it at 0.8. Observing the new position and commanding the remaining 0.2 brings it to 0.96; repeating once more reaches 0.992.

This example demonstrates the effect of observation-based replanning without changing the model. It uses only the Python standard library and includes neither a physical robot nor Dreamer training.
