## 1. In one sentence

**Can lithium ions move quickly through a solid while its interface with an electrode resists continued degradation at high voltage? This study changes both the electrolyte’s composition and its atomic arrangement to address those two problems, and tests the result in laboratory cells held under high pressure.**

The authors call the design non-equimolar cation–anion amorphous engineering. They introduce positively and negatively charged constituents in unequal molar proportions to create a structure without a regularly repeating atomic arrangement. The composition, ionic conductivity and interface measurements below explain how the design works. The 10C cycling result needs to be read with the test conditions, including 50 MPa of pressure and a Li–In anode.

## 2. Why it is difficult

All-solid-state batteries use a nonflammable solid electrolyte, but ions can encounter obstacles inside it and at the electrode interface. Halide electrolytes may be more compatible with high-voltage cathodes than sulfides, yet conventional crystalline Li₂ZrCl₆ generally conducts below 1 mS/cm at room temperature. Fluorine substitution can improve oxidative stability at the cost of conductivity. The challenge is to maintain fast transport while limiting decomposition at high voltage.

## 3. Basic concepts

An electrolyte provides a path for lithium ions between electrodes. An interface is the boundary where electrode and electrolyte meet. Self-passivation means the reaction products at that boundary form a protective layer that slows further reaction. Some decomposition can occur.

The C in 10C expresses current relative to cell capacity. It is distinct from degrees Celsius. Interpreting a high-rate cycling measurement as a six-minute full charge for a finished battery would require the delivered capacity, voltage window, electrode loading, pressure and other test conditions.

Ionic conductivity is calculated from pellet thickness <Math tex={"L"} />, resistance <Math tex={"R"} /> and contact area <Math tex={"A"} />.

<Math display tex={"\\sigma=\\frac{L}{R A}"} />

With length L in cm, area A in cm² and resistance R in Ω, the result is in S/cm. Even the same material gives a different measured resistance when it is thicker or the contact area is smaller. Conductivity accounts for that geometry. The authors read electrolyte resistance directly from the high-frequency real-axis intercept of the Nyquist plot rather than fitting an equivalent circuit. Conductivity describes how readily Li⁺ passes through the material. Cell energy density, safety and manufacturing yield require their own measurements.

## 4. Limits of earlier approaches

### Room-temperature conductivity and the barrier to ion motion

Supplementary Figure 2 shows impedance curves from 25 to 85°C. Figure 3 compares the temperature dependence of conduction. Impedance is an electrical response that varies with frequency. These data allow the researchers to read resistance. A cycling-lifetime curve would measure a different property.

In Figure 3a the horizontal axis is reciprocal absolute temperature, so moving right means moving to colder conditions. The vertical axis is the natural logarithm of conductivity multiplied by temperature. Interpreting the slope as a transport activation energy requires those exact definitions. A plot of log conductivity alone uses a different expression. Figure 3b places conductivity and activation energy on separate vertical axes. Even a composition that performs well on both still requires cell-level checks of its interfaces, electrode loading and pressure. The figure covers only the tested temperature range.

Grain boundaries and a limited supply of vacant sites hinder rapid cycling in crystalline halides. Cation doping adjusts the Li⁺-to-vacancy ratio and migration pathways, but metal–chlorine bonds remain vulnerable to oxidation at high voltage. Anion substitution can protect the interface, yet excessively strong bonds can slow Li⁺ transport. The LZC reference conducted at about 0.4 mS/cm and retained only 25.8% of its initial capacity after 40 cycles at 1C with a corrected upper cathode potential of 4.8 V.

## 5. The three stages of the design

First, the team introduced Ta⁵⁺ and O²⁻ into Li₂ZrCl₆ in unequal molar proportions to form Li₂Zr₀.₆₂₅Ta₀.₃Cl₄.₂O₀.₉, designated LZTCO0.9. Second, a disordered local Cl/O environment with bridging and nonbridging oxygen and fewer grain boundaries opened paths for Li⁺. Third, the authors interpret Ta–O bonds and oxygen-containing decomposition products as contributing to a protective cathode–electrolyte interphase (CEI) that localizes the reaction. Changes in current and impedance and surface measurements after cycling support this interpretation. They do not directly establish an absolute film thickness or a single cause of the lifetime improvement. The proposed mechanism is kinetic stabilization, where limited decomposition slows further decomposition. The intrinsic thermodynamic stability window need not extend to 4.8 V.

## 6. The cell and its voltage reference

The cell stack was `sc-NCM88 cathode composite → LZTCO0.9 pellet → Li₆PS₅Cl buffer → Li–In anode`. The cathode composite mixed NCM88 and the electrolyte in a 70:30 mass ratio without added carbon or binder. The actual upper cell voltage of 4.18 V converts to a cathode potential of 4.80 V vs Li⁺/Li after correcting for the Li–In equilibrium potential of 0.62 V.

<Math display tex={"E_{\\mathrm{cathode}}=V_{\\mathrm{cell}}+0.62\\,\\mathrm{V}"} />

This equation restates the paper’s reference-electrode correction. Its left-hand side is the cathode potential relative to lithium. The first term on the right is the measured cell voltage between the cathode and Li–In anode. Both use volts but have different references. The 4.8 V value therefore specifies the cathode potential, not the terminal voltage of a finished battery.

## 7. Experimental results

Room-temperature ionic conductivity was 4.11 mS/cm for LZTCO0.9, about ten times the roughly 0.4 mS/cm of reference LZC. At 2.8–4.6 V relative to lithium and 10C, the cell retained 83.2% after 4,000 cycles with 4.58 mg/cm² NCM88 at 2,200 mA/g. At 2.8–4.8 V, 4.46 mg/cm² and 2,400 mA/g, three cells retained 80.4%, 81.2% and 83.9% after 2,000 cycles at 10C, averaging 81.8 ± 1.8% (n = 3). At 1C, LZTCO0.9 retained 82.6% after 200 cycles, while LZC retained 25.8% after 40. Interfacial resistance increased from about 25 to 37 Ω for LZTCO0.9 and from 120 to 207 Ω for LZC.

A higher-loading comparison used 24 mg/cm² of NCM88 and 5.76 mAh/cm², retaining 90.2% after 100 cycles at 0.5C. A separate pouch cell retained 95.5% after 200 cycles at 25°C and 30 MPa, tested at 0.33C and 2.8–4.3 V relative to lithium. Its discharge capacity was approximately 44–45 mAh with 13.18 mg/cm² of NCM88. Those conditions differ from the 4.8 V, 10C pellet cell. The long-duration 10C result at 4.46 mg/cm² corresponds to about 1.0 mAh/cm² and does not establish commercial energy density.

## 8. Counterevidence

The authors explicitly distinguish the 4.8 V result from expanding the intrinsic oxidative stability window to that voltage. The reported kinetic stability applies where the composition, pressure and interface support passivation. Excess oxygen, as in the y = 1.0 composition, produced a LiCl impurity and impaired ion transport. The same strategy can fail outside the optimum composition.

## 9. Limitations

The cells were assembled in an argon glovebox. The main pellet cells cycled in an environmental chamber at 25 ± 0.5°C, under sustained pressure of 50 MPa except in the separate pressure comparison. They used a Li–In anode and a Li₆PS₅Cl buffer instead of lithium metal. The pouch cell also required sustained pressure of 30 MPa. The long high-rate test used three cells. The authors say the figure for each experiment shows the cell closest to the mean. This review checked the repeat curves in Supplementary Figure 30 and their reported spread. Reanalysis of raw data, humidity endurance, manufacturing yield at scale, thermal-runaway safety, full-size pouch energy density and independent replication remain unverified here.

### How should the pressure graph be read?

Supplementary Figure 27 plots cycle count horizontally and specific capacity on the left vertical axis. At 1C and 2.8–4.8 V relative to lithium, retention after 200 cycles was 73.1% at 25 MPa, 82.6% at 50 MPa and 77.1% at 75 MPa. **Of the three pressures tested, 50 MPa performed best.** The result neither demonstrates that more pressure always helps nor establishes an optimum for all solid-state batteries.

The 10C repeat test over 2,000 cycles in Supplementary Figure 30 is a separate comparison. It shows similar retention for three cells. Combining it with the 1C pressure data would not establish performance across all conditions.

**Check your understanding.** Did increasing pressure from 50 to 75 MPa improve retention? In this trial it fell. Compare at equal current, voltage and cycle count before attributing a result to pressure. See the original plots in Supplementary Figures 27 and 30.

### What dry-room exposure establishes

Supplementary Figure 8 compares dry-room exposure for samples prepared with different oxygen sources and the LZC reference. The horizontal axis is exposure time and the vertical axis ionic conductivity. For LZTCO0.9 prepared with 0.9 Li₂O, conductivity fell from 4.11 to 2.49 mS/cm after six hours. The LZC reference fell from 0.40 to 0.11 mS/cm. High initial conductivity and stability during processing require separate measurements.

This figure cannot establish long-term stability in ambient air or humid environments. The caption reviewed here does not specify relative humidity, the number of repeats or what the error bars represent. Short-term dry-room conductivity, sealed-cell cycle life and environmental durability of a product are three distinct tests.

## 10. Practical implications

### What did the interface measurements count?

Supplementary Figures 32 and 33 examine the cathode composite from its surface inward after cycling. In Figure 32, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measures electron binding-energy signals from the constituent elements. In Figure 33, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) measures ion signals while sputtering material from the surface. Equal curve heights across the techniques do not indicate equal amounts of material.

Figure 33 distinguishes cycled reference LZC from the new electrolyte after its first charge, first discharge and prolonged cycling. The horizontal axis is sputtering time in seconds. The vertical axis is a signal normalized to total secondary-ion counts at each time point. The caption calls this a semiquantitative comparison. The curves track relative signal changes under the measurement conditions. An absolute concentration needs additional calibration.

**Check your understanding.** Does a smaller signal at 100 seconds tell us exactly how many nanometers thinner the protective layer has become? That requires calibration of sputtering rate and material-specific signal sensitivity. These profiles contribute evidence about interface reactions alongside electrochemical and structural analyses. On their own they cannot establish layer thickness, absolute composition or product safety.

The study links conductivity and high-voltage durability through a mixed-anion amorphous structure and a self-limiting CEI. The evidence connects reference LZC, changes in composition y and pressure, impedance, post-mortem XANES and ToF-SIMS, and a pouch-cell trial. It establishes laboratory testing of a materials candidate. Industrial deployment remains a separate milestone.

## 11. When to revisit

Update the assessment after independent replication, long high-rate tests at 5 mAh/cm² or above, tests at 10 MPa or below, a lithium-metal or other practical anode, broader temperature and humidity coverage, Wh/L and Wh/kg measurements and safety tests, reanalysis of supplementary and raw data, or pilot-scale manufacturing yield.
