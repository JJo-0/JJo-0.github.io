Let us move beyond evaluating actions and directly change the probabilities of actions that produce good outcomes. This chapter explains how to obtain policy gradients and what makes those gradients useful in practical robot learning.

[Oier Mees · ETH Zurich 2026, Lecture 5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE) · [Official lecture](https://cvg.ethz.ch/lectures/Robot-Learning/) · [Original PDF](https://cvg.ethz.ch/lectures/Robot-Learning/lectures/lecture5_rl_II.pdf)

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={1} caption="Policy gradients directly change the probabilities of good actions" priority />

Imagine a robot reaching toward a cup in a slightly different direction each time. What if it chose movements that happened to grasp the cup successfully more often, and movements that knocked it over less often? This chapter turns that intuition into derivatives with respect to neural-network weights.

In [Chapter 04](/en/posts/rl-robot-learning-ch04-value-learning/), we learned a <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> function that scores actions and selected high-scoring actions. Here, we adjust the <strong>policy</strong> itself. A policy is a rule for choosing actions from states; a stochastic policy provides a probability or probability density for each possible action. Improving a policy means changing that distribution toward better outcomes.

Starting with the derivation of REINFORCE, we refine noisy learning signals with baselines and advantages, examine the conditions for reusing past data, and continue to PPO and SAC. Even algorithms sharing an actor–critic structure differ in how they collect data and which objective they optimize.

[Original lecture from 0:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=0s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={2} caption="The algorithm table compares information requirements and reusable data" />

The table's four columns cover exact computation, learning small tables, deep reinforcement learning for discrete actions, and deep reinforcement learning for continuous actions. Reading across each row reveals their information requirements. A dynamics model describes how the state changes after an action. Value iteration with a known model starts from different information than Q-learning based on stored experience.

<strong>Discrete actions</strong> are enumerable choices such as left and right; <strong>continuous actions</strong> choose real values such as joint target angles or torques. High-dimensional image inputs and continuous actions are separate properties. A policy choosing one of four directions from an image has a large input but discrete actions.

A replay buffer stores past states, actions, rewards, and next states. Q-learning methods can use past actions in updates even when those actions differ from the current policy. SARSA and basic policy gradients are more directly tied to the data distribution generated by the current policy. The table's convergence marks rely on conditions such as a small finite MDP, sufficient visitation, and suitable learning rates. Replacing a table with a neural network does not automatically preserve those guarantees.

DDPG already has an actor, so it would be inaccurate to say that Chapter 04 contains only values and Chapter 05 introduces policy networks for the first time. We revisit the relationship here through stochastic-policy gradients and the problem of estimating them.

[Original lecture from 0:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=5s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={3} caption="Evaluate actions before choosing, or learn selection probabilities directly?" />

Value-based methods first ask, “How much will I receive in the future if I take this action?” Policy gradients ask, “Which policy weights should change to make actions that produced good outcomes more likely?” These approaches can compete, or they can be combined in actor–critic methods.

Weights <Math tex={String.raw`\theta`} /> collect the neural network's adjustable parameters into a vector. The distribution <Math tex={String.raw`\pi_\theta(a\mid s)`} /> selects action <Math tex={String.raw`a`} /> in state <Math tex={String.raw`s`} />. A logit is a score before conversion to a probability. For example, logits of 0 and 1 for two actions become probabilities of approximately 0.269 and 0.731 under softmax: exponentiate each score and divide by their sum. For continuous actions, a network can output a mean and standard deviation defining a Gaussian distribution. A Gaussian concentrates values in a bell shape around its mean, with the standard deviation controlling its spread.

Learning a policy directly does not remove the need to evaluate future outcomes. Actual rewards or a critic still have to indicate which actions were better. Stochastic behavior alone also guarantees neither safe exploration nor sufficient experience.

[Original lecture from 1:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=90s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={4} caption="Improve expected return across repeated runs, rather than one lucky outcome" />

A <strong>reward</strong> is an evaluation signal at one time step; a <strong>return</strong> combines rewards across multiple time steps. If approaching a cup gives small rewards and a successful grasp gives a large reward, the return from one run adds those signals. The discount factor determines how much distant future rewards contribute.

<Math display tex={String.raw`G(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_t,\qquad J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[G(\tau)]`} label="Return and expected return" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e01" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`\tau`} /> is a trajectory covering an entire run, <Math tex={String.raw`p_\theta`} /> is the trajectory distribution generated by the current policy, and <Math tex={String.raw`J(\theta)`} /> is its average return. The branching lines on the right indicate different outcomes from the same starting point, caused by random policy choices and environment uncertainty. We must consider not just the best path but also how likely each path is.

To keep the derivation readable, the main text uses a finite horizon <Math tex={String.raw`T`} /> and an undiscounted return. Differentiating a discounted objective exactly requires consistent time-dependent discount weights. If the objective is defined as <Math tex={String.raw`G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}r_k`} />, the gradient term at the corresponding time also carries <Math tex={String.raw`\gamma^t`} />. Merely changing the reward-sum notation does not justify mixing these two objectives.

[Original lecture from 2:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=160s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={5} caption="Trajectory probability multiplies the initial state, actions, and transitions" />

A trajectory records states and actions in time order, as in <Math tex={String.raw`\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\ldots,s_T)`} />. Sample an initial state, let the policy select an action, let the environment produce the next state, and repeat.

<Math display tex={String.raw`p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)`} label="Factoring trajectory probability" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e02" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`\rho_0`} /> is the initial-state distribution and <Math tex={String.raw`P`} /> the environment transition. The terms multiply because every step must occur to form the trajectory. Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum, which later lets us calculate gradients for long runs.

The environment remains part of this probability even in model-free policy gradients. We assume, however, that changing policy weights does not directly change the initial distribution or environment laws. Their direct derivatives with respect to those weights therefore disappear later. This does not ignore the policy's effect on the state distribution: that effect is already contained in the full trajectory distribution.

[Original lecture from 3:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=200s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={6} caption="Monte Carlo evaluation averages the outcomes of sampled runs" />

Run the robot several times under the same policy and calculate each return to estimate the policy's average performance. Monte Carlo methods use random samples to estimate averages or integrals. We evaluate actual outcomes instead of trying to judge the policy by inspecting its network weights.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\widehat J(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N G(\tau_i),\qquad \tau_i\sim p_\theta`} label="Monte Carlo estimate of expected return" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e03" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`N`} /> is the number of trajectories. Counting successes in ten runs and including each trajectory's energy costs both fit this framework. More samples usually make estimates more stable, but data collected consecutively on one robot can be correlated and may not adequately represent all initial conditions.

When taking an average, the policy that collected the data matters. Simply mixing trajectories from yesterday's different policy into an expression evaluating today's policy creates a mismatch between the desired average and the actual sample distribution. Importance sampling, introduced later, attempts to correct that difference.

[Original lecture from 3:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=227s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={7} caption="Must differentiating rewards with respect to weights pass through the environment?" />

A derivative describes the direction and magnitude of an objective's change under a small weight change. Moving weights along that gradient is gradient ascent. In ordinary supervised learning, we can backpropagate through the operations connecting the network output to the loss.

A robot moving into contact with a cup, followed by the cup slipping and the grasp failing, is not a chain consisting only of differentiable neural-network operations. Contacts may be discontinuous, and the real world's transition rules are unknown. Yet we can still obtain a gradient for improving the policy. Instead of differentiating the numerical reward with respect to the action, we differentiate the probability of producing the action that earned it.

To do so, write expected return as an integral of trajectory probability times return. Exchanging differentiation and integration requires appropriate differentiability and integrability conditions. Linearity of integration alone does not make the exchange valid in every case.

[Original lecture from 5:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=337s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={8} caption="The log-derivative identity separates what we differentiate from what we sample" />

The derivative of a positive function's logarithm equals the function's derivative divided by the function. Multiplying both sides by the original probability density yields the following identity.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)=p_\theta(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)`} label="The log-derivative identity" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e04" />

Substituting it into the derivative of expected return gives an expectation that can be estimated with samples from the trajectory distribution.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p_\theta}[G(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)]`} label="The score-function policy gradient" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e05" />

Collect trajectories from <Math tex={String.raw`p_\theta`} />, calculate <Math tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(\tau)`} /> for each sample, and weight it by the return. The symbol <Math tex={String.raw`\nabla`} /> denotes the vector of partial derivatives with respect to all weights. Return is treated as a number in this calculation, so a nonsmooth reward function can still be handled when the probability distribution meets the required conditions.

This does not differentiate through the sampling operation in the ordinary way. It is a <strong>score-function estimator</strong> that differentiates how likely a sampled action is under the policy. [Silver's likelihood-ratio explanation in Lecture 7](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KHZVXao4qXs&t=2065s) covers the same connection.

[Original lecture from 7:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=447s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={9} caption="Action log probabilities can be computed without knowing the environment" />

Taking the logarithm of the trajectory-probability product gives a sum of log probabilities for the initial state, each action, and each transition. If the initial distribution and environment transitions do not directly share parameters with the policy weights, their derivatives with respect to those weights are zero. What remains is the log derivative of the action probabilities produced by the policy network.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)`} label="Sum of action log-probability gradients" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e06" />

We can now proceed without an environment model. Record states, actions, and rewards on the robot, then calculate the log probabilities the policy assigns to the recorded actions. The principle is the same whether the input consists of images or joint states.

For continuous actions, <Math tex={String.raw`\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)`} /> is a probability density, not the probability of drawing one exact real-valued action. A density can exceed 1, and using its logarithm is normal. This estimator can nevertheless become unstable if Gaussian variance collapses to zero or log-probability calculations encounter numerical errors.

[Original lecture from 11:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=688s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={10} caption="REINFORCE repeats collection, evaluation, and policy updates" />

REINFORCE follows a simple sequence: collect multiple trajectories under the current policy, calculate their returns, multiply action log-probability gradients by those returns, and average. Finally, update the policy weights in that direction.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\widehat g=\frac1N\sum_i G(\tau_i)\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t}),\quad\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\widehat g`} label="REINFORCE gradient ascent" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e07" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`\eta`} /> is the learning rate. The update adds the gradient because we maximize return. With an optimizer that minimizes an objective, use a loss equal to negative log probability times return and apply gradient descent. Differentiating the return weights together with the policy can produce a different calculation from the intended score-function estimator, so those weights are usually detached from backpropagation.

Our example executes this update in a one-state, two-action problem. The good action has reward probability 0.8 and the other 0.2. We perform 1,500 updates with 64 samples each and calculate the policy's <strong>exact expected reward</strong> at every update, separately from the observed mean return. The executable file and CSV appear later. These are not results from training robot PPO or SAC.

[Original lecture from 13:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=830s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={11} caption="Weighting a behavior-cloning-style gradient by outcomes" />

Behavior cloning learns to increase the log probability of expert actions. Its basic form does not assign each demonstration a return measuring how successful it was. REINFORCE weights actions collected under its own policy by their outcomes. To that extent, both use derivatives that make observed actions more likely.

But “increase good actions and decrease bad actions” requires a reference point. If every return is positive, trajectories returning 99 and 101 both contribute terms that increase log probability. A sample's coefficient does not become negative simply because 99 is relatively low. Normalization among competing action probabilities and averaging across samples jointly determine the result.

Subtracting a baseline of 100, as we do later, changes the weights to −1 and +1. Only then does the intuition of reducing the probability of below-average actions directly match each sample's sign. A similar-looking equation does not mean that inserting past expert data unchanged gives the same policy gradient.

[Original lecture from 14:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=894s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={12} caption="Future actions cannot change rewards already received" />

The figure shows a robot approaching a cup successfully, then moving too far at the end and losing it. Multiplying every action by the full trajectory return mixes earlier rewards unrelated to that decision into its learning signal. An action can affect only rewards from the current time onward.

In this example, weighting by the full return can also give the good early approach an unfavorable signal. Time-specific reward-to-go removes past rewards and reduces variance. Identifying which particular movement caused the failure remains a separate problem.

<Math display tex={String.raw`G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}r_k,\quad\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)G_t]`} label="Policy gradient with future returns" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e08" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`G_t`} /> is the <strong>reward-to-go</strong>: rewards remaining from that time onward. Multiplying only by rewards after <Math tex={String.raw`t`} /> preserves the expected gradient because the score function has conditional mean zero given the history before the action. This removes unnecessary noise from the past.

This change does not mean that early approach movements are always judged successful. A penalty for later dropping the cup is still in an early action's future and therefore remains in its <Math tex={String.raw`G_t`} />. Reward-to-go removes past rewards; it does not perfectly assign responsibility to each action. Keeping this distinction in mind reconciles the cup illustration with the actual equation.

[Original lecture from 15:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=954s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={13} caption="Separating the difference between 99 and 101 from their large common offset" />

The two robot-arm illustrations compare similar high scores. Suppose one movement overshoots the cup but returns 99, while another grasps it successfully and returns 101. The difference is 2, yet each sample gradient is multiplied by a number around 100. Attaching similarly large numbers to random gradients can require many samples to estimate the mean reliably.

Here, variance measures how much gradient estimates fluctuate across repeatedly collected samples. The difference between two returns alone does not determine variance for every problem. Score-function magnitude, action probabilities, and reward noise also matter. The figure's “2%” illustrates a contrast; it is not a general formula for measuring signal-to-noise ratio.

Subtracting a baseline removes the large common component while preserving the difference of 2. We calculate the actual variance change exactly in the small problem below. Saying that a suitable baseline helps is different from saying that every baseline always reduces variance.

[Original lecture from 18:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1086s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={14} caption="An action-independent baseline leaves the expected gradient unchanged" />

A baseline represents roughly how much return to expect in the current state. Returns above it receive positive weights; returns below it receive negative weights. Subtracting 100 from 99 and 101 gives −1 and +1, making the distinction between the actions clearer.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}[b(s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)]=b(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0`} label="Why the baseline term has expectation zero" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e09" />

As in [Silver's baseline explanation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KHZVXao4qXs&t=4069s), this term has expectation zero, so using <Math tex={String.raw`G_t-b(s_t)`} /> in the policy gradient gives the same expected gradient. The key is that the baseline <strong>does not depend on the action currently sampled</strong>. It may vary by state. Even when a baseline network is trained alongside the policy, its output is treated as a fixed weight during the actor update.

There is a subtlety when using the same minibatch's mean return as the baseline, as illustrated in the lecture. If a sample contributes to its own baseline, the independence argument no longer applies unchanged. In a simple problem with <Math tex={String.raw`N`} /> independent trajectories, the expected estimate is scaled by <Math tex={String.raw`1-1/N`} />. A baseline learned from separate data or a leave-one-out mean avoids this issue. Our example verifies it by exhaustive calculation for <Math tex={String.raw`N`} />=2.

[Original lecture from 19:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1179s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={15} caption="State value provides a reference score for the current situation" />

A return of 10 can be disappointing from an easy starting point and excellent from a situation that usually ends in failure. Instead of subtracting a constant 100 in every state, it is often useful to use the performance the policy normally expects in the current state.

The quantity <Math tex={String.raw`Q^\pi(s,a)`} /> is the expected return from taking action <Math tex={String.raw`a`} /> now and following policy <Math tex={String.raw`\pi`} /> afterward. The quantity <Math tex={String.raw`V^\pi(s)`} /> is the policy's average return from that state. Reward-to-go can be viewed as a noisy sample of this <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> value. A state-value network learns the average from those samples and serves as a baseline.

<strong>State value is not the variance-minimizing baseline for every policy.</strong> Accounting for the squared magnitude of the gradient in a single state gives the following weighted mean as the optimal constant.

<Math display tex={String.raw`b^*(s)=\frac{\mathbb E[\|g(a)\|^2Q^\pi(s,a)\mid s]}{\mathbb E[\|g(a)\|^2\mid s]}`} label="The variance-minimizing baseline" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e10" />

Here, <Math tex={String.raw`g(a)=\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)`} />. If score-function magnitudes differ between actions, this value may differ from the ordinary mean <Math tex={String.raw`V^\pi(s)`} />. Silver's Lecture 7 likewise introduces <Math tex={String.raw`V`} /> as a “good baseline.” The graph below deliberately isolates this difference.

<LearningFigure src="/assets/posts/rl-robot-learning-ch05-policy-gradients/baseline-variance.png" alt="Exact policy-gradient variance as the baseline changes, distinguishing state value 99.6 from the variance-minimizing baseline 100.4" caption="Calculated for action-selection probabilities of 0.7 and 0.3. [Original lecture from 21:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1303s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={16} caption="Advantage measures improvement over the current policy's average" />

<strong>Advantage</strong> describes how much better an action is than the current policy's average in the same state, rather than the action's absolute score.

<Math display tex={String.raw`A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s),\quad\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi}[A^\pi(s,a)]=0`} label="Advantage: definition and mean" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e11" />

If two actions have <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> values 4 and 10, chosen with probabilities 75% and 25%, then <Math tex={String.raw`V`} /> is 5.5. Their advantages are −1.5 and +4.5. The simple arithmetic mean is not zero, but the mean weighted by policy probabilities is. The reference point is therefore the current policy's average, rather than the arithmetic average over every possible action.

Subtracting a learned state value from a sampled return gives an advantage estimate. When an approximate critic replaces the exact <Math tex={String.raw`A^\pi`} />, its error also affects the actor. Managing error and bias in exchange for lower variance becomes a central issue in actor–critic methods.

[Original lecture from 23:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1386s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={17} caption="Changing the policy also changes the data distribution" />

If a policy starts moving left more often, the camera sees different scenes and the robot visits different subsequent states. A dataset collected by the previous policy does not directly represent the world the new policy will encounter. This is distribution shift in policy learning.

Basic REINFORCE takes an average over trajectories of the current policy. This condition holds when calculating the gradient at the current weights on a fresh batch, but it no longer holds exactly after changing the weights and repeatedly updating on that same batch. The data need not be deleted from disk; they <strong>cannot continue to be treated as current-policy samples without correction</strong>.

Collecting real robot data takes time and entails wear and reset costs. Learning multiple times from the same data would help, but requires handling the distribution mismatch that develops during reuse. Importance sampling, small policy updates, and off-policy critics offer different approaches to this problem.

[Original lecture from 24:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1462s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={18} caption="Importance sampling corrects averages from data collected under another distribution" />

The blue distribution in the figure is the target distribution; the yellow one is the distribution actually used for sampling. Samples drawn from the yellow distribution receive larger weights when they should occur relatively more often under the target. Each weight is target density divided by collection density.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathbb E_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\mathbb E_{x\sim q}[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)]`} label="The importance-sampling identity" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e12" />

Suppose collection probabilities for two actions are 0.8 and 0.2, while the new policy assigns 0.4 and 0.6. If only the second action rewards 1, the collection mean is 0.2 but the target mean is 0.6. Multiplying the second action's outcomes by 3 and the first action's by 0.5 makes the weighted mean under the collection distribution exactly 0.6.

The identity requires a <strong>support</strong> condition: wherever the target distribution is positive, the collection distribution must also be positive. If the old policy truly assigned an action probability zero, its outcomes cannot be recovered from the data. Distinguish an outcome that happens not to appear in a finite sample from one assigned zero probability by the distribution itself.

[Original lecture from 25:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1539s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={19} caption="In the same environment, environment terms cancel in the trajectory-probability ratio" />

Write the previous policy as <Math tex={String.raw`\pi_{\rm old}`} /> and the policy we wish to change to as <Math tex={String.raw`\pi_\theta`} />. For the same trajectory collected under the same initial distribution and MDP, initial-state probabilities and environment transitions cancel between numerator and denominator.

<Math display tex={String.raw`w_\theta(\tau)=\frac{p_\theta(\tau)}{p_{\rm old}(\tau)}=\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\rm old}(a_t\mid s_t)}`} label="The trajectory importance ratio" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e13" />

Saving the log probabilities that the collection policy assigned to each recorded action is enough to compute this ratio by comparison with the new policy's log probabilities. This is why exact knowledge of the robot's contact dynamics is unnecessary.

[Berkeley Lecture 5, Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZd508qGFt0&t=314s) also shows initial-distribution and transition terms canceling, leaving only policy-probability ratios. This cancellation assumes the same environment. If transitions differ when moving from simulation trajectories to the real robot's distribution, policy ratios alone do not correct every difference. Data with changed reward definitions or termination conditions also cannot be treated as equivalent without explanation.

[Original lecture from 28:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1698s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={20} caption="Products of many probability ratios can give a few samples excessive weight" />

Even a per-step ratio of 1.1 becomes approximately 13,781 after 100 multiplications. A ratio of 0.9 raised to the 100th power is about 0.0000266. Small differences accumulate over long trajectories, leaving some samples almost ignored while others dominate the estimate.

This is not a theorem that all importance weights always explode exponentially. Variance depends on the policies, environment, horizon, and reward structure. Summing log probabilities can postpone numerical overflow from multiplication, but it does not solve the statistical problem of weight concentrating on a few samples.

The full trajectory weight at this stage attempts to change the distribution exactly. The later calculation retaining only a single time step's action ratio is more convenient but introduces an additional approximation. Do not read both expressions as equally exact identities.

[Original lecture from 29:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1790s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={21} caption="Action-probability ratios alone leave changes in state visitation uncorrected" />

At a particular time, the probability of a state–action pair factors into the visited-state distribution and conditional action distribution. The new-to-old state–action ratio therefore also has two factors.

A single-step action ratio can be computed by feeding the same observation to both policies. But the policies may encounter that observation at different frequencies. For example, if one policy visits a corner of the workbench more often, correcting grasp probabilities in that corner does not also correct the difference in visitation frequency.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\frac{d_\theta(s)\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{d_{\rm old}(s)\pi_{\rm old}(a\mid s)}=\frac{d_\theta(s)}{d_{\rm old}(s)}\frac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\rm old}(a\mid s)}`} label="State- and action-distribution ratios" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e14" />

The two policy networks can compute the action-probability ratio. In contrast, <Math tex={String.raw`d_\theta(s)`} /> describes how often a policy visits each state in the environment and cannot be obtained from one network output. Setting this ratio to 1 yields a <strong>surrogate objective</strong> evaluating the new policy's actions at states represented by the current batch.

Fixing advantage estimates obtained under the old policy gives the practical construction leading to TRPO and PPO. It is useful near a policy that has not changed much, but an arbitrary old replay buffer does not thereby produce an exact current-policy gradient. Consider both the state-distribution approximation and differences in evaluating future returns.

[Original lecture from 31:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1874s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={22} caption="Keep the old policy fixed while reusing a batch" />

The loop-back arrow in the algorithm diagram means performing several updates on the same batch without collecting fresh data. The old policy's recorded log probabilities must remain the same reference throughout. Replacing the denominator with the current policy at every update makes the ratio always 1, preventing it from tracking change.

Estimate returns and advantages from the collected batch, hold them fixed, and update across several minibatches and epochs. An epoch is one pass through the collected data. After enough updates, collect fresh data and reset the old-policy reference.

This differs from off-policy learning with unlimited reuse of old data. The lecture's heading “off-policy version” marks a derivation extending the basic on-policy expression. Grouping PPO, with its approximations and restrictions, together with algorithms such as DQN and SAC that keep learning from a replay buffer obscures their different data-use strategies.

[Original lecture from 32:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=1955s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={23} caption="Entropy controls how strongly the action distribution concentrates on one choice" />

Entropy quantifies how spread out a distribution is. Choosing each of two discrete actions with probability 50% gives entropy approximately 0.693 in natural-log units; effectively choosing only one gives entropy near zero. An entropy bonus can help sustain exploration when reward maximization would otherwise concentrate the policy too early.

<Math display tex={String.raw`H(\pi(\cdot\mid s))=-\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\log\pi(a\mid s)`} label="Discrete entropy" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e15" />

For a diagonal Gaussian over continuous actions, differential entropy is proportional to the sum of log standard deviations. Differential entropy can be negative and depends on units and coordinate transformations, so its numerical value should not be directly compared with discrete entropy.

Adding entropy does not ensure that every unvisited state will be reached or automatically solve importance sampling's support problem. Spreading actions within a state and reaching that state in the environment are separate issues. Since dangerous actions can also become more likely, physical robots require additional design such as command limits and stopping conditions.

[Original lecture from 33:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2005s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={24} caption="Measure policy change in action distributions rather than weight distance" />

In the figure, an action assigned probability 0.005 by the old policy receives 0.10 under the new policy, giving a ratio of 20. This illustrates how one rare high-scoring experience can be amplified excessively over repeated updates. Small changes in network weights do not guarantee small changes in the action distribution.

KL divergence measures how much two distributions differ. The KL from the old policy to the new policy becomes large when the new policy makes previously possible actions almost impossible.

<Math display tex={String.raw`D_{\rm KL}(\pi_{\rm old}\Vert\pi_\theta)=\sum_a\pi_{\rm old}(a\mid s)\log\frac{\pi_{\rm old}(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}`} label="KL divergence from the old policy to the new policy" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e16" />

KL generally changes when its direction is reversed, so it is not an ordinary distance. A small average KL across states also does not mean that every action ratio in every state lies within a fixed range. It is more accurately understood as a statistical constraint on average policy change.

[Original lecture from 35:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2131s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={25} caption="TRPO seeks expected improvement while restricting policy change" />

TRPO increases the product of the new-policy probability ratio and old-policy advantage at states and actions collected by the old policy. At the same time, it constrains average KL under the old state distribution to remain below a threshold. That threshold determines the size of the trust region.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\rm old}[u_t(\theta)\widehat A_t],\quad u_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\rm old}(a_t\mid s_t)}`} label="The TRPO surrogate objective" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e17" />

The original TRPO paper derives improvement guarantees from a lower bound on policy performance, then uses average KL, sample estimates, and local approximations in its practical algorithm. The slide's “monotonic improvement guarantee” should therefore not be read as a promise that return increases in every actual neural-network run.

[Berkeley Lecture 9, Part 4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QWnpF0FaKL4&t=1074s) adds context on natural gradients and matrix–vector computations. Explicitly constructing and inverting the Fisher information matrix, which describes curvature, is expensive with many parameters. The original paper's practical implementation approximates a direction using matrix–vector products and conjugate gradients without explicitly constructing the matrix, then adjusts the step through line search. The lecture's “about 20×” depends on the implementation and problem; it is not a fixed computational law. K-FAC is not a mandatory extra step in every TRPO implementation either.

[Original lecture from 37:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2259s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={26} caption="Rough-terrain locomotion depends on both joint sensing and the training environment" />

The photograph shows a quadruped negotiating ledges and obstacles. Having four legs does not automatically confer terrain understanding. Maintaining balance requires quickly using information such as whether a foot slips after contact, whether the body tilts, and whether joints track their target angles.

The 2020 work by Lee, Hwangbo, and colleagues first trained a teacher policy through reinforcement learning in simulation, with access to additional terrain and contact information. They then distilled it into a student policy receiving only histories of proprioceptive signals from joint encoders and an IMU available on the real robot. An IMU is an inertial sensor measuring signals related to body rotation and acceleration. The student learns to imitate the teacher's actions and internal representations.

<strong>Zero-shot</strong> means deployment without retuning the policy for each test terrain. It does not mean that training data or simulation design were unnecessary. The paper combines a terrain curriculum, a physics model, and sensory-history processing. Reducing this result to “insert the TRPO equation and walk on any terrain” removes essential conditions. Read it within the actual evaluation scope of the [original paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251).

[Original lecture from 39:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2360s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={27} caption="PPO cuts off further gains when a favorable change becomes too large" />

Instead of complex KL-constrained optimization, PPO-Clip clips an objective formed from probability ratios and advantages. The ratio <Math tex={String.raw`u_t`} /> divides the new action probability by its probability during collection. With <Math tex={String.raw`\epsilon`} /> set to 0.2, the clipped ratio lies between 0.8 and 1.2.

<Math display tex={String.raw`L^{\rm clip}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\rm old}[\min(u_t\widehat A_t,\operatorname{clip}(u_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat A_t)]`} label="The PPO clipped surrogate objective" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e18" />

With advantage +2, ratio 1.5 gives an unclipped objective of 3, but the clipped term is 2.4, so the smaller value 2.4 is used. Once a good action has been increased sufficiently, that sample provides no further incentive to increase it. Conversely, with advantage −2 and ratio 0.5, the clipped term −1.6 is smaller than the original term −1, so −1.6 is used. This cuts off additional gains after a bad action has already been reduced sufficiently.

<LearningFigure src="/assets/posts/rl-robot-learning-ch05-policy-gradients/ppo-clipping.png" alt="PPO objective curves flatten on different sides for positive and negative advantages" caption="The probability-ratio interval 0.8–1.2 is shaded. Which side becomes flat depends on the advantage's sign." source="https://github.com/JJo-0/JJo-0.github.io/tree/main/docs/robot-learning/labs/05-policy-gradients" credit="JJo; executable code and numerical CSV are available" width={1350} height={525} />

<strong>The policy's actual ratio is not forcibly kept inside the interval.</strong> Changes in an unfavorable direction retain an unclipped penalty, and updates from other samples through shared weights can move this sample's ratio farther. PPO does not inherit TRPO's theoretical monotonic-improvement guarantee. Some implementations also monitor KL or stop updates early. Its ease of use with first-order optimizers such as Adam is a major advantage.

Related objectives also appear in language-model training, but GRPO and PPO should not be called the same algorithm. This chapter focuses on robot-policy data collection and continuous control.

[Original lecture from 40:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2405s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={28} caption="Rubik's Cube manipulation combines control, state estimation, and simulation" />

The five-fingered robot hand in the photograph reorients a cube and turns its faces while maintaining its grasp. Familiar to humans, this manipulation is a difficult control problem involving changing contacts and possible object slippage. The rubber glove illustrates a perturbation to friction and contact conditions.

OpenAI's 2019 research used a recurrent control policy trained with PPO and a separate vision model estimating cube pose and face rotations from camera images. It should not be read as feeding raw camera images directly into the final manipulation policy to solve every part jointly. Also distinguish planning the high-level cube-solving sequence from physically executing the next manipulation target with the hand.

Automatic domain randomization (ADR) progressively broadens the physical and visual conditions encountered in simulation. This encourages operation under varied conditions rather than fitting only one simulator's friction or appearance. The photograph does not demonstrate every form of human-level dexterity; it shows results for the cube task and perturbations defined in the [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07113). Hardware degrees of freedom and the number of independently commanded actuators are not always identical either.

[Original lecture from 42:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2525s).

The lecture's video with some fingers tied together illustrates policy robustness when familiar manipulation conditions change. Even rotating an object in the same direction becomes difficult to accomplish by replaying the old trajectory if available fingers and contacts change. ADR varies physical conditions during training to broaden experience with such differences. Read the demonstration alongside the actual altered conditions and successful tasks, rather than extending it into a recovery guarantee for every finger failure or object.

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={29} caption="PPO's full objective combines the actor, critic, and exploration" />

The first term is the clipped surrogate objective encouraging the actor to increase good actions' probabilities. The second is squared error fitting the critic's state value to a target return. The third is an entropy bonus discouraging the policy from concentrating too quickly on one action.

<Math display tex={String.raw`L^{\rm total}=L^{\rm clip}-c_V\mathbb E[(V_\phi(s)-V^{\rm target})^2]+c_H\mathbb E[H(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s))]`} label="The full PPO maximization objective" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e19" />

Because the expression is <strong>maximized</strong>, value error carries a minus sign. In a loss to be minimized, reverse the first and third terms' signs and keep value error positive. The coefficients <Math tex={String.raw`c_V`} /> and <Math tex={String.raw`c_H`} /> balance terms with different units.

The quantity <Math tex={String.raw`V^{\rm target}`} /> is the target the critic attempts to fit. Its construction varies by implementation, using complete observed returns, bootstrapped returns, or other choices. Store the actor's advantage estimates separately from critic targets, and usually hold advantages fixed during actor updates. When the networks share an initial representation, gradients from all three terms change it together, so clipping a single sample does not strictly constrain overall policy change.

[Original lecture from 43:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2602s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={30} caption="Actor–critic learns action selection and future evaluation together" />

The actor is the policy, and the critic is a value estimator. The actor uses whether outcomes exceeded the critic's expectations as a signal for changing action probabilities. Actor–critic is not restricted to continuous actions; it also applies to discrete-action policies. Nor is every actor–critic method on-policy, as the contrast between PPO and SAC demonstrates.

Giving a critic object positions available only in simulation can improve value estimation during training. The deployed actor can be restricted to actual cameras and sensors. Record critic and actor inputs separately to make clear which information is required on the real robot.

The simplest advantage estimate based on a one-step TD error is:

<Math display tex={String.raw`\delta_t=r_t+\gamma(1-d_t)V_\phi(s_{t+1})-V_\phi(s_t)`} label="TD error accounting for termination" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e20" />

The indicator <Math tex={String.raw`d_t`} /> is 1 on actual termination. If the critic is accurate and the state contains sufficient information, this error indicates the action's relative value. An inaccurate critic does not automatically yield low bias. Future value is zero when an episode truly ends, whereas a rollout cut short merely by collection length requires appropriate bootstrapping.

Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) combines multistep TD errors, using <Math tex={String.raw`\lambda`} /> to balance short-term bootstrapping against longer sampled returns. With <Math tex={String.raw`\lambda=0`} /> it becomes one-step TD; summing over longer horizons brings it closer to sampled returns. This controls the trade-off between variance and value-approximation error. The [original GAE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438) is useful background before reading a PPO implementation.

Robot simulation can also use an asymmetric setup where only the critic sees additional information, such as true terrain friction or exact object state. The deployed actor must still operate from real sensors, and not every use of privileged information satisfies the same statistical conditions.

[Original lecture from 45:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2714s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={31} caption="SAC evaluates current-policy actions at past states" />

SAC trains its critic on past transitions from a replay buffer. Actor updates also use past states, but draw fresh actions at those states from the <strong>current policy</strong>. This differs from learning to reproduce the old actions stored in the buffer. The critic estimates the value of these current actions and supplies the actor's learning signal.

SAC uses a maximum-entropy reinforcement-learning objective that increases policy entropy as well as reward. The actor's maximization objective combines expected <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> and entropy. Converting it into a minimization loss gives the following signs.

<Math display tex={String.raw`L_\pi(\theta)=\mathbb E_{s\sim D,a\sim\pi_\theta}[\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)-\min_{j=1,2}Q_{\phi_j}(s,a)]`} label="The SAC actor loss to be minimized" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e21" />

Because entropy is the negative expected log probability, minimizing <Math tex={String.raw`\alpha \log \pi-Q`} /> corresponds to maximizing <Math tex={String.raw`Q+\alpha H`} />. Minimizing the slide's actor expression <Math tex={String.raw`+\alpha H-Q`} /> unchanged would instead reduce entropy, so the main text explicitly uses the original paper's log-probability form to clarify the sign.

In the equations below, theta denotes actor weights and phi critic weights. The subscript <Math tex={String.raw`j`} /> identifies which of the two critics is meant, and a bar over phi denotes a target copy. The symbol <Math tex={String.raw`D`} /> denotes the replay buffer of past transitions. Practical continuous-action SAC uses two <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> estimators and slowly updated target copies. A current-policy action sampled at the next state forms the target below; each critic reduces its squared error against that target.

<Math display tex={String.raw`y=r+\gamma(1-d)[\min_j Q_{\bar\phi_j}(s',a')-\alpha\log\pi_\theta(a'\mid s')],\quad a'\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s')`} label="The SAC soft TD target" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e22" />

Using the smaller <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> estimate helps mitigate overestimation. The temperature <Math tex={String.raw`\alpha`} /> determines the relative importance of entropy and reward. Changing reward scale also changes an appropriate temperature. Automatic temperature tuning learns this coefficient against a target entropy; it does not enforce identical randomness in every state. The update equations were also checked against [SAC Algorithms and Applications](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05905).

[Original lecture from 46:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2817s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={32} caption="Reparameterization constructs an action from fixed noise and differentiates that path" />

When a policy outputs a mean and standard deviation, treating an action simply as a sampled value does not preserve its connection to those parameters in the autodifferentiation graph. Instead, first sample standard-normal noise independent of the parameters, then combine it with the policy outputs to construct the action.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\quad u=\mu_\theta(s)+\sigma_\theta(s)\odot\epsilon`} label="Gaussian reparameterization" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e23" />

Holding <Math tex={String.raw`\epsilon`} /> fixed for this sample makes the path from mean and standard deviation to action differentiable. The symbol <Math tex={String.raw`\odot`} /> means elementwise multiplication. This path passes the critic's action derivative to the actor. Unlike REINFORCE's log-probability derivative, this approach uses a differentiable critic and continuous action-generation function. It does not backpropagate through real-world contact.

When commands have bounded ranges, policies often squash Gaussian samples as in <Math tex={String.raw`a=\tanh(u)`} />. The action log density then requires a Jacobian correction for the coordinate transformation.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\log\pi(a\mid s)=\log p_U(u\mid s)-\sum_i\log(1-\tanh^2u_i)`} label="The tanh log-density correction" />

<RobotEquationGuide id="rl05-e24" />

Simply clipping actions while retaining the original Gaussian log density in the entropy term gives a distribution mismatch. Numerical stability near saturation also needs checking. The example below compares a reparameterized sample's derivative with finite differences and separately checks numerical values for the tanh density correction.

[Original lecture from 49:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=2971s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={33} caption="Read real-robot results alongside both the algorithm and experimental conditions" />

The three photographs illustrate different contact problems: manipulating objects, quadruped locomotion, and reorienting an object within a hand. Understanding each result requires knowing its observations, action space, reward design, reset procedure, and hardware conditions.

The original SAC paper and subsequent Applications paper combine replay buffers, a stochastic actor, and an entropy objective to evaluate data efficiency and stability in continuous control. They also present real quadruped locomotion and hand manipulation. We should not assume that every photograph assembled on the slides comes from one experiment or the same SAC version. A still image alone also cannot reveal a video's sequence or failure rate.

Replay buffers do not eliminate collection costs. An overly optimistic critic in a new state–action region can be exploited by the actor. Evaluations favoring SAC's data efficiency do not establish that it always outperforms PPO on every robot task. This chapter's small executable examples likewise do not reproduce those robot experiments.

[Original lecture from 50:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AdTGz8YnnlE&t=3000s).

<LectureSlide lecture={5} page={34} caption="Distinguishing policy gradients, PPO, and SAC by their data and objectives" />

REINFORCE weights action log probabilities using outcomes generated by the current policy. Reward-to-go removes past rewards; a baseline subtracts a reference independent of the current action; advantage compares against the current state's expected average. Their shared aim is a more accurate, less noisy signal for policy improvement.

TRPO and PPO control policy change to keep improvement estimates from the previous batch useful. PPO trains several times on a recent batch but does not reuse old data without limit. SAC learns <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> from past transitions, asks the critic about freshly sampled current-policy actions, and optimizes an objective that includes entropy.

| Method | Signal used by the actor | What matters about data use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| REINFORCE | Sampled return × log-probability gradient | Runs generated by the current policy |
| PPO | Fixed advantages and a clipped probability-ratio objective | Limited repeated updates on a recent batch |
| SAC | Soft <Math tex={String.raw`Q`} /> and log probability for current-policy actions | Replay of past transitions; fresh current-action samples |

For any algorithm, the meanings of state, action, and reward come first. Likewise, read reported success rates alongside observations, hardware, training-data volume, and evaluation conditions. The code below shows how this chapter's key equations differ numerically.
