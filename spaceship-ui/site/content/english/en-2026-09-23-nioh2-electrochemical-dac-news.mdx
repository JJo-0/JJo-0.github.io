<BeginnerGuide lang="en" title="How can a battery-like device draw CO₂ from air?">

<NewsFigure media="sep23-dac-fig-1" lang="en" priority compact />

This device uses reversible redox reactions like those of rechargeable electrodes. It does not convert CO₂ into electricity.

<strong>Nickel hydroxide electrodes raise and lower the pH of water</strong> so that one side captures CO₂ from air and the other releases a concentrated stream. Think of a shuttle moving material between two rooms. A dust filter in an air purifier works differently.

- <strong>Direct air capture (DAC)</strong> separates CO₂ directly from ambient air.
- <strong>pH</strong> is a logarithmic measure of acidity and alkalinity. A difference of one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold difference in hydrogen ion activity.
- An <strong>electrode</strong> exchanges electrons in a chemical reaction.
- A <strong>membrane</strong> permits transport of selected ions and helps keep two flow paths from freely mixing.
- A <strong>stack</strong> combines multiple cells to increase the processing area.

<strong>Reading Figure 1</strong>. Panel a shows CO₂ captured as carbonate in the upper air path and released as gas on the other side of the membrane. Panel b photographs the 25 cm² laboratory cell. Panel c photographs the pilot stack of nine 300 cm² cells. The authors built both sizes. The pilot photograph alone does not establish a commercial plant.

</BeginnerGuide>

## 1. The result in 30 seconds: moving CO₂ by changing pH electrically

Direct air capture selects roughly 400 CO₂ molecules from every million air molecules. A September 22, 2026 *Nature Energy* paper describes a symmetric cell in which the reaction between nickel hydroxide <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{Ni(OH)_2}`} /> and nickel oxyhydroxide <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{NiOOH}`} /> changes pH. One side captures CO₂ and the other releases purified gas.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The 25 cm² cell ran for 5,000 hours. A stack of nine 300 cm² cells captured and released purified CO₂ for 48 hours. The stack used 0.83 MWh of cell electricity per tonne of CO₂, at a capture flux of 75 kgCO₂ per square meter of electrode per year. <strong>The 5,000 hours apply to the small cell and the 48 hours to the larger stack.</strong> Blower electricity and final compression lie outside the 0.83 MWh/tCO₂ figure.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The experiments <strong>connect low cell electricity use, extended single-cell operation and a larger capture area through battery-like pH cycling in place of thermal regeneration</strong>. Multiyear stack lifetime, energy including blowers and compressors, nickel corrosion and actual cost per tonne remain open.

## 2. Why is capturing CO₂ from air difficult?

At 400 ppm, CO₂ makes up 0.04% of air, about 400 in every million molecules. In a power-plant exhaust stream containing several percent CO₂ or more, molecules encounter a sorbent much more often. Capturing the same amount from air requires moving more gas and makes pressure losses important to fan demand.

After capture, the material must release CO₂ for reuse. This step is called regeneration. A thermal swing changes temperature, a pressure swing changes pressure, and an electrochemical swing uses voltage or current to change chemical state. Even good capture is difficult to deploy if regeneration consumes too much energy or the material fails quickly.

The study asks whether pH can sustain the concentration gap between 400 ppm air and purified CO₂, whether the same electrodes can cycle for thousands of hours, and whether airflow and energy performance persist when cells are stacked. These are questions about thermodynamics, durability and stack engineering.

## 3. Why does the form of CO₂ change with pH?

In water, CO₂ takes several forms. Dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid are relatively abundant on the acidic side. As the liquid becomes more alkaline, bicarbonate <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{HCO_3^-}`} /> and carbonate <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{CO_3^{2-}}`} /> account for larger fractions. Conversion to ions at high pH holds carbon in solution. Lowering pH shifts the equilibrium toward gaseous CO₂.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

<NewsFigure media="sep23-dac-fig-2" lang="en" />

**Figure 2 asks whether the small cell captures repeatedly.** Panel a shows voltage over time, with initial conditioning shaded gray. The orange curve in b is CO₂ concentration at the capture outlet. Concentrations below inlet air provide evidence of removal. Panel c tracks charge exchanged each cycle, d the fraction of electrons contributing to CO₂ transfer, e electrical energy per captured and purified mass, and f capture rate per area. Panels g–k explain how a cycle establishes the pH gap and rebuilds it after release temporarily disrupts it. Compare concentration, energy, flux and electron efficiency over matching cycles rather than voltage alone.

The supplement calculates equilibrium at 30°C and 1 bar. Capturing from roughly 400 ppm air requires pH at least 10.4 on the capture side, while releasing purified CO₂ requires pH at most 7.6 on the other side. The minimum gap is <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta\mathrm{pH}=2.8`} />. Capturing half the CO₂ so the outlet reaches 200 ppm requires a gap near 3.0 in another example. These are thermodynamic limits for specified concentrations. The real cell need not stay at exactly those pH values.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

<CandidateEquation id="dac-nernst" lang="en" title="Why does a pH difference of 2.8 require at least about 0.17 V?" tex={String.raw`\Delta E=\frac{2.303RT}{F}\Delta\mathrm{pH}\approx0.17\;\mathrm{V}\tag{1}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`R`} /> is the gas constant <Math tex={String.raw`8.314\;\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}`} />, <Math tex={String.raw`T`} /> is absolute temperature, and <Math tex={String.raw`F`} /> is the Faraday constant <Math tex={String.raw`96485\;\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}`} />. The pH difference is <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta\mathrm{pH}`} />. The factor 2.303 converts between natural and base-ten logarithms because pH uses a base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity. <Math tex={String.raw`RT/F`} /> has units of J/C, or V.

At 303 K the voltage per pH unit is <Math tex={String.raw`2.303RT/F\approx0.060\;\mathrm{V}`} />. Multiplying by 2.8 gives about 0.168 V, rounded to 0.17 V. This is a reversible lower bound. Finite reaction rates add activation overpotential. Ionic and electronic resistance add ohmic losses, and gas and ion transport add further losses.

The calculation applies established equilibrium and Nernst relations to DAC concentrations. It specifies a required pH gap and minimum voltage that guide electrode and membrane design. Temperature, pressure and capture fraction can change the requirement.

</CandidateEquation>

## 4. How do matching nickel electrodes pump acid and base?

Both sides use Ni(OH)₂/NiOOH electrodes, with a hydroxide exchange membrane between them. Reduction at one electrode produces OH⁻, raising pH and capturing CO₂. Oxidation at the other consumes OH⁻, lowering pH and releasing CO₂. Electronic and ionic currents must balance. The membrane, gas channels, solution and porous-electrode transport all participate.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<CandidateEquation id="dac-nickel-redox" lang="en" title="How do the Ni(OH)₂ and NiOOH reactions change pH?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}\mathrm{NiOOH+H_2O+e^-}&\rightarrow\mathrm{Ni(OH)_2+OH^-}\\\mathrm{Ni(OH)_2+OH^-}&\rightarrow\mathrm{NiOOH+H_2O+e^-}\end{aligned}\tag{2}`}>

The first line is reduction. One electron <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{e^-}`} /> enters and one OH⁻ is produced. The added base shifts CO₂ toward bicarbonate and carbonate. The second line is the reverse oxidation. It consumes OH⁻ and releases an electron, moving that solution toward lower pH. The coefficients give molar ratios and conserve atoms and charge.

Ideally one mole of electrode reaction involves one mole each of electrons and OH⁻. It need not transport one mole of CO₂. Side reactions, membrane ion transport and carbonate equilibrium make electron efficiency a separate quantity to measure.

With the same electrode material on both sides, neither electrode must always capture. At the end of a half cycle the current direction and flow roles can switch, with A releasing and B capturing. The design uses a known reversible nickel reaction to drive both sides of a repeatable pH swing.

</CandidateEquation>

Earlier electrochemical DAC includes redox-active molecules that change their affinity for CO₂, bipolar membranes that generate acid and base, and electrochemical amine regeneration. This study combines the OH⁻ difference generated by nickel hydroxide battery electrodes with a hydroxide exchange membrane. Its contribution joins a symmetric cell and low current density with air passages and stacking, linking extended operation to purified release.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

## 5. What goes into 0.83 MWh/tCO₂?

<NewsFigure media="sep23-dac-fig-3" lang="en" />

**Figure 3 asks whether capture persists beyond the first few cycles.** The horizontal axis samples separated windows around cycles in the 100s, 2,230s and 3,010s. The four rows show energy per mass, flux per area, cycle duration and electron efficiency. Their scatter still leaves a capture signal after extended operation. The figure concerns the 25 cm² single cell. The nine-cell stack was tested separately.

The capture and purified release (CR) boundary counts cell electricity used to capture CO₂ from dilute air and release a purified stream. It excludes the blower driving air through the cell and compression for transport or storage. Some commercial DAC figures include more heating, airflow and compression. Comparing energy numbers requires aligning system boundaries.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The supplement obtains instantaneous capture flux from the inlet–outlet CO₂ mole-fraction difference, flow rate, pressure, temperature and electrode area. It integrates power <Math tex={String.raw`I V`} /> over the same interval and divides by captured mass. Even at low voltage, a low flux can raise energy per tonne. Measure voltage, current, capture and duration over the same period.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

<CandidateEquation id="dac-energy" lang="en" title="How do voltage and CO₂ flux determine energy per tonne?" tex={String.raw`E=\frac{\int I\,V(t)\,\mathrm dt}{\int j(t)\,\mathrm dt}\tag{3}`}>

In the supplement, <Math tex={String.raw`I`} /> is current density, <Math tex={String.raw`V(t)`} /> is time-dependent cell voltage, <Math tex={String.raw`j(t)`} /> is CO₂ mass flux per unit area and <Math tex={String.raw`t`} /> is time. Multiplying A/m² by V gives W/m² and integrating over time gives J/m². Integrating kg/m²/s gives kg/m². Dividing yields J/kg, convertible to MWh/tCO₂.

Multiplying 0.83 MWh/tCO₂ by 3.6 GJ/MWh gives about 2.99 GJ per tonne. A tonne of CO₂ is roughly 22.7 kmol, giving about 132 kJ/mol. This reconciles the paper's two units. It does not estimate an electricity bill or total plant cost.

For charge and discharge, the supplement combines about 0.09 V of kinetic loss, 0.01 V of ohmic loss and 0.17 V of Nernst potential to estimate 0.27 V before remaining losses. A large oxygen-reaction overpotential can appear late in regeneration. This breakdown helps identify where energy can be saved. The voltage need not stay fixed at 0.27 V.

</CandidateEquation>

## 6. Which device ran for 5,000 hours and which for 48?

<NewsFigure media="sep23-dac-fig-4" lang="en" />

**Figure 4 asks whether nine stacked cells can run for 48 hours.** Brown points in a show CR electricity around 0.5–1.0 MWh/tCO₂. Purple points in b show flux roughly 75–100 kgCO₂·m⁻²·yr⁻¹. Panels c and d give cycle time and electron efficiency. The spread shows variation during operation. Figure 3 addresses <strong>duration</strong>, Figure 4 <strong>scale</strong>. The same device has yet to demonstrate both together.

The 25 cm² cell ran for 5,000 hours capturing and releasing CO₂ from roughly 400 ppm air, using about 0.46 MWh/tCO₂ of CR electricity. The nine-cell stack addressed area and flow scale-up. In 48 hours it used 132 kJ/molCO₂, or 0.83 MWh/tCO₂, at 0.19 molCO₂·m⁻²·h⁻¹. That flux corresponds to roughly 75 kgCO₂·m⁻²·yr⁻¹.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Why did the small cell use less energy? Larger areas and stacks can introduce uneven flow, contact differences, membrane resistance and differences among cells. The two tests used different designs and operating conditions, so the increase from 0.46 to 0.83 cannot be assigned to one cause. The two values should not be averaged as repeated measurements of one system.

A pressure drop of 300 Pa still requires blower power. Ideal hydraulic power is approximately pressure drop times volume flow. Actual demand must account for fan efficiency. The authors designed air passages alongside the cell chemistry, but the reported 0.83 MWh/tCO₂ excludes blower electricity.

## 7. What has been demonstrated and what remains?

First, the work connects thermodynamic pH-swing requirements with cycling Ni(OH)₂/NiOOH electrodes. The authors calculated the required gap, created it in a device and discharged captured CO₂ as a purified stream. This reaches beyond equilibrium calculations or simply keeping carbon dissolved.

Second, lifetime and size were separately tested. The next experiment would operate a nine-cell 300 cm² stack or a larger one for thousands of hours, reporting individual cell voltages, flux, pressure drop, electrode mass changes and membrane replacement intervals.

Nickel corrosion is a third consideration. The authors suggest an initial corrosion-derived Ni(OH)₂ layer passivates the electrode, and capacity did not fall in the long cell test. A separate rapid-cycling test showed substantial corrosion after 16,000 cycles. The 5,000 hours therefore do not guarantee durability at every cycling rate or stack condition.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The $92/tCO₂ value is an nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) projection from techno-economic analysis (TEA). The supplement assumes 20 years of operation, a 95% capacity factor, a 92% net/gross ratio, higher flux at scale and automated mega- or gigafactory manufacturing costs. Depending on project scale it uses 100–300 kgCO₂·m⁻²·yr⁻¹, above the current stack's 75. Changing these assumptions changes required electrode area and capital cost.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Corresponding author Yushan Yan is founder and CEO of membrane company Versogen and a cofounder of RepAir, and several authors work for RepAir. Independent pilots, customer-site data and long-term operating costs would strengthen assessment of commercial timing and cost projections.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 8. What to watch and what to ask

Look for <strong>evidence at the same scale over time</strong>. Can a stack of at least 2,700 cm² operate for 5,000 hours or more? What is all-in MWh/tCO₂ including fans, vacuum, compression and cooling? After electrode, membrane and pump replacement and maintenance downtime, does availability approach the TEA's assumed 95%? Does flux and electron efficiency persist as outdoor temperature, humidity, dust and CO₂ concentration change?

Four questions help interpret claims. What concentration entered the device? What equipment does energy use include? How large was the apparatus and how long did it run? Was the number measured or modeled? The paper identifies laboratory air, the CR boundary, durations for two scales and a separate TEA. Combining them would produce the unsupported claim of a large commercial plant running 5,000 hours at $92/t.

An introductory reader can start with pH controlling the balance between gaseous and ionic carbon. Undergraduates can follow the Nernst voltage and energy integral.
