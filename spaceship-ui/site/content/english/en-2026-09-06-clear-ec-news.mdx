> <strong>Study stage: multicenter, single-arm phase 2 clinical trial in humans.</strong> This article explains the study design and findings. Individual treatment decisions belong with the treating clinical team.

## What a response rate can tell us about a treatment

CLEAR-EC investigated cadonilimab plus lenvatinib for advanced endometrial cancer after platinum-based treatment. In 32 patients, the investigators reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 40.6%, with a 95% confidence interval of 23.7–59.4%. <strong>The treatment had no comparison arm, so 40.6% cannot be interpreted as an improvement over existing therapy.</strong> [Study abstract][primary]

### The cancer type alone does not define a trial population

The endometrium lines the uterus. Cancers arising there can differ in stage, histology, molecular characteristics, and prior treatment. Eligibility criteria define the population to which a trial result can reasonably be applied.

CLEAR-EC studied advanced disease after at least one course of platinum-based therapy and enrolled patients without prior immunotherapy. The 32 participants entered between May 2023 and March 2024. Publication in 2026 followed treatment, follow-up, analysis, and review. [Results in the early publication][primary]

The immunotherapy restriction matters as treatment patterns change. Patients seen later in the same disease setting may already have received immunotherapy. Benefits of the combination in that treatment sequence need separate evidence.

## Why test drugs with different mechanisms together?

Combining a drug that modulates an immune pathway with one that targets the tumor environment and signaling could produce effects different from either approach alone. Whether the expected synergy occurs, and whether toxicity increases, must be assessed in patients. A description of the molecular mechanism cannot establish clinical benefit.

In a single-arm trial, all enrolled patients are assigned to the treatment under investigation. The design can identify early signals, but it offers no concurrent group of similar patients receiving another treatment. Differences in patient characteristics, assessments, or follow-up can also distort comparisons with earlier trials.

### Targeting immune checkpoints and the tumor environment

The immune system uses regulatory signals to limit damage to normal tissue. Tumors can exploit these pathways to evade an immune response. Checkpoint inhibitors block particular inhibitory signals to restore activity. Effects can extend beyond the intended tissue, making toxicity part of the efficacy assessment. [National Cancer Institute introduction][nci-checkpoint]

Cadonilimab is a bispecific antibody targeting PD-1 and CTLA-4. Bispecific describes recognition of two targets. It does not imply twice the efficacy. Lenvatinib targets multiple signaling pathways. A rationale for combining mechanisms has to be tested for patient benefit and tolerable risk. [Study introduction][primary]

A single-arm phase 2 trial can look for responses to the combination. Establishing superiority to an alternative treatment requires a comparator. The design also cannot isolate each drug's contribution or establish synergy simply because both were given.

### Why enrollment started with a safety lead-in

The trial began with a small safety cohort. No protocol-defined dose-limiting toxicity was observed in the first three participants during the specified assessment period. This leaves open the possibility of serious adverse events later in a larger cohort. The trial regimen describes the research protocol. [Study design][primary]

The researchers also used a Simon two-stage design. If too few responses occur initially, enrollment can stop before exposing more people to the regimen. Eight confirmed responses in the first 17 participants met the prespecified criterion to proceed. Passing that threshold supports continuing the trial; it does not supply a randomized comparison. [Efficacy analysis][primary]

## How is ORR calculated?

<Math display tex={String.raw`\widehat p_{\rm ORR}=\frac{N_{\rm CR}+N_{\rm PR}}{N_{\rm analysis}}`} label="The number of complete and partial responses divided by the size of the prespecified analysis population" />

CR and PR denote complete and partial responses under defined imaging criteria. Check whether the denominator includes everyone enrolled, everyone treated, or only those evaluable for response. Excluding unevaluable patients after the fact can increase the reported percentage without changing the observations.

The 40.6% is the proportion meeting a defined threshold for change in tumor burden. It cannot be read as a cure rate, a survival probability, or an individual patient’s chance of benefit. Duration of response is another endpoint.

### Why the same 13 responses yield 40.6% and 46.4%

There were 13 partial responses. Among all 32 enrolled and treated participants, 13/32 gives 40.6%. Among the 28 evaluable for efficacy, 13/28 gives 46.4%. Of the four omitted from the evaluable denominator, two stopped after initial treatment before tumor assessment, and two could not be evaluated. Both estimates describe the same responses in explicitly different populations. [Efficacy results][primary]

Excluding early discontinuations or unevaluable patients can affect an estimate, particularly when the reasons relate to treatment or illness. Present both denominators and the reasons for exclusion instead of reporting only the larger percentage.

<strong>All 13 responses counted in the objective response rate were partial responses.</strong> Even a complete response on imaging would describe a finding under the assessment rules and would not establish that disease could never recur.

The disease control rate was 26/32, or 81.3%: 13 partial responses and 13 cases of stable disease. Stable disease means that progression criteria were not met. It need not indicate substantial tumor shrinkage. Disease control therefore includes a broader set of outcomes than objective response. [Efficacy results][primary]

## Why is the confidence interval wide?

In a simple binomial model, observations from n people estimate a response probability p. An approximation to the standard error of the sample proportion is:

<Math display tex={String.raw`\operatorname{SE}(\widehat p)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)}{n}}`} label="Standard error of a sample proportion under the simplifying assumption of independent binomial observations" />

This approximation illustrates the effect of sample size. It is not necessarily the method used to construct the trial’s small-sample confidence interval. Roughly four times as many observations are needed to halve the standard error.

Highlighting 40.6% alone hides the uncertainty shown by the reported interval of 23.7–59.4%. Retain the interval in the original report when interpreting the result.

### What the 95% refers to

A confidence interval describes sampling uncertainty in an estimated population parameter. Its frequentist interpretation concerns how often intervals produced by the same procedure over repeated samples would contain the parameter. It does not assign an individual patient a 95% chance of response.

With 32 participants, one person's outcome moves a percentage by about 3.1 points. The arithmetic supports reporting 40.6% to one decimal place, while the underlying estimate can still carry considerable uncertainty. Read the confidence interval with the percentage.

Larger samples can improve precision without correcting selection or assessment bias. Conversely, a small exploratory trial in a well-defined population can inform the design of a later comparative study.

### Each subgroup has a smaller denominator

Three participants had mismatch repair-deficient disease (dMMR) and 29 had mismatch repair-proficient disease (pMMR). A rate estimated in three people has different precision from the overall rate. Such a subgroup cannot by itself establish a molecular test for treatment selection. [Baseline characteristics][primary]

A comparison of treatment effects across subgroups needs evidence on the difference between the effects. A statistically significant result in one subgroup and a nonsignificant result in another do not establish a significant difference between them.

## Reading the figures

Each bar in a waterfall plot shows a patient’s change in tumor burden. Selecting only the largest reductions loses sight of the denominator. Check who is missing from the graph, whether responses were confirmed, and whether patients were assessed at comparable times.

For progression-free survival (PFS), examine how the analysis handles short follow-up and censoring. An association between a blood biomarker and an outcome does not establish a clinical test that predicts response. A threshold developed and retested on the same small sample can give overly optimistic results.

### Magnitude of response and duration of response

A waterfall plot compares changes in lesion size across participants. A swimmer plot or duration-of-response analysis adds time. The largest shrinkage in one patient represents neither the rest of the group nor the duration of benefit.

Progression-free survival (PFS) measures time to a defined event, such as progression or death. A median of 13.8 months marks the point at which the estimated survival curve reaches one-half. Participants still under follow-up contribute censored observations. The median does not give every participant's duration. [Survival results][primary]

Median overall survival “not reached” means that follow-up and events at the data cutoff were insufficient to locate the median. It does not mean indefinite survival. Compare data maturity and cutoff dates before comparing medians across studies.

### What circulating DNA can and cannot establish

Circulating tumor DNA consists of tumor-derived DNA fragments detectable in blood. This study explored associations between early on-treatment measurements and PFS or overall survival. Those findings do not establish that one blood draw can identify all patients who will benefit. [Exploratory ctDNA analysis][primary]

A marker measured after treatment starts and a pretreatment selection test address different decisions. An observed association between falling levels and better outcomes needs prespecification and external validation of any proposed cutoff before supporting selection of therapy.

## Put response and toxicity side by side

The paper also reports treatment-related adverse events. Even if the percentages of severe adverse events and objective responses look similar, they count different outcomes. Look at timing, duration, dose adjustments, and treatment discontinuation. These observations define the scope of the study.

### Two outcomes can both be 40.6%

Grade 3 or higher treatment-related adverse events occurred in 40.6% of participants. The same percentage was reported for objective response, but the counted events differ and the people need not be identical. Subtracting the rates would not estimate net benefit. Consider response magnitude and duration alongside toxicity type, recovery, and treatment discontinuation. [Safety results][primary]

No dose-limiting toxicities during the initial safety lead-in and later serious adverse events in the full trial can both be true. Group sizes, observation periods, and event definitions differ. Read which events occurred, when, for how long, and whether patients could continue treatment.

## What evidence would strengthen the finding?

A comparable control group, a larger sample, longer follow-up of responses, and replication in relevant patient subgroups would help. The observed rate should not simply be transferred to people with different cancers or previous treatments.

<strong>An encouraging early signal leaves open which patients benefit and how the combination compares with other therapies.</strong> That is the value and the limit of this single-arm study.

### The questions for a comparative study

A follow-up trial could use a comparator reflecting current standards and document prior immunotherapy, durability of response, quality of life, and treatment continuation. This would develop the exploratory signal into evidence for a treatment decision.

One results table can legitimately contain denominators of 32 and 28. Seeing why the percentages differ helps prevent a trial summary from becoming a personal probability. Read the population, comparator, endpoint, and time together.

The findings apply to the advanced endometrial cancer population with the treatment history studied. Extending them to other cancers, stages, or first-line treatment would require new evidence. Individual treatment decisions also depend on pathology, molecular results, and treatment history assessed by the treating team.

When comparative results become available, examine the predefined outcomes, reasons for discontinuation and loss to follow-up, quality of life, and long-term survival alongside response rate. The clinical question is how observed benefits compare with the burden of treatment.
