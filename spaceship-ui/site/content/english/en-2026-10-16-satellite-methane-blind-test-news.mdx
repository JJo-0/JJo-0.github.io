> **Research draft · Single-blind field trial.** The paper appeared in Nature on October 14, 2026. This review compared the public abstract, selected passages of a separate preprint, Sections S3.4 and S3.5 of the official supplement, and parts of its tables. The full final text and the entire 98-page supplement have yet to be checked. Detailed counts from the preprint are treated as specific to that version.

## 1. In one sentence

**Dedicated methane satellites performed well at finding large point sources in clear conditions when analysts roughly knew where to look. Cloud cover, low emission rates and false alarms from automated AI detection each require separate scrutiny before regulators rely on the estimates.**

## 2. Why the measurement is difficult

Methane causes strong warming over short timescales, but leaks can be intermittent and the wind dilutes their plumes. During a brief overpass, a satellite looks for a concentration increase in tiny differences in the absorption of reflected light. Analysts then combine that signal with wind speed to infer an hourly emission rate. Analysts who know the true field release rate can inadvertently tune their thresholds to the answer.

The researchers staged 746 single-blind events over a year in Arizona. These included 481 metered methane releases ranging from 9.5 to 1,183.7 kg/h and 265 blank controls with no release. Fifteen analysis teams in nine countries submitted more than 2,250 estimates from 12 government, commercial and nonprofit satellites.

Several teams could analyze the same field event. That distinction matters when counting false positives below. The number of analysis responses for blank scenes can differ from the 265 distinct blank events.

## 3. Basic concepts

Detection classifies an observation as release present or absent. Sensitivity measures the fraction of actual releases that were detected. The false-positive rate measures how often an analysis reports a release when none occurred.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathrm{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad
\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}`} />

TPR follows the definition of sensitivity printed in Supplement S3.4. FPR is expressed as one minus the specificity defined in that section. TP counts reports that correctly identify a release, FN counts missed releases, FP counts blanks reported as releases, and TN counts blanks correctly identified as empty. TPR uses evaluated responses for release events as its denominator, while FPR uses evaluated responses for blank events. Both rates are dimensionless. A rate is undefined for a combination with a zero denominator.

For quantification the study reports the regression slope relating reported and metered emission rates and the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE). A slope of 1 means reported emissions increase at the same rate as metered emissions. A nonzero intercept can still introduce a fixed bias, and scatter is a separate feature of the data. The slope alone cannot establish accuracy on average or for an individual event.

## 4. Limits of previous evidence

Success stories chosen by operators and ordinary field observations make missed detections hard to count. Calculating false alarms also requires scenes known to contain no release. Even the same image can yield different rates depending on plume masking, wind data and filter thresholds. The trial addressed these weaknesses with blank controls, metered releases and staged unblinding after the submission deadlines.

## 5. The three stages of the trial

First, the field team timed methane releases or blank controls to satellite overpasses and recorded flow and weather. Second, analysis teams submitted detections and emission rates without knowing the field result. Third, the organizers revealed the truth only after each stage and compared purpose-built methane sensors (Group A), hyperspectral sensors repurposed from other applications (Group B) and wide-area multiband scanners (Group C).

The supplement excluded severe timing mismatches and events overlapping with releases for other overpasses. It also excluded events from quantification if the standard deviation of metered flow exceeded 5% of the mean. Clouds were the most common reason analysis teams gave for their own exclusions. The resulting estimates describe performance under documented selection conditions. Operational performance across all opportunities includes those exclusions.

## 6. From release to estimate

The measurement chain runs from a metered release through atmospheric dispersion, a satellite spectrum, plume detection and wind-speed estimation to a result in kg/h. Each step can contribute error. Supplement S3.4 defines accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 and balanced accuracy and reports them for team–satellite combinations. The authors used 10,000 permutations to assess classification significance and 100,000 bootstrap resamples to compare false positives from AI and non-AI methods.

## 7. Results

Dedicated methane satellites had true-positive rates of 92–100% and false-positive rates of 0–11% in acquired imagery after exclusions for clouds and other conditions. The rates are conditional on obtaining an evaluable observation. Quantification slopes ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, with a MAPE of 44%. Repurposed sensors varied more, with detection rates of 62–88% and a MAPE of 46%. Wide-area scanners performed poorly over the release rates in this trial.

In Supplement S3.5, AI methods produced 58 false positives among 302 classification responses to blanks, an FPR of 19.2%. Non-AI methods produced 13 among 317 responses, or 4.1%. Those denominators count responses to field events, many of which were assessed more than once. The 95% bootstrap interval for the difference was 0.1018–0.2004, or roughly 10.18–20.04 percentage points, with `p < 0.00001`. The difference persisted in Group C and was not statistically significant when only Groups A and B were considered. Comparing methods therefore requires attention to sensor group and method mix.

## Why can the same satellite have different detection rates?

Figure S1 in Supplement S3.3.2 recounts releases missed for any reason, including clouds and failed observation requests. The horizontal axis shows metered emission rate, with blue markers for detections and red markers for misses. On this basis GHGSat detected 53.4% and Tanager-1 detected 80.4%. These figures and the earlier clear-condition rates can coexist because different failures enter the denominator.

The results cannot directly supply an annual detection probability for every climate or region. The site, release rates, participating teams and available satellite passes were fixed. Finding a release in an image retained for analysis and finding a release across all trial opportunities answer different questions.

One event in S3.2 with highly variable metered flow remained in the detection analysis but was excluded from quantification. Recognizing that a release occurred and accurately estimating how much escaped are separate tests of the same event.

**Check your understanding.** Does including cloud-obscured scenes show that the algorithm itself performed worse? Including them measures a wider operation that encompasses both acquiring and analyzing imagery.

## Which responses enter the quantification regression?

Supplement S3.9.1 requires at least five true-positive detections for a satellite–team combination to fit a regression. Among models that estimate both a slope and an intercept, the authors distinguish fits using true positives alone from fits that also include false positives. Slopes estimated from different response sets answer different questions.

True negatives are excluded from these quantification regressions. In a real survey, the number of empty locations can grow very large with the area and time surveyed. Adding all of them as points at the origin could pull the fit strongly toward zero. True negatives still enter relevant classification metrics, including the false-positive rate. One response can count in the classification analysis while being excluded from the quantification model.

**Check your understanding.** Does a slope of 1 mean the reported amount is always accurate? Examine the intercept, scatter and included responses as well. Applying a calibration from this trial to another climate or terrain would require separate validation.

## 8. Counterevidence

The abstract attributes 79% of all false positives to AI methods. That percentage describes the composition of false alarms across methods. The number of participating teams, blank responses and sensor groups differed. Some team–satellite combinations had small samples. The supplement also notes that no multiple-comparison correction was applied, so borderline significance could have arisen by chance.

Analysts roughly knew the source location. The trial did not establish performance when searching globally for sources at completely unknown locations, under interference from complex industrial facilities, or through prolonged cloud cover in humid tropical regions.

## 9. Limitations and disclosures

The trial used one Arizona site and reached a maximum release rate of 1,183.7 kg/h. Several systems performed worse when ground-observed cloud cover exceeded 20%. Even the “perfect” result for 40–60% cloud cover in Supplementary Figure S11 was based on only `n=9`. The researchers did not collect the original spectral imagery themselves, and some images require a commercial purchase. Funding came from the Global Methane Hub, the Grantham Foundation, Saudi Aramco and the Stanford Natural Gas Initiative’s industrial membership program. Two authors disclosed ties to a Carbon Mapper committee.

## 10. Practical implications

Controlled releases and genuine blanks let us compare satellite sensor types on detection, false alarms and quantification errors. For a regulator or company interpreting an estimate, the relevant interval should reflect the sensor group, cloud cover, release rate, prior location information and analysis method.

## 11. When to revisit

Reassess after independent blind tests in different climates and terrain, searches for wholly unknown sources, reproduction with the original commercial imagery, larger AI/non-AI comparisons within Groups A and B, or validation under cloud cover above 20%.

## Check your reading

Is “AI accounted for 79% of false positives” the same as “AI had a false-positive rate of 79%”? The first denominator is all false positives. The second would use all AI responses to blanks. From S3.5, the latter rate is 58 ÷ 302 ≈ 19.2%. Changing the denominator changes the question.
