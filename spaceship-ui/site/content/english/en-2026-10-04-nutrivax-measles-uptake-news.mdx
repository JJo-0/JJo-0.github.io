> **Evidence stage.** This cluster-randomized field trial evaluated vaccination uptake. It did not test a new vaccine or measure reductions in measles infections or deaths.

## The result in 30 seconds

Twenty communities in Nigeria tested a strategy that offered nutritional supplementation alongside routine immunization. At endline, cards confirmed receipt of a first measles vaccine dose in 41.2% of control children and 55.3% of NutriVax children. The primary adjusted odds ratio was 2.08 (95% CI 1.30–3.35). Odds compare vaccinated with unvaccinated children. Doubling the odds changes the probability by a different factor. A separate analysis estimated a 20.1-percentage-point increase accounting for baseline and clustering, while subtracting the two endline percentages gives 14.1 points. [Paper, Primary outcome]

The question was whether offering two needed services at the same visit increased immunization uptake. The effect of the supplement on immunity, reductions in infection and death, and effects in other regions require separate evidence.

## Meeting two needs in the same visit

NutriVax is a service strategy that connects nutritional supplementation with routine immunization. The team assigned 20 communities in northern Nigeria to two groups. The program was aimed at children aged 6–23 months. The primary outcome survey covered children aged 12–23 months. Eligibility for services and inclusion in the survey therefore differed.

In the Methods, children actually starting supplementation were aged 6–22 months. Supplementation ended when they reached 23 months or completed 12 months of supplementation. Children with acute malnutrition were referred to treatment. This service model therefore did not prescribe the same preventive supplement to every infant and toddler.

An eligible child could receive supplementation even when the caregiver declined a scheduled vaccination. Receipt of food was independent of accepting the vaccine. Both groups received information about immunization and infant and young-child nutrition. The NutriVax group also received information about the supplement.

## Communities received the random assignments

The trial randomized 20 communities. Its endline survey covered 1,604 children. Children in a community may share health facilities, travel distances and service conditions. Treating all children as independent randomized replicates can misstate uncertainty.

Baseline and endline were separate cross-sectional household surveys. The study did not follow the same 1,604 children and track which individuals changed vaccination status. Connecting the two survey points as if they traced personal trajectories would misread the design.

The May 2024 baseline included 1,597 children, and the May 2025 endline included 1,604. Following community assignment, researchers twice sampled the same age group in those communities. The two reported percentages cannot establish that a particular child received a supplement and subsequently received a vaccine. [Paper, Characteristics of clusters and survey participants]

**Check the diagram.** Should the randomization arrow point to communities or to individual children? It points to communities. Outcomes were measured in children in the assigned communities. Increasing the number of surveyed children and increasing the number of randomized communities change different aspects of the design.

## What exactly doubled?

The reported OR of 2.08 compares vaccination **odds**. For probability p, odds are p/(1−p). This standard definition is background for reading the analysis. The fitted comparison accounts for within-community similarity and baseline coverage. It cannot be interpreted as a direct ratio of vaccination probabilities.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="odds-prerequisite" title="The ratio of vaccinated to unvaccinated" tex={String.raw`\mathrm{odds}=\frac{p}{1-p}`}>

Here p is the probability of vaccination and 1−p the probability of remaining unvaccinated. Both are dimensionless numbers from zero to one. Their ratio is the odds. At a hypothetical p = 0.5, odds are 0.5/(1−0.5) = 1. With hypothetical vaccinated-to-unvaccinated odds of 2:1, probability is 2/(2+1) = 2/3 ≈ 66.7%. These **illustrative values** show why doubling the odds differs from doubling a 50% probability to 100%.

An odds ratio (OR) compares two odds. The study's 2.08 was a cluster-conditional estimate from a mixed-effects model. It cannot be derived simply by inserting the two endline proportions into this definition. The hypothetical starting probability of 50% does not predict trial coverage. [Statistical background and paper, Figure 2 and Statistical analysis]

The 20.1-percentage-point estimate likewise comes from the paper's difference-in-differences analysis. A simple endline difference and a before-and-after change each compare different quantities.

The reported percentages make the comparisons clearer.

| Card-confirmed first measles dose | Baseline survey | Endline survey | Raw change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Control | 29.9% | 41.2% | +11.3 percentage points |
| NutriVax | 25.0% | 55.3% | +30.3 percentage points |

Subtracting the endline proportions gives 14.1 percentage points. Subtracting the two changes gives 19.0 points. The fitted mixed-effects logistic model accounted for differences among communities and their starting levels and produced 20.1 points. Each calculation answers its own question.

**Check the table.** Can the whole 30.3-point rise in the NutriVax group be attributed to the strategy? The control group rose 11.3 points over the same period. Their raw difference in changes is 19.0 points, and the cluster-adjusted estimate is different again. An increase since baseline differs from an additional increase relative to controls.

The primary OR of 2.08 also differs from the additional difference-in-differences OR of 2.39. Choosing whichever is larger obscures the analysis. Read the coverage differences, odds ratios and uncertainty intervals alongside the comparison that produced each one.

<details>
<summary>Graduate level: odds and probability scales in the same model</summary>

The primary outcome was a child's endline vaccination status. Fixed effects represented service assignment and community-level baseline coverage. A random intercept allowed each community its own underlying level and accounted for clustering of observations. The cluster-conditional OR was 2.08 (95% CI 1.30–3.35, p = 0.004). [Paper, Statistical analysis and Figure 2]

The additional difference-in-differences (DiD) model included group, time and group-by-time interaction. The interaction captures differences in change between groups. Its OR was 2.39 (95% CI 1.76–3.25). Contrasts of predicted probabilities from the same model gave 20.1 percentage points. Supplementary Table 4 describes 5,000 parametric bootstrap replicates to estimate the probability-scale confidence interval. Those replicates quantify uncertainty from the fitted model. [Supplementary Table 4]

The public peer-review response also compared models with and without adjustment for baseline coverage. The authors reported 20.1 percentage points with adjustment and 20.2 without it. This comparison suggests little sensitivity to that particular adjustment. It does not resolve every model assumption or source of bias. The authors distinguish baseline adjustment from the random community intercept that accounts for within-cluster correlation. These are the authors' explanations and reanalysis, without independent replication here. [Peer Review File, PDF page 21, response to statistical comment 13]

The 2024 statistical analysis plan specified DiD as a sensitivity or robustness analysis. The published article presented it as an additional analysis. The primary and supplementary analyses have different comparisons and scales. The source describes the model terms in prose. No unverified coefficient or distributional assumption has been added here. [Statistical analysis plan v1.0, pages 19–20]

For primary-analysis uncertainty, plan v1.0 specified a Kenward–Roger adjustment. The published Methods describe profile likelihood for the primary confidence interval and p-value. The Methods explicitly mention a likelihood-ratio test for the cluster-conditional OR in the additional DiD analysis. Those descriptions need to be attached to the analyses where they appear. The material reviewed did not establish the reason for the change or identify a formal amendment. A procedural difference alone does not establish that the result is wrong.

The footnote to Supplementary Table 14 also specifies a profile-likelihood interval and likelihood-ratio test for the primary OR. For multiple secondary endpoints the authors used Benjamini–Hochberg to control the false discovery rate at 5%. This controls a property of the set of discoveries. It does not assign each result a 5% probability of being wrong. The primary endpoint was tested separately. The secondary outcomes therefore should not all be counted as independent confirmatory findings. The footnote does not explain the difference from the original plan. [Supplementary Table 14, PDF page 19, printed page 18]

## How were missing vaccination records counted?

The primary analysis required vaccination-card confirmation. Children without a card or without a first measles dose documented on the card counted as unvaccinated. A service can affect both actual vaccination and retention or completeness of records. The endpoint measured documentation and did not directly test immunity.

At baseline, 384 of 796 control children (about 48%) and 318 of 801 NutriVax children (about 40%) had a card. At endline, adding the two administrative areas in Supplementary Table 7 gives 474/801 (about 59%) in control and 552/803 (about 69%) in NutriVax. These separate cross-sectional totals do not count the same children newly keeping their cards. Absence of a card counted as failure by definition of the endpoint, without missing-value imputation. Documentation differed between groups. [Paper, Characteristics, and Supplementary Table 7, PDF page 12, printed page 11]

Actual receipt also differed from assignment. At least one supplement was reported for 386/803 NutriVax children and 33/801 controls. The paper describes about 4% cross-community contamination. Analysis by assignment evaluates the strategy of offering the services together. It does not isolate an effect among those who actually collected a supplement.

A post hoc comparison of recipients with nonrecipients therefore lacks random assignment. Families with easier access or a stronger inclination to seek services may have collected supplements more often. The authors identify the comparison as post hoc and nonrandomized. Higher vaccination among recipients alone cannot establish the causal effect of collecting a supplement.

**Change one condition.** Suppose two communities have identical actual coverage, but one retains cards more often. Must card-confirmed coverage be the same? The endpoint requires documentation, so differences in record availability can change the measured value. This hypothetical example identifies a measurement pathway. It does not attribute the whole observed effect to better record keeping.

## Randomization and masking serve different purposes

The communities were assigned by public lottery. In each of two administrative areas, five communities went to control and five to NutriVax. Assignment was public. Data collectors were independent of the delivery team and unaware of assignments through baseline. After implementation, caregivers and field staff could tell which service was available. Randomization sets the basis for comparison, whereas masking limits effects of expectations or behavior on observation. [Paper, Randomization and masking]

## What to check next

The trial reported no stockouts of vaccine or supplements during the study, with ALIMA and local authorities coordinating supplies. Whether the result holds under frequent disruptions requires testing. The outcomes paper reserved cost-effectiveness and detailed implementation barriers and facilitators for subsequent analyses. An increase in coverage alone cannot settle value for money. [Paper, Discussion]

Applying the strategy elsewhere requires evidence on access, supply chains and cost. More visits may have different consequences when supplements run out or queues grow. Replication in other settings, separate measures of actual vaccination and record retention, and long-term operating costs would inform that decision. A card-confirmed first-dose coverage of 55.3% alone cannot establish that community infection risk is sufficiently low. [Paper, Discussion]
