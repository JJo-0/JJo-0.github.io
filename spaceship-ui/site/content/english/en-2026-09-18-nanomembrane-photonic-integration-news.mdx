## 1. Bringing a useful optical material onto a silicon chip

A single material rarely does everything that an on-chip optical circuit needs. Guiding light, electrically modulating it, suppressing light traveling in reverse, and converting light into current each favors different physical properties. <strong>The researchers grew crystals separately, released them as thin single-crystal membranes, and integrated them with silicon or silicon nitride photonic circuits.</strong> <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

A paper published in Nature on September 16, 2026, demonstrates electro-optic modulation, magneto-optic functions, and photodetection using single-crystalline nanomembranes of BaTiO₃, CoFe₂O₄, GaAs, and GaN. The advance lies in <strong>separating the environment in which each crystal grows well from the environment in which the finished device operates</strong>. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

This separation gives “heterogeneous photonic integration” a concrete meaning. Instead of growing every functional material directly on the final substrate, the researchers transfer a grown crystal as a membrane to add the desired function. We need to follow both the material’s properties and the behavior of the integrated device.

## 2. The best substrate for growth can differ from the best substrate for use

In a crystal, atoms follow an ordered arrangement. When one crystal grows on another, their atomic spacings and orientations matter. The paper identifies stringent lattice-matching requirements between unlike substrates as a barrier to growing high-quality functional materials on a photonic platform. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Think of a component whose manufacturing jig differs from its final mounting point. With nanomembranes, the desired crystal quality is established under favorable growth conditions and the material is then moved to the photonic circuit. “Membrane” describes the thin functional material attached to the chip. “Single-crystal” identifies the internal order on which its performance depends.

Placing materials side by side is only part of the task. Light traveling in a waveguide must interact with the new material, while the electric or magnetic field must reach the relevant region. <strong>A device requires both a transfer process and a design that brings the optical, electric, or magnetic fields into the right overlap.</strong>

That changes which questions process engineers ask. Does the material retain its properties after transfer? Does the guided light experience enough of its effect? Can losses at the interface cancel the gain? These are questions about applying the result, not claims of measured production performance.

## 3. Three functions the nanomembranes add

Different materials serve different functions. BTO provides electro-optic modulation. CFO supports nonreciprocal magneto-optic behavior. GaAs and GaN extend the range of photodetection. Silicon (Si) and silicon nitride (SiN) serve as photonic platforms for the functional materials. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

| Material and configuration | Demonstrated function | Mechanism to follow |
|---|---|---|
| BTO nanomembrane on Si | Voltage-driven optical modulation | Electric field → refractive-index change → optical signal change |
| CFO nanomembrane on a Si microring | Magneto-optic nonreciprocity and optical isolation | Magneto-optic interaction → direction-dependent optical response |
| GaAs and GaN on SiN | Photodetection from the ultraviolet to the near infrared | Wavelength and material selection → absorption and electrical signal |
| Vertical CFO/BTO heterostructure | Combined electro-optic and magneto-optic functions | Both material responses contribute to a single resonant structure |

The table summarizes a family of devices. The individual demonstrations establish particular material properties and integration methods. They do not establish equal, simultaneous performance of every function in a single general-purpose product.

“Van der Waals integration” describes <strong>a family of methods for connecting different materials to a photonic platform</strong>. The name alone guarantees no particular material performance. Figures 2–4 show devices organized by function, and Figure 5 compares performance. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 4. How does a large BTO coefficient affect light?

In electro-optic modulation, an applied voltage produces an electric field that changes the material’s optical response. The Pockels effect in BTO is a first-order change in refractive index with field. A crystal responds differently along different directions, so a particular reported tensor component and the effective coefficient experienced in a device need to be distinguished.

The paper reports a BTO <Math tex={String.raw`r_{42}`} /> above 1,290 pm/V and a 3-dB electro-optic bandwidth above 23 GHz. Those figures come from integrating single-crystal membranes of controlled quality and orientation with silicon photonics. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<NewsFigure media="photonics-bto" lang="en" />

<CandidateEquation id="photonics-pockels" lang="en" title="How does an electric field produce a change in refractive index?" tex={String.raw`\Delta n\approx-\frac{1}{2}n^3r_{\mathrm{eff}}E`}>

This expression approximates a small change along one effective direction. <Math tex={String.raw`n`} /> is the baseline refractive index, <Math tex={String.raw`E`} /> is the electric field, and <Math tex={String.raw`r_{\mathrm{eff}}`} /> is the effective electro-optic coefficient for the crystal orientation, polarization, and geometry. Set the change in inverse squared index to <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta(1/n^2)=r_{\mathrm{eff}}E`} />. Differentiating <Math tex={String.raw`n^{-2}`} /> gives the first-order term <Math tex={String.raw`-2n^{-3}\Delta n`} />. Solving for the change in index gives the equation above.

The negative sign and factor of <Math tex={String.raw`1/2`} /> arise from converting the inverse-square expression to an index change. Interpreting the sign requires the conventions for field direction, polarization, and coefficient. The reported tensor component <Math tex={String.raw`r_{42}`} /> cannot simply replace <Math tex={String.raw`r_{\mathrm{eff}}`} /> in every geometry.

For a unit example, <strong>assume</strong> <Math tex={String.raw`r_{\mathrm{eff}}=1{,}000\,\mathrm{pm/V}=10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m/V}`} />, <Math tex={String.raw`E=10^5\,\mathrm{V/m}`} />, and <Math tex={String.raw`n=2`} />. The approximation gives <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta n\approx-4\times10^{-4}`} />. The example illustrates the equation and units. Its assumed index and field are not measurements of the paper’s BTO device.

</CandidateEquation>

A change in refractive index changes the phase accumulated by light over a given distance. For wavelength <Math tex={String.raw`\lambda`} /> and interaction length <Math tex={String.raw`L`} />, the small phase shift can be written as <Math tex={String.raw`\Delta\phi=(2\pi/\lambda)\Delta n L`} />. Even a large index change may produce a limited device response if the interaction length is short or the optical mode overlaps little with the material. The material coefficient alone therefore cannot determine modulator performance.

The expression shows the phase change growing with <Math tex={String.raw`L`} />, all else held fixed. Making the device longer also affects loss, capacitance, bandwidth, and footprint. That trade-off helps explain why the study reports both material response and device bandwidth.

## 5. Reading 23 GHz and 3 dB

Bandwidth concerns how quickly a signal can change. Converting a bandwidth in GHz into a numerical bit rate also requires the modulation format and signal-processing conditions. First consider what the 3-dB point in a frequency response measures.

<CandidateEquation id="photonics-bandwidth" lang="en" title="How much has the response fallen at the 3-dB point?" tex={String.raw`20\log_{10}\!\left|\frac{H(f)}{H(f_{\mathrm{ref}})}\right|=-3\ \mathrm{dB}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`H(f)`} /> is the amplitude response at frequency <Math tex={String.raw`f`} />, and <Math tex={String.raw`H(f_{\mathrm{ref}})`} /> is the response at the reference frequency. Their ratio is dimensionless. Taking a base-10 logarithm and multiplying by 20 expresses the amplitude ratio in dB. At equal impedance, power is proportional to amplitude squared, consistent with <Math tex={String.raw`10\log_{10}`} /> for a power ratio.

At an amplitude ratio of <Math tex={String.raw`1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.7071`} />, the result is <Math tex={String.raw`20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt{2})\approx-3.0103\,\mathrm{dB}`} />. That corresponds to about half the reference power. It describes a reduction in normalized response, not a fixed loss of half of all incident photons.

One cycle at 23 GHz lasts about 43.48 ps. That time scale is the reciprocal of frequency. A digital data rate also depends on the signaling format and other system limits, so this calculation does not demonstrate 23 Gb/s communication.

</CandidateEquation>

A large Pockels coefficient characterizes how strongly a field changes the material’s optical response. High bandwidth characterizes how quickly the device responds. In the final system, loss, operating range, and drive conditions matter alongside both.

## 6. What do CFO and the III–V semiconductors add?

Integrating a CFO nanomembrane with a silicon microring demonstrated a nonreciprocal magneto-optic function. The authors report a Faraday rotation coefficient of 33,800°/cm. Connecting GaAs and GaN laterally on SiN also demonstrated photodetection in several wavelength regions from the ultraviolet to the near infrared. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Faraday rotation measures how much the polarization rotates per unit propagation length. A finished isolator’s forward–reverse transmission difference and insertion loss also depend on mode structure, resonance, and coupling. Converting the material coefficient to familiar units therefore answers a narrower question than evaluating the complete isolator.

<CandidateEquation id="photonics-rotation" lang="en" title="What does 33,800°/cm mean over a short path?" tex={String.raw`\vartheta=\Theta_F L,\qquad 33{,}800\,{}^{\circ}/\mathrm{cm}=3.38\,{}^{\circ}/\mathrm{\mu m}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`\Theta_F`} /> is rotation per unit length, <Math tex={String.raw`L`} /> is the interaction length, and <Math tex={String.raw`\vartheta`} /> is the angle. Since <Math tex={String.raw`1\,\mathrm{cm}=10{,}000\,\mathrm{\mu m}`} />, divide 33,800 by 10,000 to convert from degrees per centimeter to degrees per micrometer.

<strong>Assume</strong> an ideal single pass with the reported coefficient applying uniformly. A path of 1 μm would then give 3.38°, and 0.1 μm would give 0.338°. An actual waveguide’s effective interaction and the microring resonance require their own calculations. The numbers illustrate a unit conversion. They do not measure device isolation or insertion loss.

</CandidateEquation>

GaAs and GaN illustrate another form of diversity. Choosing materials suited to different wavelengths expands the capabilities available on one platform. A range from ultraviolet to near infrared says little by itself about whether responsivity, noise, or speed are the same throughout. The relevant device and measurement conditions need to be compared at each wavelength. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The vertical CFO/BTO structure goes beyond placing materials side by side. Stacking two functional membranes combines electro-optic and magneto-optic responses in one resonant structure. This expands the design space and calls for closer attention to the alignment between layers and the optical-field distribution. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 7. The gap between a device demonstration and a manufacturing platform

The most direct evidence is the demonstration of functional single-crystal membranes integrated into photonic devices. A manufacturing platform also requires repeatability. If the same transfer is performed at many positions and across chips, how consistent are the properties, loss, and alignment error? How will failed devices be identified in inspection?

These manufacturing questions are proposals for future evaluation. The public abstract and figure titles do not establish wafer-scale yield, long-term reliability after packaging, or cost per device. <strong>Identifying a metric that matters is different from observing poor performance on it.</strong> Open questions require additional measurements.

A good average coefficient could coexist with chip-to-chip variation large enough to require calibration. Good optical properties could coexist with unstable contacts or electrodes that change drive requirements. Temperature-induced shifts in resonance could require compensation. These are concrete system questions that arise when making use of an integrated device.

Read Figure 5 with the measurement conditions for each device in view. A large material coefficient, a fast device, and a low-cost system can be related, but each has a different unit and measurement boundary. Preserve those units and boundaries when comparing the figures.

### From material properties to circuit metrics

Three levels help organize the evidence. The material level measures responses per unit length or applied electric or magnetic field. The device level adds optical-mode overlap, electrode placement, resonance, and coupling. The system level includes drive circuitry, thermal control, interconnections, and losses between source and detector.

Consider two hypothetical devices made with the same material. One provides strong optical overlap but has slow electrodes. The other responds quickly electrically but has weak optical overlap. Their material coefficients can match while their modulation efficiencies and bandwidths differ. The example explains the need for separate metrics and supplies no new measurement of the paper’s devices.

For a proposed low-power optical link, ask what voltage produced the required optical change, and what speed and loss accompanied it. The same reasoning applies when translating magneto-optic rotation into isolator performance.

For photodetection, distinguish the range over which a signal is detectable from the linearity of output versus incident power, noise in darkness, and response to rapid changes. Each calls for its own measurement. These are ways follow-up manufacturing and system studies can build on the material combinations shown here.

### A worked calculation of optical phase

Use the earlier assumed magnitude <Math tex={String.raw`|\Delta n|=4\times10^{-4}`} /> and a uniform path with complete overlap between the optical field and the material. Taking <Math tex={String.raw`\lambda=1.55\,\mathrm{\mu m}`} /> and <Math tex={String.raw`L=1{,}000\,\mathrm{\mu m}`} /> gives a phase-change magnitude of <Math tex={String.raw`2\pi\times0.4/1.55\approx1.62`} /> rad. The 0.4 μm in the numerator is the change in optical path length, obtained by multiplying the index change by the physical length. Dividing by wavelength gives the fraction of a cycle, and <Math tex={String.raw`2\pi`} /> converts cycles to radians.

Halving the length under the same assumptions halves the phase change. If the mode overlaps only part of the functional film, even at the same physical length the calculation requires an effective index change that accounts for where the light is. <strong>The 1.62 rad result is a hypothetical illustration of the path from a material coefficient to an optical signal.</strong> It is separate from the measured device results.

The calculation separates five steps: applied field, effective electro-optic coefficient, index change, optical path change, and output phase. This makes it easier to identify which information is missing when predicting performance. Reading the device geometry and assigning each number to its proper step also helps compare a material record with an integrated-device result.

## 8. What matters next is the quality of integration

Silicon need not supply every optical function directly. <strong>Bringing suitable functional crystals to the platform that carries the light</strong> allows materials and circuit roles to be designed separately. The interfaces that turn an individual material response into a circuit function then become particularly important.

Look for repeatable combinations of materials on the same platform, consistent verification of crystal quality and orientation after transfer, and the combination of response strength, bandwidth, and loss achieved in the same device. Evidence linking a laboratory demonstration to a repeatable process gives “platform” a manufacturing meaning.
