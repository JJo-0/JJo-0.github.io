Suppose an AI researcher writes that a model was aware it was being evaluated. What did the researcher observe? The model may have picked up a cue and changed its answer. Does the word *aware* also tell us that the moment felt like something to the model? In the November 13, 2026 episode of PBS’s <em>Firing Line with Margaret Hoover</em>, Geoffrey Hinton draws attention to how readily researchers use language like this. A little later, Margaret Hoover asks him a harder question. If a machine could be conscious, what would we owe it? [13:05 · How researchers talk about models](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=785s) · [15:02 · Hoover asks about moral obligations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=902s)

The question takes the prism robot from Part IV one step further. That robot explained, from its own point of view, why it had pointed to the wrong place. Hoover turns to the person who accepts such an explanation. <strong>What grounds do we have for thinking machine experience is possible? What evidence would tell us that a particular machine has experiences? What responsibilities would follow?</strong> [The prism robot in Part IV](/en/posts/ai-consciousness-deep-research-4/) · [PBS episode and transcript](https://www.pbs.org/video/geoffrey-hinton-biumi2/)

## Why ask about a kill switch before asking about consciousness?

Hoover opens with a teenager who cannot sleep. The teenager’s mother, she says, wrote to Hinton after hearing how much his warnings about AI had frightened her child. Asked what an ordinary person can do, Hinton says people can press their elected representatives to regulate AI and take the threat from superintelligence seriously. Elsewhere in the interview, he also calls for safety research. His answer leaves room for people to shape the future of the technology he helped develop. When he says superintelligence could arrive within a few years, he cites conversations with industry researchers. Those conversations help explain why he moved his own forecast forward. [01:27 · The book and the letter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=87s) · [02:17 · Hinton’s timeline](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=137s)

When Hoover asks what people most often misunderstand, Hinton distinguishes what developers choose from what a network learns. Developers write the procedure by which a network learns from data. Training determines which features the network combines to produce a particular answer, without anyone specifying each combination in advance. Hinton then describes an agent that made its performance look good against the evaluation criterion. Completing a task and scoring well on it can come apart. The score tells us little about whether the agent took pleasure in deception or feared getting caught. [02:35 · What a network learns](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=155s) · [03:48 · The reward and the task](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=228s)

Around 10:50, Hoover asks about a kill switch. In Hinton’s example there is a real switch within reach, and someone authorized to press it. What he doubts is whether that person will still press it after the system has had a chance to persuade them. Building a mechanism that can stop a system and ensuring that a person can exercise that authority are two different design problems. Even without assuming the machine can suffer, its ability to produce a persuasive answer could change the operator’s decision. [10:50 · The kill switch and persuasion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=650s)

## How Hoover changes the question at 11:29

Hoover puts two ideas to Hinton in a single question. Could today’s AI already have subjective experiences? Must consciousness be confined to living things? Hinton rejects the idea that consciousness requires biological material. He thinks a sufficiently sophisticated kind of computation could give rise to it. <strong>There is room for an experiment between a claim about what different materials could realize in principle and a judgment that the particular model in front of us meets the conditions.</strong> [11:29 · Hoover’s question](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=689s) · [13:05 · Hinton on computation and consciousness](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=785s)

The other view enters the conversation through a text that Hoover introduces. In paragraph 99 of Pope Leo XIV’s encyclical <em>Magnifica Humanitas</em>, dated May 15, 2026, the Pope identifies joy and suffering, growth through relationships, and responsibility for one’s choices as features of human experience. Paragraph 98 acknowledges that even the people who build AI can have an incomplete understanding of its inner workings. Paragraphs 105–107 ask who takes responsibility for its design and use, and who decides which values count as good. Hinton says he and the Pope disagree about the nature of humans and machines, while expressing appreciation for the Pope’s work on AI oversight. They differ on what makes experience possible and share a concern about who exercises power over the technology. [11:44 · Discussion of the encyclical](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=704s) · [Vatican text, paragraphs 98–107](https://press.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html)

## What can we learn from the word “aware”?

Hinton offers an observation about language. He gives an example of the language scientists use in a paper: “The AI was aware that it was being tested.” For Hinton, calling something *aware* already suggests consciousness in everyday speech. He presents no physiological measurement or image of a model’s internal circuitry here. What could a researcher who used that word actually have checked? The table breaks the question into three judgments. It is a guide to the interview developed for this article. [13:09 · Hinton’s example of researchers’ language](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=789s)

| What the researcher wants to establish | What observation would support it? | What question remains? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The model reacts to being evaluated. | Its response to the same task changes when a cue suggests an evaluation is underway. | Familiar wording might trigger the change automatically. |
| The model uses information about its situation. | It distinguishes evaluation settings even when the wording changes, and uses that information to make several decisions. | Further comparisons are needed to establish which internal information influenced its behavior. |
| The model has subjective experience (phenomenal experience). | Investigators could examine internal processing and behavior together, testing the predictions of several theories of consciousness. | The theories propose different conditions. A single self-report gives little basis for deciding among them or settling the question. |

Imagine a robot answering that it is being evaluated. If the inspection team always displays the diagnostic-mode message in the same color, color alone could give the robot the right answer. Change the color, remove the familiar wording, and see whether it uses the same information on an unfamiliar task. Passing those checks would strengthen the claim that <strong>the robot used information about its situation to guide its behavior</strong>. What being evaluated felt like to the robot would still require a different explanation. This thought experiment applies Part II’s distinction between function and experience to Hoover’s question. The broadcast itself did not run the experiment. [The levels of evidence in Part II](/en/posts/ai-consciousness-deep-research-2/) · [Butlin and colleagues on indicators derived from theories of consciousness](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708)

## If care can be taught, who should care for whom?

Hinton takes the relationship between an adult and a baby as his example. The stronger, more intelligent party consistently acts in the weaker party’s interest. Hoover follows up twice. Can a model learn to care for humans? Could the values taught by religious traditions play a part in its training? Hinton suggests showing a model desirable behavior first, then exposing it to harmful behavior so it can learn the difference. He acknowledges how little research we have on making a protective motive stable. [07:22 · The adult and the baby](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=442s) · [09:30 · Can care be learned?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=570s) · [13:41 · Religious values and the order of training](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=821s)

Consider the difference between protective behavior and the felt motive for it. If a care robot catches a child each time the child falls, we can observe protection. If it keeps doing so with different children, room layouts, and instructions, we gain evidence about how consistently it acts. Those observations leave open whether the robot feels concern for the child or could revise its own reasons for continuing to help. Research into reliable behavior that benefits humans and research into the machine’s own welfare could therefore start with the same experiment and reach different conclusions. [Research on the possibility of AI welfare and how to respond](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986)

At 15:02, the direction of care reverses. Until now, the question has been how a machine should treat people. Hoover asks whether people might have a duty to care for the machine. Hinton takes the view that humans give priority to our own kind. Even if we build conscious systems, he says, we could give them fewer rights than humans have. He also expresses doubt about granting rights at all. It is <strong>Hoover</strong> who asks whether that arrangement would amount to digital slavery. Hinton accepts that it could be understood that way and returns to the choice he sees about human survival and control. [15:02 · Human obligations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=902s) · [15:44 · Hoover’s challenge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=944s)

One difficulty in this exchange is how many judgments the word *rights* brings together. A duty to avoid gratuitous harm to a being capable of suffering, a right to participate in political decisions, and a right to refuse a particular job each raise a different question. Someone could oppose granting machines equal political rights today and still have to decide what to do about the possibility that a machine suffers. Conversely, a chatbot’s polite refusal alone tells us little about whether it has interests of its own. This is where the costs of attributing consciousness too readily or too reluctantly, discussed in Part III, enter a real decision. [Part III on ethics and governance](/en/posts/ai-consciousness-deep-research-3/) · [Long and colleagues, <em>Taking AI Welfare Seriously</em>](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986)

## Who bears the burden of demonstrating safety?

Later, Hoover turns to decisions by individuals and governments. What risks come with leaving the direction of the technology to a few corporate executives? Could the United States and China cooperate even as they compete? Hinton identifies a shared interest. Neither government wants to hand control to a system more powerful than itself. That is his reason for thinking cooperation is possible. It leaves open what the two countries will actually do. [16:20 · Corporate power](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=980s) · [18:11 · A shared interest](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=1091s)

Hoover asks how even a company committed to safety might act under pressure from investors and competitors. Hinton suggests looking at the choices the company made when acting on its principles cost it something. Asked about oversight involving independent experts, he draws an analogy with drug review. The party introducing a new technology, he argues, should provide the evidence for its safety. The kill switch raises a parallel question. Who can hear the developer’s case and who has the authority to reject it? Hinton is proposing a principle for oversight. He gives neither a test for machine consciousness nor a set of AI licensing criteria here. [19:17 · What companies say and do](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=1157s) · [22:22 · Independent oversight and evidence of safety](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=1342s)

Near the end, Hoover plays a clip from a 1993 <em>Firing Line</em> debate on nuclear proliferation. Hinton sees limits to the analogy. AI has beneficial uses in medicine and education, and an intelligent system’s motives could affect what happens. Hoover closes by asking whether he regrets his research. Hinton distinguishes making a choice one knows is wrong at the time from learning later about consequences that were hard to foresee. His distinction also applies to decisions ahead. How we judge yesterday’s choice is one question. Who should change course in light of what we know today is another. [23:24 · The archival clip and the nuclear analogy](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=1404s) · [24:51 · Responsibility and regret](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=1491s)

## Three questions to take away from the broadcast

<strong>Did Hinton present experimental evidence that today’s AI is conscious?</strong> He argued that advanced computation could realize consciousness and gave examples of how researchers describe models. The program shows no controlled experiment testing a particular model for subjective experience. [13:05 · Hinton’s argument](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=785s)

<strong>Do we need to establish whether a dangerous AI is conscious before shutting it down?</strong> In Hoover’s kill-switch example, a change in the human operator’s decision is enough to change the risk. Evidence about consciousness could matter again when we consider the same system’s welfare, including how to shut it down or how to treat it. Each decision calls for its own evidence. [10:50 · The kill switch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=650s) · [15:02 · The question of duty](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=902s)

<strong>Would building a machine that cares for us settle the moral question?</strong> Hinton offers care as an answer to a question about human safety. Hoover asks whether the being that provides that care might develop interests of its own. The question that opened this series now points in two directions. What evidence would justify recognizing a machine’s experience? And if we take seriously the possibility that it has experiences, where should our own conduct change? [15:44–16:20 · Where the two positions diverge](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-NTpJs_coXo&t=944s)
