**The short version.** Even a phone can stop charging when it slips off its pad. Powering an implant is harder because skin and heart tissue move. Here, **an external circuit finds a suitable frequency as a soft internal coil stretches**. The researchers demonstrated function in rabbits and pigs. Evidence for a human product would require further testing.

<BeginnerGuide lang="en" title="New to wireless charging, resonance or PTE? Start with three diagrams">

<WearableImplantWptPrimer lang="en" />

<strong>Wireless power transfer (WPT)</strong> sends energy by electromagnetic
coupling across a gap. Here, a wearable transmitter creates an alternating magnetic field at the skin.
An implanted receiver beneath the skin harvests the field to supply electrical power.

- The <strong>transmitter (Tx)</strong> is the external coil and drive circuit generating the alternating field.
- The <strong>receiver (Rx)</strong> is the internal coil and implant circuit receiving AC power from the field.
- <strong>Resonance</strong> is a condition under which transmitter and receiver exchange energy efficiently
  at a particular frequency.
- <strong>Misalignment</strong> is a positional or angular offset between the coils.
- <strong>Strain</strong> measures relative extension. At 30% strain, a soft implant initially 10 mm long
  extends to 13 mm.
- <strong>PTE</strong> is received power divided by transmitted power at specified measurement boundaries, or power-transfer
  efficiency

PT symmetry here concerns the dynamics of two coupled resonators, one with transmitter gain and the other with receiver loss. The circuit moves to a new operating frequency as position, deformation or load changes. It is a classical circuit design. The <a href="#news-ref-1">original article [1]</a> and <a href="#news-ref-2">supplement [2]</a> provide the evidence.

</BeginnerGuide>

### Read the diagram first: where does energy travel?

The blue coil is outside the body and the green receiver is inside. Curved arrows depict magnetic coupling. The enlargement at right is an **illustration** of a liquid-metal conductor accommodating deformation in an elastic medium. It does not reproduce the device's shape, scale or implantation depth. The power source remains outside, and harvested energy runs the implanted circuit.

Read in three steps: **① why movement weakens power transfer → ② what the circuit and material change → ③ why 50% and 6% measure different efficiencies**. Expand the equation explanations for the calculations.

## 1. In one sentence: a power link that allows for movement

On September 25, 2026, *Nature Electronics* published a study combining a **self-oscillating nonlinear parity–time (PT) symmetric transmitter and a stretchable liquid-metal receiver**. The transmitter adjusts frequency in real time as the receiver stretches or the coils shift. The authors report coil-to-coil power-transfer efficiency (PTE) above 50% with 30% strain and 30 mm lateral misalignment, and demonstrate wireless cardiac pacing and termination of tachyarrhythmia in rabbits and pigs.<Cite n={1} />

The evidence spans <strong>device and circuit testing plus preclinical in vivo demonstrations in rabbits and pigs</strong>. Human clinical trials and approval for long-term implantation remain separate milestones. Public source data include selected measurements for Figures 2 and 3 and Extended Data Figures 4 and 5. The Arduino IDE code for the microcontroller is available on request. This distinction matters when assessing the completeness of a reproduction package.<Cite n={1} />

Movement can produce a chain of events: `coil misalignment or deformation → detuning → power interruption`. The authors addressed it by **changing both the adaptive circuit and the soft conductor**. These two design choices explain the engineering advance and its current clinical limits.
The official *Nature Electronics* Research Briefing likewise presents the work as addressing the sensitivity of soft-implant power links to movement and deformation.<Cite n={3} />

## 2. Why is transfer into the body harder than a bench test?

Two fixed, facing coils on a bench can maintain their separation, angle and load. On the body, the wearable can slide, skin, muscle and heart can move, and the implant can bend or stretch. Changes in separation and alignment alter magnetic coupling. Deforming the receiver can also change inductance and resistance and move it away from the original resonance.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

Near-field inductive coupling (NIC) can be efficient at shallow depth with aligned coils and is used in medical devices. The challenge is a fixed-frequency system without active tuning under several simultaneous changes in moving soft tissue. The supplement describes conventional NIC weakening markedly with displacements of several centimeters or lateral offsets around 5 mm under cited conditions. Those values are specific to the comparisons reviewed.<Cite n={2} />

Removing an implanted battery leaves an external energy source and transmitter. Harvested power must pass through a rectifier and regulator to reach implant electronics. Coil-to-coil efficiency, rectification efficiency, regulated output and actual stimulation energy need separate measurement boundaries.

## 3. What is resonance, and why does movement detune it?

A coil with inductance <Math tex={String.raw`L`} /> and a capacitor with capacitance <Math tex={String.raw`C`} /> exchange energy between magnetic and electric fields at a characteristic frequency. The elementary LC resonator gives the expression below. The actual study circuit adds resistance, load, nonlinear gain and other components.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="wpt-resonance" title="Why does a stretching coil change the tuned frequency?" tex={String.raw`f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\tag{1}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`f_0`} /> is the resonant frequency of an ideal LC circuit, <Math tex={String.raw`L`} /> its coil
inductance and <Math tex={String.raw`C`} /> the capacitance. When stretching changes coil geometry,
<Math tex={String.raw`L`} /> and parasitic capacitance can change. Changes in conductor length and cross section
also affect resistance.

Frequency is in hertz (Hz), inductance in henries (H) and capacitance in farads (F). This natural-frequency expression neglects resistive losses. Computing efficiency through two coupled coils, tissue, a rectifier and a nonlinear amplifier requires a larger model than this simple circuit.

For an illustrative 21% increase in inductance with capacitance unchanged, the new frequency is <Math tex={String.raw`1/\sqrt{1.21}=1/1.1`} /> of the original, or about 90.9%. This example does not infer the study device's inductance from measurements.

In the supplement, the measured resonance of the liquid-metal (LM) receiver fell from 2.64 MHz at 0% strain to 2.39 MHz at 50% strain, a shift of about 9.5%. A transmitter fixed at 2.64 MHz would then operate away from the receiver's strongest response.<Cite n={2} />

</CandidateEquation>

Distance and misalignment mainly alter coupling between coils. Changing receiver load also changes the loss condition. Resonance, coupling and loss thus vary together in the body. The study uses a self-oscillating feedback loop that follows a stable frequency of the coupled system without measuring every change and applying a lookup-table correction.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

## 4. PT symmetry in a classical gain–loss circuit

Parity–time symmetry is often associated with quantum mechanics. Here it provides a mathematical framework for a **classical electrical circuit** coupling a transmitter resonator with gain to a receiver resonator with loss. Coupled modes can exchange energy stably under specific relationships between gain, loss and coupling.<Cite n={1} />

The wearable transmitter uses self-oscillating gain and real-time feedback. As coupling changes, the oscillation frequency moves to a new operating point. A time-domain simulation in the supplement follows the transient departure from steady state and adaptation to a new steady oscillation after an abrupt coupling change.<Cite n={2} />

Self-adaptation operates within limits. If coupling falls below receiver loss, the PT-symmetric regime breaks and efficiency can drop sharply. Excessive separation, a very small receiver coil or high loss imposes physical limits on feedback. This condition makes receiver coil size an important constraint in the present device.<Cite n={2} />

Think of two connected swings. A push represents energy supplied by the transmitter, and friction represents receiver loss. **Limit of the analogy.** The actual circuit exchanges energy through AC voltages and magnetic fields. The equations below quantify that relationship. The design replaces a forced, fixed-frequency drive with self-oscillating feedback under the same electromagnetic laws.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="wpt-coupled-modes" title="For students and researchers: when coupling exceeds loss, two resonant modes persist" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}a_S\\a_R\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}i\omega_S+g_S&-i\kappa\\-i\kappa&i\omega_R-\gamma_R\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_S\\a_R\end{pmatrix}\\\omega_\pm&=\omega_0\pm\sqrt{\kappa^2-\gamma_R^2}\quad(\kappa>\gamma_R)\end{aligned}\tag{3}`}>

The source is **Supplementary Note 2, printed pages 6–8, Equations S1 and S2 and the two cases analyzed below them**. The first line is a **differential equation governing the time evolution of the coupled modes**. <Math tex={String.raw`a_S,a_R`} /> are the energy-normalized transmitter and receiver amplitudes, in <Math tex={String.raw`\sqrt{\mathrm J}`} />. Time <Math tex={String.raw`t`} /> is in seconds and <Math tex={String.raw`i`} /> is the imaginary unit. <Math tex={String.raw`\omega_S,\omega_R`} /> are the angular resonant frequencies (rad/s), <Math tex={String.raw`g_S`} /> the transmitter gain rate, <Math tex={String.raw`\gamma_R`} /> the receiver loss rate and <Math tex={String.raw`\kappa`} /> the energy coupling rate. The last three quantities have units of inverse seconds. Multiplying the amplitude vector by the matrix gives the rate of change of each amplitude.<Cite n={2} />

Diagonal entries describe the gain or loss of each oscillating coil. Off-diagonal <Math tex={String.raw`-i\kappa`} /> terms describe their mutual influence. The paper's normalization gives <Math tex={String.raw`\kappa=\omega k/2`} />, where <Math tex={String.raw`k`} /> is the dimensionless coupling coefficient. Misalignment generally weakens this coupling. Changes in receiver resistance or load change the loss rate.

The second line requires **matched natural frequencies**, <Math tex={String.raw`\omega_S=\omega_R=\omega_0`} />, nonlinear gain saturated to <Math tex={String.raw`g_{S,\mathrm{sat}}=\gamma_R`} /> at steady state, and coupling stronger than loss. Then the two allowed operating frequencies <Math tex={String.raw`\omega_+,\omega_-`} /> follow. Changing coupling changes the square-root term, letting the circuit follow a new mode. The condition to remember is **coupling strength greater than energy loss**.

As a hypothetical example, for <Math tex={String.raw`\kappa=2\gamma_R`} /> the mode's frequency-offset magnitude is <Math tex={String.raw`\sqrt{3}\gamma_R`} />. This illustrates the dependence without inferring experimental MHz values. For <Math tex={String.raw`\kappa<\gamma_R`} /> the real-frequency-pair interpretation no longer applies, and the supplement treats weak coupling separately. A stretched receiver can also detune the two natural frequencies, requiring the supplement's analysis of unmatched resonances.

A fixed-frequency drive holds its input frequency. An externally tuned system uses sensors and a controller to estimate resonance. This circuit finds its operating point through self-oscillation of the coupled system. The contribution is a **system implementation connecting established coupled-mode theory to a soft receiver and a working circuit**.

</CandidateEquation>

## 5. What problem does the liquid-metal receiver solve?

Even a circuit that follows resonance can lose delivered power when receiver resistance increases. The team built an LM coil and interconnects into an elastomer and integrated commercial components, a rectifier, regulator, microcontroller and stimulation electrode. The liquid conduction path accommodates deformation, a different strategy from making rigid copper traces stretch through a serpentine layout.<Cite n={1} />

The combined deformation test subjected the LM–elastomer conductor to simultaneous 30% stretch, a 2 cm bending radius and three turns of twist over 5,000 cycles. The normalized resistance of three specimens tested mechanical and electrical fatigue under those conditions. Years of exposure to fluids, immune responses or encapsulation damage in a chronic implant remain separate tests.<Cite n={2} />

Stretching still shifts resonance: at 50% strain it fell from 2.64 MHz to 2.39 MHz. Liquid metal helps limit resistive losses under deformation and provides a mechanical platform on which frequency adaptation can work. It does not eliminate strain-induced detuning.

## 6. What did the headline experiment measure?

The main paper reported **coil-to-coil PTE above 50% with 30% mechanical strain and 30 mm lateral misalignment**. Additional tests covered bending, tilt, rotation and changing load. Extended Data Figure 5 specifies a single device (n=1) for the bending, tilt and rotation measurements. This sample cannot characterize manufacturing variability across devices.<Cite n={1} />

In rabbits, a receiver placed beneath the skin of the back was tested across positions. In pigs, the wearable transmitter was mounted on the back. LED activation and power delivery were evaluated at axial separations up to 40 mm and lateral offsets up to 30 mm. The team also reported cardiac pacing and termination of tachyarrhythmia through an epicardial electrode.<Cite n={1} />

The animal demonstration went beyond switching on an LED at a fixed site in anesthetized tissue: the team connected a wearable module to a freely moving pig. Animal numbers, observation time, implant positions and tissue thicknesses still limit transfer to a patient population. ## 7. Why 50% PTE and 0.4 W → 24 mW give different numbers

<NewsFigure media="sep28-wpt-cover" lang="en" />

**Follow the power path:** external input → transfer between coils → rectification → voltage regulation → implant electronics. “Above 50%” refers to transfer between coils, and “24 mW” to a separate measurement after voltage regulation. Their appearance together in the diagram does not provide a complete loss measurement under identical conditions.

Efficiency depends on its numerator and denominator. Headline PTE describes AC power transfer between resonators. Power must still pass a rectifier and regulator to reach the implant as regulated DC, with losses at each step.<Cite n={1} /><Cite n={2} />

Supplementary Note 7 reports 0.4 W transmitter input and 24 mW regulated output for the in vivo electronics demonstration. With 24 mW written as 0.024 W, their ratio is:

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="wpt-end-to-end" title="What is the overall ratio for 24 mW out of 0.4 W in?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}\eta_{\mathrm{demo}}&=\frac{P_{\mathrm{regulated\ out}}}{P_{\mathrm{transmitter\ in}}}\\&=\frac{0.024\ \mathrm W}{0.4\ \mathrm W}=0.06=6\%\end{aligned}\tag{2}`}>

The denominator is transmitter input power. The numerator is regulated output after the rectifier and regulator, supplied to stimulation and control electronics. The calculation divides the input and output boundaries reported for this particular in vivo demonstration.<Cite n={2} />

PTE above 50% and the calculated 6% cover different boundaries and conditions. PTE describes transfer between resonators, while 6% spans transmitter input to regulated DC output. Rectification, regulation, impedance matching and circuit consumption intervene.

This ratio also does not imply that tissue absorbed all of the remaining 94% as heat. Losses arise in transmitter drive electronics, coil resistance, mismatch, rectification and regulation. A detailed loss budget would require measuring each stage under the same conditions.

</CandidateEquation>

The delivered 24 mW powered the demonstrated stimulation and control circuit. Neural recording, multichannel stimulation, sensors or communications would change demand. A working pacing demonstration establishes function for this load.

## 8. Size and safety remain bottlenecks

First comes miniaturization. Under current material conductivity and system power requirements, Supplementary Note 5 estimates a minimum receiver coil diameter of about **60 mm** to maintain near-unity transfer at a target depth of 15 mm. This is a substantial physical dimension for an implant.<Cite n={2} />

Note 9 simulates higher LM conductance and replacement of the LDO with a more efficient switching regulator. Under those assumptions a roughly 15 mm receiver coil might operate at 15 mm depth. This is a **projection assuming improved materials and circuitry**. The current 60 mm estimate and projected 15 mm coil have different levels of experimental support.<Cite n={2} />

The safety evidence also has defined boundaries. At 0.4 W input, the maximum averaged SAR of about 0.02 W/kg comes from an **Ansys HFSS electromagnetic simulation** with 3 mm skin and 7 mm muscle layers. The authors compare this with a localized SAR guideline of 2 W/kg averaged over 10 g. The value depends on geometry, material properties and averaging assumptions. It was not measured during long-term human use.<Cite n={2} />

The supplement also includes thermal validation during continuous WPT, ion-leaching tests and EMI and spectral analyses. These address specific engineering questions. Chronic encapsulation failure, fibrosis, infection, lead and electrode fatigue, patient-specific tissue thickness and interference with concurrent devices still require assessment.

## 9. What should be measured beyond peak PTE?

Can an actual receiver in the 10–20 mm size range deliver the required regulated power? Can an end-to-end efficiency and stage-by-stage loss budget be measured from transmitter battery to implant load under consistent conditions? Distributions across alignment, strain, load and tissue thickness are more informative than a single peak PTE.

Chronic operation over weeks or months in freely moving large animals would test the stability of coils, encapsulation, electrodes and tissue responses together. Independent teams also need to reproduce the reported tolerance to misalignment and deformation. Human use requires evidence on heating, SAR, EMI, biocompatibility, sterility and medical-device reliability.

If those conditions are met, the approach could extend to neural stimulators, gastrointestinal electronics, electroceutical therapy and closed-loop biosensors designed as coordinated wearable and implanted systems. The present achievement is a **power architecture that treats motion as a design input**.
