## Is reflecting sunlight enough?

Sunlight heats a power line, and the current flowing through the conductor heats it from within. A reflective surface helps only so much if that internal heat struggles to escape. The h-BN–PDMS coating in this study addresses heat transport from the interior to the surface as well as solar reflection and infrared emission.

Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) forms the particles, and PDMS is a silicone polymer. Follow the path of the heat: the particle–polymer coating conducts it toward a surface that reflects sunlight and emits heat in the infrared. Radiative cooling uses thermal radiation to carry energy away without circulating a refrigerant.

**Distinguish the two kinds of evidence.** The researchers measured the temperatures of mock cables outdoors. Separately, they used weather data to model current-carrying capacity, or ampacity. The maximum increase of 18.3% was calculated **relative to a standard insulated cable** under July conditions in Riyadh. It was not measured on an operating transmission line.

## Three steps from experiment to calculation

First, three commercial cable types were placed outdoors without current. In Thuwal, Saudi Arabia, on July 3–5, 2025, with midday solar irradiance around 1,100 W/m², the coated cable was about 5°C cooler than bare aluminum and 15°C cooler than the insulated cable. This test examines sunlight and heat loss from the surface.

Next, the team used mock cables with internal resistive heating. Figure 4e shows the temperature record for August 2–8, 2025. The test includes internally generated heat but was conducted outside the operating grid. The 10- and 20-year sag values in Figure 4g are catenary-model predictions, rather than measurements accumulated over those periods.

Finally, the authors fed weather and conductor conditions into the CIGRE 601 heat-balance model to calculate ampacity. They report annual-average daytime increases of 6–10% for 13 representative hot cities. The peak value of 18.3% applies to July in Riyadh with an insulated cable as the reference. Other cities and seasons need their own comparisons.

The model treats temperature across the ACSR 450 AlFe 8 conductor section as approximately uniform. Inputs in Note S21 include temperature, solar irradiance, wind speed, and wind direction from Visual Crossing. Weather changes by the hour, but the heat balance at each time assumes steady state. The model does not independently validate transient responses to rapid weather changes.

## Separating reflection from thermal emission

A cooler white surface alone cannot distinguish solar reflection from infrared emission. The control experiment in Note S15 compared black insulated, bare aluminum, and coated samples under the same outdoor conditions and heating loads. Aluminum absorbs relatively little sunlight but is also a weak infrared emitter. Comparing it with the coated sample helps isolate cooling that reflection alone cannot explain.

An insulated box and a thin polyethylene windscreen reduced environmental conduction and convection. The arrangement helped distinguish radiative effects. An exposed line would experience different wind conditions. The coated sample was also cooler than aluminum at night, when solar reflection contributes almost nothing. The authors attribute part of that difference to infrared emission.

### Cooler than which reference?

In a separate test in Note S16, a coated sample without electrical heating was up to about 6°C cooler than **ambient air** at midday. Air was the reference in this test. The approximately 84 W/m² in the same section is net radiative cooling power density calculated from optical properties and environmental conditions. It is neither measured transmission power nor ampacity.

These values belong to different experiments from the internally heated mock cable, the comparison of unloaded commercial cables, and the city-level ampacity model. The 6°C result does not establish that an energized line will always remain below ambient temperature.

**Check your understanding.** Why compare temperatures at night? If a difference persists without sunlight, infrared emission becomes a plausible contributor. Match the heating load and environmental conditions before drawing that inference.

## How the coating changes the path of heat

The paper represents conduction, convection, and radiation as thermal resistances. Heat first conducts through the coating. At the surface, convection carries heat into the air and radiation carries heat away in parallel. The electrical-resistance analogy helps with the structure, but the quantities and units describe heat flow.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="cable-total-thermal-resistance" title="Paper, Equation (1): from the conductor to its surface and then to the surroundings" tex={String.raw`R_{\mathrm{tot}}=R_{\mathrm{cond}}+\left(\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{conv}}}+\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{rad}}}\right)^{-1}`}>

R_tot is total thermal resistance, R_cond is conductive resistance, and R_conv and R_rad are convective and radiative resistance. Combine terms defined over the same length and boundary. Whole-object resistance is in K/W. Any length normalization must be consistent. Supplementary Note S1 specifies cable length L and surface area A. Here, for the full length, surface resistances are 1/(hA) and 1/(hᵣA), with h and hᵣ in W/(m²·K). In the rendered supplement, the expanded surface-resistance expressions omit L, and the radiation line repeats the R_conv label. This article uses the area-based definitions with an explicit length convention. The printed expansions were not used to reproduce a numerical result.

The two reciprocal surface resistances add because heat can leave by either convection or radiation. Taking the reciprocal again gives their combined resistance. Conduction through the coating precedes both paths and adds in series. This is the paper’s model.

Increasing coating thickness can increase surface area but also lengthen the conduction path. Thicker coatings therefore need not cool better. Conductor size, thermal conductivity, and wind conditions affect the optimum.

## How a temperature limit enters the ampacity calculation

Note S20 gives Joule heating per unit length as the square of RMS alternating current times the temperature-adjusted AC resistance. Solving the steady heat balance for current gives the square-root expression below. The model balances Joule heating Pⱼ and absorbed solar power Pₛ against convective P𝚌 and radiative Pᵣ cooling. Here all powers are in W/m. To connect the balance with a total resistance in K/W, match the length basis first.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="cable-heat-balance" title="Supplementary Note S20: heat input and heat loss" tex={String.raw`P_j+P_s=P_c+P_r`}>

The subscripts distinguish Joule heating (j), sunlight (s), convection (c), and radiation (r). The model describes steady operating points at a specified conductor temperature, current, and weather condition. A changing temperature also involves heat storage.

More reflection reduces Pₛ, and stronger emission can increase Pᵣ at the same temperature. Convection and radiation depend on temperature, wind, and the surface. Reflectance alone cannot determine the permissible increase in current.

</CandidateEquation>

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="cable-ampacity" title="Supplementary Note S20: ampacity at the selected temperature limit" tex={String.raw`I_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{P_c+P_r-P_s}{R_{\mathrm{AC},20}[1+\alpha(T_s-20)]}}`}>

I_max is RMS alternating current in A. R_AC,20 is AC resistance per unit length at 20°C in Ω/m. The resistance temperature coefficient α has units K⁻¹, and Tₛ is the chosen conductor temperature limit in °C. The 20 refers to the 20°C reference, so α(Tₛ−20) is dimensionless.

The numerator P𝚌+Pᵣ−Pₛ is the cooling margin after subtracting solar input. The denominator is positive resistance per unit length at the stated temperature. Dividing W/m by Ω/m gives A² and the square root gives A. A negative numerator yields no real-valued ampacity under those conditions. Solar input alone may exceed the chosen limit. Simply substituting zero would not establish safe operation.

For illustration, a cooling margin of 100 W/m and resistance of 0.0001 Ω/m give 1,000 A. Increasing the margin to 121 W/m at the same resistance gives 1,100 A. These hypothetical numbers illustrate the square-root relationship.

The study connects measured reflection, emission, and conduction properties to an established heat-balance model. Adopting an operating rating would also require evidence on long-term adhesion, insulation, corona, mechanical conditions, and applicable operating rules.

## Interpreting strength and lifetime

If a coating improves the thermal margin by 21%, does current also increase by 21%? In the worked example, fixed resistance and a fixed temperature limit give a 10% current increase. To reproduce a percentage from the paper, also match the city, weather, and comparison cable. A calculation alone does not authorize a new line rating.

The reported tensile strengths are about 2.5 MPa for the coating and 21 MPa for the comparison XLPE. With a thin nylon reinforcement the coating reached about 45 MPa. Specify the reinforcement when comparing strength. The 240-hour accelerated UV test and 1,000 sandpaper abrasion cycles likewise do not directly measure years of service life.

The roughly $10/m² in Note S22 is a material cost estimated from ingredients and formulation. It excludes installation and maintenance and gives no payback period. Building cooling products serve different applications and use different costing boundaries.

## What was heated outdoors?

Predicting sag also requires mechanical assumptions. Note S19 uses a catenary model dominated by the conductor’s own weight, with limited wind loading and icing. Cooling changes thermal expansion and tension. Permanent elongation from creep accumulates over time. Predicting sag in an actual span needs support spacing, conductor weight and cross section, tension, and material-specific creep data in addition to temperature. The authors explicitly used modeling in place of a full-scale sag-reduction test. [Supplementary Note S19, PDF pp. 32–33, including the rendered equations on p. 32.]

The Methods section specifies a total heating load of 30 W per mock cable from a nickel–chromium resistance wire inside it. This reproduces one effect of Joule heating and tests thermal response to outdoor weather. The setup did not validate an increased rating while transmitting power on an operating grid.

Accelerated UV exposure also cannot guarantee years of outdoor durability. Material properties, test temperatures, modeled ampacity, and in-service reliability are successive questions requiring different evidence.
