> A robot swarm can learn about its global connectivity from how quickly information diffuses among neighbors, even if no robot maps the arena. This study estimated the second eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian from that rate and used it to distinguish a disk from an annulus. “Shape recognition” here means classifying a laboratory arena among predefined candidates. Reconstructing arbitrary spaces or discovering new shapes is a different task.

This article follows the public authors’ manuscript, arXiv:2403.17147v2, updated October 22, 2026. The paper appeared in *Science Robotics* the day before. The final publisher text was unavailable for this review, so figure numbers and descriptions refer to the manuscript. Wording and layout have not been cross-checked against the final version.

## Asking about shape without making a map

Robots often observe space with cameras or range sensors, estimate their positions to build a map, or detect walls. These methods require sensing, computation and coordinate alignment. Small swarm robots may know neither their position nor their heading. **Can robots communicating only with nearby neighbors collectively distinguish the shape of the space they occupy?**

The researchers compared a disk with an annulus, a ring-shaped arena with a hole in the middle. A single robot might see little difference between them, but the hole and the boundary affect which robots can communicate across the swarm. The experiment measures how quickly information diffuses through that graph without explicitly tracing the boundary.

## A graph and its connectivity score

Treat each robot as a **node** and each communicating pair as an **edge**. The graph Laplacian collects both the neighbor counts and the links into one matrix. Here the basic form is <Math tex={"\\mathbf{L}=\\mathbf{D}-\\mathbf{A}"} />. The adjacency matrix <Math tex={"\\mathbf{A}"} /> contains 1 for neighbors and 0 otherwise, while <Math tex={"\\mathbf{D}"} /> places each robot’s number of neighbors on the diagonal.

For a connected graph the Laplacian eigenvalues are ordered <Math tex={"0=\\lambda_1\\leq\\lambda_2\\leq\\lambda_3\\leq\\cdots"} />. The second, <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} />, is the **algebraic connectivity**. If <Math tex={"\\lambda_2=0"} />, the graph splits into disconnected components. A positive value indicates connectivity, but a small positive value signals difficulty exchanging information across part of the graph. A bottleneck such as a narrow passage in an annulus can lower this value.

A single number need not uniquely identify a shape. The researchers use a narrower claim. Under matching conditions, selected shapes such as a disk and an annulus can be classified if their estimates of <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} /> are sufficiently distinct. The paper also explains why <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} /> alone cannot reconstruct an arbitrary shape.

## How the robots measure decay

Each robot starts with an internal numerical state. The mathematical description initializes positive and negative values with a mean of zero. The algorithm describes a preliminary diffusion stage to remove the initial mean component. Both eliminate the first mode, which would otherwise leave every robot converging to the same value. Physical placement at the start of a particular experiment is a separate matter.

Each robot then exchanges state information with its neighbors and updates according to their differences. Larger differences produce larger corrections. As updates repeat, information spreads through the swarm. Faster-decaying components fade first, and after enough time the second eigenmode becomes relatively prominent. The signs of this mode show a pattern dividing the swarm into two regions.

Finally, each robot fits a line to the decay of its own absolute state on a logarithmic scale. Its slope is linked to the graph’s <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} />. The robots exchange and average their estimates with neighbors until they agree. The classifier compares that estimate with the reference values for predefined shapes and displays the classification as an LED color. The distributed computation **estimates the spectral value**. A rule defined in advance maps the estimate to labels such as disk or annulus.

## Reading the equations and their assumptions

Let sᵢⁿ be robot i’s state, sⱼⁿ the state of neighbor j, and Aᵢⱼ their communication link. The first equation updates each robot from the sum of neighbor differences. The next writes this same rule using the graph Laplacian L = D − A. The states, adjacency indicators and unweighted Laplacian are dimensionless. Here c = 1 s⁻¹ and the update interval τ is in seconds.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-1-local-update" lang="en" title="How does a robot use differences with its neighbors?" tex={String.raw`s_i^{n+1}=s_i^n+c\tau\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}(s_j^n-s_i^n)`}>

N is the number of robots. The symmetric adjacency matrix has Aᵢⱼ = 1 for connected neighbors. The parentheses give the neighbor’s state minus the robot’s own state. Adding a fraction of these differences makes neighboring states more alike. The factor cτ sets the fraction applied in one step. Too large a step can destabilize explicit Euler integration.

</CandidateEquation>

### One update with two robots

Suppose two robots share one edge and currently have dimensionless states 1 and −1. In this **hypothetical example**, choose c = 1 s⁻¹ and an update interval τ = 0.1 s, giving cτ = 0.1. Both robots update simultaneously using values from the preceding step.

- First robot: 1 + 0.1 × (−1 − 1) = 0.8
- Second robot: −1 + 0.1 × (1 − (−1)) = −0.8

The mean stays at zero, while the difference falls from 2 to 1.6. This illustrates what it means to incorporate a fraction of the difference with a neighbor. The edge is bidirectional and has unit weight. Because cτ is dimensionless, the update term has the same units as the state. These values and update interval are illustrative. One update cannot establish general convergence or classify an arena.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-2-matrix-update" lang="en" title="How can one matrix represent every neighbor update?" tex={String.raw`\mathbf{s}^{n+1}=\mathbf{s}^{n}-c\tau\mathbf{L}\mathbf{s}^{n}`}>

The vector s collects every robot’s state. L = D − A is the unnormalized graph Laplacian, obtained by subtracting adjacency matrix A from degree matrix D. This compact expression gives the same sum of differences and the same sign as the first equation. The mode analysis uses the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of L.

</CandidateEquation>

For small cτ, repeated updates can be approximated by the matrix exponential of continuous-time diffusion. The exponential describes how each mode of the initial state decays over time.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-3-matrix-exponential" lang="en" title="How can a continuous decay approximate the repeated updates?" tex={String.raw`\mathbf{s}^{n}=e^{-nc\tau\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{s}^{0}`}>

The vector s⁰ is the initial state. This expression approximates the existing update rule for small cτ. It introduces no additional robot action or measurement. As ncτ increases, components associated with larger eigenvalues of L decay faster.

</CandidateEquation>

A symmetric Laplacian lets us decompose the initial state into mutually orthogonal eigenmodes. The magnitude of each mode decays at a rate set by its eigenvalue. The decay record thus contains information about graph connectivity.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-4-spectral-decomposition" lang="en" title="How do the decay rates separate by eigenmode?" tex={String.raw`\mathbf{s}^{n}=\sum_{q=1}^{N}e^{-\lambda_qnc\tau}(\mathbf{s}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{v}_q)\mathbf{v}_q`}>

The index q labels the mode, λq is a dimensionless Laplacian eigenvalue and vq is a normalized eigenvector. The projection s⁰·vq gives the initial amplitude in that mode. Each component decays exponentially at its own rate and the components sum to the state. The symmetric, undirected graph assumption is important for this orthogonal decomposition.

</CandidateEquation>

After removing the initial mean and waiting long enough, faster higher modes shrink and the second mode becomes relatively visible. The expression below is a late-time approximation. If the initial state is nearly orthogonal to the second mode, the signal may be too weak to observe reliably.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-5-dominant-mode" lang="en" title="Which mode remains after the transient?" tex={String.raw`\mathbf{s}^{n}\approx e^{-\lambda_2nc\tau}(\mathbf{s}^{0}\cdot\mathbf{v}_2)\mathbf{v}_2`}>

The second-smallest eigenvalue λ₂ is the graph’s algebraic connectivity. Once the first (mean) mode is removed and the faster modes have decayed sufficiently, the state retains the shape of v₂ while its amplitude decays exponentially. This conditional approximation leads to the log-slope estimate.

</CandidateEquation>

Each robot now fits the logarithm of its own state magnitude over time. Equation (6) scales the time axis as ṫ = ncτ, giving a fitted slope of −λ₂.

<CandidateEquation id="eq-6-log-slope" lang="en" title="How does the log-decay slope reveal connectivity?" tex={String.raw`\log(|s_i^{n}|)=-\lambda_2nc\tau+C=-\lambda_2\tilde{t}+C,\quad\tilde{t}=nc\tau`}>

C is the intercept set by each robot’s initial amplitude and sᵢⁿ its dimensionless state. Scaled time ṫ is also dimensionless, so the slope −λ₂ is dimensionless. The implementation uses linear regression over part of the log-state record, averages estimates from three diffusion sessions, then reaches consensus with neighbors. Fitting too early or too close to zero, where numerical errors become important, can upset the estimate.

</CandidateEquation>

A zero λ₂ indicates a disconnected graph and a positive value indicates a connected one. One value cannot uniquely specify the arena’s shape. The experiment compares it with previously established disk and annulus references.
## Reading the two robot platforms and simulations separately

The v2 manuscript covers both **Kilobots and Pogobots**. Kilobots have low communication bandwidth. Pogobots offer faster infrared communication and greater computing power. According to the paper, a Kilobot sends at most two nine-byte messages per second, with an effective throughput of about five bytes per second once collisions are included. A Pogobot can send up to 120 messages per second, with 20–100 bytes per message depending on conditions, and a reported communication range up to 135 mm center to center. These are distinct platforms with different capabilities.

Kilombo simulated the Kilobots and Pogosim the Pogobots. The authors report running each platform’s same C code on its simulator and physical robots. The simulators separately model collision physics, communication losses and sensor and motor biases. The main text gives processing times of about five minutes for Pogobots and 79 minutes for Kilobots including pre-diffusion. Supplementary Section 3.6 gives roughly three minutes and one hour, respectively. Both accounts put Pogobots ahead, but the precise times differ.

The main physical comparison used 25 robots of each platform in disk and annular arenas, with 15 experiments per shape. The main text says the robots were placed to give good initial coverage. A separate supplementary Kilobot simulation compared random and uniform placements and notes that an experimenter can distribute physical Kilobots evenly before the start. This differs from the general algorithm’s run-and-tumble dispersion phase. Robots stop during each diffusion session. Short motions between repetitions change the neighbor graph. The Figure 6 caption also states that its time-lapse omits pre-diffusion.

Kilobots correctly classified 22 of 30 physical runs across the two shapes, or 73%. Pogobots correctly classified all 30, or 100%. Simulations under the corresponding settings report 95% for Kilobots and 100% for Pogobots. Those four numbers measure classification accuracy. Separately, manuscript Figure 5 plots distributions of normalized <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} /> estimates. Each condition uses 64 simulations or 15 experiments per shape. Each point is one run averaging the individual estimates of 25 robots. The reported averages, ranges and statistical comparisons are a different analysis from the confusion matrices in Figure 6.

Arena area also differed. The caption gives about 70,000 mm² for Kilobots and 150,000 mm² for Pogobots, with roughly similar robot densities. Communication speed, sensing range, arena area, implementation and initial placement can all affect performance, so the difference cannot be attributed to robot type alone.

## Robot count and communication range matter

The simulations varied robot count <Math tex={"N"} />, communication range <Math tex={"\\sigma"} /> and arena area <Math tex={"S"} />. Approximating the area within one robot’s radius <Math tex={"\\sigma"} /> as <Math tex={"\\pi\\sigma^2"} /> gives the following overall coverage ratio.

<Math display tex={"\\alpha(N,\\sigma)=\\frac{N\\pi\\sigma^2}{S}"} />

In simulations of seven shapes, 50–500 robots and communication ranges of 30–320 mm, good settings tended to cluster near <Math tex={"\\alpha\\approx17"} />. Supplementary Tables S5 and S6 list 50–550 robots for sim-large-7, so the upper bound differs from the main text. This is an empirical relationship for the tested settings. If <Math tex={"\\alpha"} /> is too small, the graph breaks into components. If it is too large, neighborhoods become so broad that shape differences blur, or the explicit Euler update can become unstable. The main text discusses borderline cases around <Math tex={"\\alpha=7.5"} /> and <Math tex={"\\alpha=32"} />. It also reports configurations with more than 25 neighbors on average that failed to converge.

Figure 4 displays accuracy only for simulations in which diffusion converged. Its caption says cells were left blank when instability exceeded 50%. Read these omissions alongside the accuracy scores. The well-connected r1 setting achieved 100% accuracy across seven simulated shapes and the intermediate r3 setting 86%. Similar <Math tex={"\\lambda_2"} /> values can require multiple repetitions. The paper reports about 20 repetitions for its disk-versus-square example. These simulation results answer a different question from the two-shape physical test.

The Laplacian eigenvalues of a continuous domain depend on length scale. The paper explains that they scale inversely with the square of length. The unweighted graph Laplacian in the experiments is a discrete object counting links among robots. The two representations can be normalized for comparison, but what they measure and their units differ.

## Comparison with simpler alternatives

The supplement compares three simple baselines in simulations with 25 Pogobots, three shapes and 64 runs per shape. The mean-degree baseline computes each robot’s neighbor count, reaches consensus on the average and uses it for classification. The degree-statistics method collects the mean, variance, 25th and 75th percentiles of neighbor counts and compares their distances to reference centroids. The convergence-time method estimates locally when diffusion has flattened and averages the times across neighbors. All three use inter-robot communication without an external sensor such as a wall detector.

These comparisons are confined to simulation. The authors examine both fully random placement and more even placement produced using Lloyd’s method. In the three-shape task with three iterations, mean degree and degree statistics carried some shape information but performed below spectral swarm robotics (SSR). Simple convergence time performed worse. The comparison applies to those initial placements and Pogobot simulations. Other shapes and hardware need their own tests.

## Failure modes and stability

Parts of the mathematical analysis rely on a connected graph with a symmetric Laplacian. Real infrared communication can be asymmetric because of collisions, different reception rates and environmental effects. One robot may reach its neighbor without hearing the reply. The authors use a handshake to identify neighbors that exchange messages in both directions. The supplement notes that this cannot eliminate every short-lived asymmetry.

Robots also need to execute diffusion steps at similar times. The Kilobot setup synchronized internal counters and fully charged the batteries before each experiment to reduce clock differences. Numerical errors can propagate when states approach zero, and asymmetry or delays can disrupt diffusion even after apparent convergence. Each robot evaluates its linear-regression error over time and chooses the estimate from the interval with the lowest mean squared error. A single image showing a partition cannot establish stable convergence in every run.

Time drift was tested in **Pogobot simulations**. Small drift left reported accuracy intact. At the 100 ms scale, resynchronization skipped several diffusion steps and the algorithm stopped working. The authors did not report this as an observed failure in physical Pogobots. Communication noise and maximum message age were also tested separately as simulation parameters.

Physical robots stop during diffusion, and some simulated and physical setups begin with a relatively even distribution. The study leaves open performance with continuously moving swarms, asynchronous robots or obstacles in an unknown environment. Reliance on a single eigenvalue also makes spaces with similar spectral values difficult to distinguish.

## What was demonstrated?

In this laboratory proof of concept, robots estimated global swarm connectivity through neighbor-to-neighbor state updates without sharing maps or sending every position to a central computer. The paper reports large simulations in seven spaces and disk-versus-annulus experiments on two robot platforms. This demonstrates a form of distributed sensing. Arbitrary arena recognition and field deployment remain open.

Important next questions concern stability in larger physical swarms, estimates under sustained motion and communication loss, and the limits of discrimination between similar shapes. The authors discuss using additional eigenvalues or estimating spectra on dynamic graphs. Neither extension was demonstrated in this study.

Starting from (0.8, −0.8) in the two-robot example, what does one more update produce? Would that calculation alone tell you the arena’s shape?
