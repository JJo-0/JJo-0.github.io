## What more demonstrations give a robot

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={2} caption="Is scaling data enough?" />

More demonstrations teach a robot which actions are common in particular scenes. These expectations form <strong>prior knowledge</strong>. But locating a target on an unfamiliar table or recombining steps to grasp a handle requires connecting known objects and movements to the current scene. The lecture's <strong>embodied reasoning</strong> grounds plans in actual views, end-effector positions, and object interactions. [Original lecture, 0:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxhrjQuGEuE&t=20s) · [Slide P002](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-2.jpg)

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={3} caption="The missing ability: recombining knowledge for new problems" />

Demonstration learning compresses frequently chosen human actions into model weights. Yet even a familiar instruction such as “put it in the right bowl” needs reconsideration if that bowl is inverted or the target occluded. Compositional reasoning reconnects known objects, spatial relationships, and action orders. It does not promise to solve every unseen situation; it asks whether existing knowledge can help without demonstrating each situation in full.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={4} caption="Can reasoning join a multimodal sequence?" />

Chapters 7–9 connected images, language, and actions within model sequences, creating a point of contact with LLM reasoning improvements. Plausible verbal plans alone are insufficient. The plan's “watermelon” must refer to a particular camera-visible object, and its approach must correspond to a physical hand path.

## What does ECoT write before acting?

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={5} caption="Acting through plans, subtasks, and screen locations" />

Embodied Chain-of-Thought (ECoT) makes a vision–language–action model output intermediate plans and observations before predicting motor commands. For the watermelon example, it produces a subtask such as moving toward the slice, the target's bounding box, current gripper position, and movement direction. The plan narrows the goal, and the box locates it on screen. ### Intermediate representations people can correct

A correct subplan to approach watermelon can still place its box on the adjacent toy. Correcting only the target box tests whether subsequent actions change. Conversely, a correct box with a forward-push plan when the hand should withdraw calls for correcting movement. ECoT's interaction interface helps isolate where an error begins.

The same distinction applies to plausible reasoning on new-robot videos. Language knowledge about objects and spatial relations may transfer across cameras and arms, but reachable ranges and allowed gripper poses differ. Evaluate intermediate explanation accuracy and final manipulation success separately. [ECoT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08693)

### Where did reasoning help?

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={15} caption="Testing three hypotheses for the benefit" />

ECoT-Lite separates three possibilities: learning useful <strong>representations</strong> such as object positions, providing an intermediate <strong>learning scaffold</strong> between pixels and actions, or adding <strong>computational workspace</strong> irrespective of token meaning. In LIBERO-90 simulation, reasoning pretraining or occasional reasoning omission approached full ECoT's performance. Meaningless-token variants showed no benefit. Evidence supports both learning useful representations and reading that information during action prediction. [Original lecture, 15:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxhrjQuGEuE&t=949s) · [Slide P015](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-15.jpg) · [Slide P019](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-19.jpg) · [ECoT-Lite paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08243)

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={16} caption="Hypothesis 1: better representations during training" />

Reasoning annotations may teach action-relevant visual features such as an important handle. If this explains most gains, weights trained to generate reasoning should retain benefits when execution predicts only actions. This separates time spent printing reasoning from representations learned while training on it.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={17} caption="Hypothesis 2: scaffolding difficult action prediction" />

A VLM's pretrained task of describing images differs substantially from outputting joint targets. Intermediate movement descriptions can break the pixel-to-action mapping into smaller connections. Scaffolding experiments supply reasoning as input without a loss for predicting it, separating extra supervision from the benefit of an input scaffold.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={18} caption="Hypothesis 3: can meaningless tokens add workspace?" />

An autoregressive model reprocesses preceding context when generating another token. Even meaningless intermediate symbols could therefore provide sequential workspace. This tests useful reasoning content separately from increased computation. A phenomenon possible in language models does not automatically yield the same robot-policy benefits.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={19} caption="What remained in the LIBERO-90 ablations?" />

Reasoning pretraining and dropout approached full ECoT even when full reasoning was omitted during execution. Supplying reasoning as an input scaffold also helped. Meaningless thinking tokens did not improve baseline VLA in this setting, making extra token computation alone an insufficient explanation. No single experiment establishes one universal mechanism for all models.

### Computational order in reasoning pretraining and dropout

Reasoning pretraining first trains a VLM to explain object positions and plans. A second stage starts from those weights and predicts actual actions. Features learned in the first stage initialize action learning. This can connect human videos with reasoning annotations to separate robot data with action values.

Dropout jointly trains reasoning and actions while omitting reasoning for some examples. The model practices both reading reasoning context and acting directly from observations. Slides P020–P033 animate which inputs and losses remain in these methods. Holding the image and goal fixed while toggling reasoning tokens lets us compare action outputs and runtime. [Slides P020–P033](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/tree/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10) · [ECoT-Lite paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08243)

Rather than reducing the three hypotheses to one claim, compare information in training losses with information read during action prediction. Reasoning supervision helped representations, and pathways using them also mattered. Meaningless-token results make pure computation increase an insufficient explanation on this benchmark.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={33} caption="P020–P033: final states of the two training paths" />

This sequence gradually reveals the same architecture. Original frames P020–P032 can be opened in order from the copy index. Reasoning pretraining on the left learns reasoning before action training; dropout on the right mixes examples with and without reasoning within one process. When reasoning is omitted, ground-truth reasoning must not secretly remain in action inputs, or training would no longer match that deployment path.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={34} caption="Success and inference speed on physical WidowX" />

In real-robot comparisons, full ECoT has generalization-success advantages but slower policy calls, while lightweight variants trade some success for speed. Read both axes independently. More actions per second do not ensure shorter completion time, since failures and retries also take time.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={35} caption="Are reasoning and action data paired?" />

If human videos provide abundant reasoning annotations without matching robot actions, two-stage pretraining is natural. If the same robot trajectories contain both reasoning and actions, dropout can train direct-action and reasoning paths together. Deployment can choose between editable explanations and fast responses.

## Spending more computation at execution

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={36} caption="Beyond fixed reasoning steps: more computation for harder problems" />

Earlier ECoT outputs predefined kinds of intermediate information in order. Test-time scaling asks whether difficult problems can receive more candidates or reconsideration. It changes the current problem's computation budget while holding model size and training data fixed.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={37} caption="Connecting classical planning, learned planning, and language reasoning" />

<strong>Test-time scaling</strong> generates more candidates, revises earlier answers, or compares candidates longer without updating model weights. In Go, a policy network proposes promising moves and Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) examines continuations. The lecture's approximately 3,000 and 5,000 Elo values come from an AlphaGo Zero comparison. This is a game with explicit rules and outcomes, and the numbers belong to its evaluation system. [Original lecture, 26:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxhrjQuGEuE&t=1595s) · [Slide P037](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-37.jpg) · [Slide P038](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-38.jpg) · [AlphaGo Zero paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24270)

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={38} caption="Comparing search gains with larger Go models" />

The slide's rule of thumb associates about 120 Elo with doubling model size or search budget. Repeating it suggests roughly seventeen doublings—around a hundred-thousandfold model expansion—for a 2,000-Elo difference, conveying a dramatic cost comparison. The slide's 3,000→5,200 and approximately 100,000× do not match this approximation exactly. Do not treat it as a validated scaling law from actually expanding the model by that amount. The point is that search delivered large gains in a well-defined game.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={39} caption="Does the same trade-off hold for open-ended language problems?" />

Go defines legal moves and winning. “Tidy the kitchen” combines goal interpretation, object positions, and uncertain action consequences. Both useful candidate generation and correct candidate evaluation are needed.

Language models require a <strong>verifier</strong> to judge candidates. For instance, generate three drawer-opening plans and favor the one matching the observed handle position. The probability that at least one candidate is correct differs from the probability that the verifier actually selects it. The following educational calculation assumes independent candidates with identical success probabilities.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={40} caption="The benefit of compute budgets depends on difficulty" />

Snell and colleagues show that under equal FLOPs, smaller models allocated inference computation can outperform models about fourteen times larger on easy or intermediate problems. Hard problems can still favor larger base models. Repeated correlated wrong answers violate the independence assumption above. Robot candidate selection can also fail when a verifier mispredicts contact outcomes. <LectureSlide lecture={10} page={41} caption="Intermediate tokens provide space for sequential computation" />

An autoregressive Transformer can output intermediate tokens and reuse them as inputs. Li and colleagues theoretically show greater sequential computation under specified assumptions. Wei and colleagues' early CoT prompting elicits this through a few worked examples in the prompt. Model-size graphs depend on their experimental conditions and do not establish a universal size threshold for robot models. [Original lecture, 33:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxhrjQuGEuE&t=2001s) · [Slide P041](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-41.jpg) · [Slide P044](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-44.jpg) · [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12875) · [Wei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903)

P041's theoretical claim is that a fixed-size Transformer can solve a problem requiring T sequential steps by generating O(T) intermediate tokens under specified construction assumptions. Distinguish fixed layer depth within one forward pass from computation continued by feeding tokens back. This is an existence result about a computational construction, not a guarantee that any pretrained model discovers the algorithm merely by producing longer reasoning.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={42} caption="Eliciting CoT through worked examples" />

Instead of showing only “answer: 624,” original CoT prompting first gives an example explaining how to calculate weekly use and multiply by 52. The model continues similar intermediate steps for a new problem. Human examples condition the context; model weights are not updated during this execution.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={43} caption="Will smaller models show the same effect?" />

Early CoT graphs show little benefit or regressions from worked examples in smaller base models, with greater gains in larger models. The lecture's roughly 100-billion-parameter observation belongs to those models, tasks, and prompts. Later reasoning training and distillation enable smaller models to reason too, so it is not a universal minimum size.

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={44} caption="Limitations of relying on human-selected examples" />

Changing example wording or order can change results, and new domains require new worked examples. This motivates another question: rather than designing reasoning prompts each time, can outcome evaluation teach the model useful reasoning strategies?

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={45} caption="From elicited solutions to learned reasoning strategies" />

### One longer attempt or several candidates?

Additional inference time can be spent differently. Revising an answer after feedback is sequential computation; generating independent answers for a verifier to select is parallel candidate search. The former uses information from previous attempts, while the latter explores several directions. Both depend on verifier scores matching the actual task.

For robots, the scene keeps changing during deliberation. If a person removes a cup or an object rolls away, a long plan based on the initial image needs updating. Recording observation time, decision completion, and execution start alongside candidate counts and accuracy helps identify where additional computation benefits the system.

## Learning reasoning through rewards for correct answers

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={46} caption="The letter problem: different paths to the same answer" />

The slide asks how many pages are written in a year when sending three-page letters to two friends twice a week. Assuming 52 weeks, 3×2×2×52=624 pages. One output lists the calculation cleanly; another rechecks whether twice means per friend and verifies the interpretation and week count. Verbosity itself is not accuracy. This introduces reconsideration behavior observed in outcome-reward-trained models.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) samples several answers to one question and compares them <strong>within the group</strong>. It updates policies similarly to [PPO in Chapter 5](/en/posts/rl-robot-learning-ch05-policy-gradients/), but uses the group's mean score rather than a separate value estimator as its reference. The simplified example below assigns one outcome score to each response, omitting other objective terms such as PPO-style probability-ratio clipping. [Original lecture, 40:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CxhrjQuGEuE&t=2418s) · [Slide P047](https://github.com/SRA-VJTI/robotics-study-group/blob/077f2bfe53b01005e0b2e29ae43b6f0c0ba3c0a8/assets/lectures/week-10/slide-47.jpg) · [DeepSeek-R1 paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09422-z)

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={47} caption="GRPO: comparing answers sampled for the same question" />

PPO used a learned critic's expected state return as a baseline. Here, several answers to one question receive relative scores based on group reward mean and standard deviation. Four educational rewards 0,0,1,1 have mean 0.5 and population standard deviation 0.5, producing normalized scores approximately −1,−1,1,1. Updates favor better answers within the group. An answer checker must still supply reward signals.

<Math tex={String.raw`A_i=\frac{r_i-\bar r}{\sigma_r+\varepsilon}`} display />

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={48} caption="The base model must have some chance of finding the answer" />

If all four answers are wrong, outcome rewards provide no relatively good answer within that group. Mees uses this to explain why a base model should occasionally solve difficult problems. Intermediate examples in R1-Zero show phrases in which the model rechecks its solution. These are observed behaviors, distinct from measured correctness across all solutions. <LectureSlide lecture={10} page={49} caption="Reconsideration behavior emerging during training" />

The graph shows reconsideration phrases changing in frequency with training steps. Reward optimization can reinforce these patterns without directly labeling a particular phrase. More occurrences of “wait” do not prove humanlike internal thought or causally correct reasoning. Measure solution length, correctness, and new-problem performance separately.

### What GRPO removes and what remains

Removing a value-estimation network does not remove the need to evaluate answers. Mathematics can check final-answer agreement; code can use executed tests. Verifiable rewards can reduce the expense of human grading for each solution. GRPO itself uses the provided reward signal, so the reward rule directly shapes learning.

If all four answers to a question are wrong, the group gives no preference signal. If later samples include correct answers, they receive better relative scores. The lecture's base-capability discussion concerns this discoverable starting point. Longer outputs and more frequent correct answers are separately measurable.

## A correct answer can still misread the scene

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={50} caption="Can verifiable rewards extend to vision?" />

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={51} caption="Visual-RFT: box IoU instead of a mathematics checker" />

Visual-RFT scores detection boxes with intersection over union (IoU): overlapping predicted/target area divided by their union. A correct box can accompany an explanation with the wrong color or location. Box scores alone struggle to reject such explanatory errors. ### The cost of verifying intermediate steps

A video claim that “the person grasped the left box” must identify both its time interval and object. Argos combines localization or segmentation models, rule-based checks, and language evaluation according to the sample. This checks whether optimizing one tool's score also passes other evaluations. Automatic verification can err too, so assess final task success alongside it. [Argos team's explanation](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/multimodal-reinforcement-learning-with-agentic-verifier-for-ai-agents/)

ALFRED-style simulated planning tests which subtask to select before moving rooms or grasping objects. Continuous arm control may need new commands at tens of hertz, making repeated long deliberation part of the performance cost. This motivates discussing ECoT-Lite alongside planning methods.

## Giving planning and control different timescales

<LectureSlide lecture={10} page={55} caption="Four conclusions and the remaining integration problem" />

First, additional computation can be allocated at execution as well as training. Second, continuous control can learn representations through reasoning while omitting long explanations at deployment. Third, verifiable rewards can reinforce useful reasoning strategies. Fourth, multimodal models need checks of intermediate evidence alongside final answers. Integrating these into one policy that adapts and improves in the real world remains a research problem.

A <strong>high-level planner</strong> can choose a subtask such as finding a handle, opening the door, and inspecting inside, while a <strong>low-level policy</strong> rapidly adjusts hand motion as sensors update.
