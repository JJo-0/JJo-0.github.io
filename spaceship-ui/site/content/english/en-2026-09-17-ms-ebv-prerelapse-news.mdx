## 1. The conclusion in one sentence

<strong>EBV-associated immune-cell states changed before clinical relapse in multiple sclerosis, but the observations alone cannot establish that the virus caused relapse or that a test can predict it in individuals.</strong> Studying banked blood let the researchers examine the sequence leading up to relapse as well as blood collected afterward.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

“Priming” in the title refers to an immune state more ready to mount a subsequent response. The authors compared B-cell and monocyte states, selected viral transcripts and the proportion of cells displaying a viral protein in pre-relapse blood. Their observations connect to a hypothesis. The study did not test whether intervening on the virus changes relapse outcomes.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Three questions organize the evidence. What was measured? Did the change precede clinical relapse? Does it cause relapse or predict it reliably? Findings on the first two cannot answer the third on their own.

## 2. Why this question is difficult

### MS and EBV refer to different things

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated condition involving the central nervous system, including the brain and spinal cord. It can damage myelin, which helps conduct nerve signals, and other neural tissue. A relapse is a clinically defined event, distinct from a particularly tiring day. Researchers aligned the blood samples with the clinically determined time of relapse.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

EBV is Epstein–Barr virus. It can remain latent in host cells over long periods. Reactivation refers to changes in viral gene expression and life-cycle activity associated with leaving latency. Evidence of past viral presence is distinct from evidence that virus-associated activity increased at a particular time.

B cells are an immune lineage that can develop into antibody-producing cells. They also present antigens to other immune cells and regulate responses. Monocytes belong to another lineage. The researchers examined both because immune changes in blood can involve several cell populations.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

PBMC stands for peripheral blood mononuclear cell. PBMCs are a subset of blood cells. Detecting a change in this sample does not directly reveal every change occurring in the brain.

### Blood collected before relapse is hard to obtain

Exactly when a person will relapse is usually unknown. Blood is easier to collect at a visit after relapse than one or two months beforehand. The latter requires systematic long-term banking. The researchers used the CLIMB cohort's long-running biobank to examine that window.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

A cohort is a group followed under defined criteria. A biobank stores samples and associated information. This cohort collected material prospectively over a long period. For this analysis, investigators selected samples relative to already-known relapse dates. Prospective prediction would apply a test before knowing the outcome and follow what happened next.

Timing matters because a signal rising only after relapse could be a consequence of disease. Clinical symptom onset need not coincide with the start of underlying biological change. A blood signal preceding symptoms might still follow earlier, invisible central nervous system activity.

## 3. Limits of earlier approaches

### A group average can hide which cells changed

Blood contains a mixture of cells. More RNA from a gene could mean that more cells of the type expressing it are present, or that cells of the same type express more RNA per cell. A whole-blood average has difficulty distinguishing these possibilities.

RNA is one product of using a gene's information. Measuring it shows patterns of gene activity without editing the DNA. Cells with the same DNA can produce different RNA molecules in different amounts under different conditions.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) resolves patterns of expression by cell. Bulk RNA sequencing reads combined signals from a population. Single-cell analysis distinguishes cell identities and rare states but has limited sensitivity for each cell. Bulk sequencing of isolated B cells can give a deeper view of that population.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### Different assays address different uncertainties

An increase in viral RNA raises questions about corresponding protein changes, the cell population involved and whether other measurements show related signals. The study combined RNA analysis, flow cytometry and targeted gene assays.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Agreement across methods still draws on overlapping participants and samples, shared clinical classifications and analyses motivated by the same hypothesis. Five assays do not constitute five independent demonstrations of causation. They help check different errors while retaining common limitations.

## 4. The approach in three steps

### First, align samples with the clinical relapse date

The study included 135 people: 114 with MS and 21 healthy comparators. They contributed 240 PBMC samples because some individuals contributed at multiple times. Treating samples from one person as if they came from independent patients would exaggerate the amount of evidence.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The researchers compared pre-relapse, relapse and remission samples. Here remission denotes a comparison period without clinical relapse. It does not signify complete disappearance of disease. Some comparisons paired pre-relapse and remission samples from the same person to reduce the influence of differences in baseline immune state.

Not all 240 samples underwent every assay. Single-cell work used 52 stored vials representing 42 samples. Bulk B-cell RNA analysis used 130 samples, flow cytometry 46 and targeted qPCR 60. Some samples overlapped across methods. Adding those counts would not give a new total of patients or independent replications.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### Second, identify the immune-cell states associated with change

The single-cell analysis grouped cells by type and state to identify populations with pronounced changes before relapse. Transcriptional changes in naive B cells and monocytes were prominent. “Naive” identifies cells before an antigen-experienced memory state. It does not describe a lack of function.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The study also examined atypical B-cell states associated with markers such as CD11c. A marker is a molecule used to characterize cell identity or state. CD19 helps identify B cells, CD11c helps classify certain cell states, and gp350 is EBV-associated protein. These names denote different kinds of measurements.

The researchers examined how sets of altered host genes overlapped with EBV-associated programs and how MS genetic-risk loci intersected viral regulatory factors. Gene-set enrichment tests whether genes of a given kind occur together more often than expected. A cluster of related addresses on a map does not identify which address caused an event.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### Third, compare RNA and protein signals across measurements

RT-qPCR quantifies selected RNA targets after reverse transcription. It supplied a separate measurement for rare signals that can be difficult to capture by single-cell RNA analysis. Flow cytometry uses markers to classify individual cells and measure their proportions. RNA abundance and protein staining provide different scales of observation.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The authors report pre-relapse changes in LMP-1 transcript signals and gp350-positive B cells. These are different molecules measured at different levels. One need not change by the same amount as the other. Discordance is also a result to explain rather than a difference to dismiss automatically.

The sequence was to align observations in time, identify changing immune states and check signals with other measurements. No new drug was administered and viral activity was not experimentally blocked.

## 5. Main analyses and numbers

| Question | Available data | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| How large was the study? | 135 people and 240 PBMC samples | Some people contributed multiple samples |
| Was there pre-relapse material? | A window up to three months before clinical relapse | No claim of a three-month forecast for each patient |
| Were cell-level changes studied? | scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq of isolated B cells | Cell counts are not counts of independent patients |
| Were protein markers compared? | 46 flow samples paired across 23 people | Estimates concern small absolute differences in a rare positive population |
| Were there other viral measurements? | 60 samples for targeted qPCR | Samples may overlap with other analyses |

The study-design figure and main text provide these counts. In particular, the Figure 4 legend identifies the flow-cytometry analysis as a paired comparison across 23 people. Reading a point or a sample as an independent patient would double the apparent population.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The gp350-positive population is a rare subset of B cells, at a level below roughly 0.2% during remission in the paper. A large relative rise within such a group does not mean that most B cells became positive. For example, a hypothetical change from 0.10% to 0.15% is a 50% relative increase but only 0.05 percentage points in absolute terms. Those illustrative figures are not study measurements.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

“Up to three months before relapse” likewise describes the time window in which signals were compared. It does not mean one blood sample predicts a relapse date three months ahead. A predictive test would need a prespecified threshold and forecast horizon, with evaluation of both missed relapses and false alarms.

This article compared the public text, study design, main figures, limitations and data-sharing statements. It did not reprocess the raw single-cell data or independently reproduce the assays. Data availability and an independent reanalysis are distinct.

## 6. Limitations and counterevidence

### Earlier observation does not establish an earlier cause

The authors state that their observational design cannot establish causation. Among the possibilities, central nervous system changes beginning before clinical symptoms might stimulate peripheral immunity and increase EBV-associated signals. Another cause might activate B cells first, with EBV amplifying the response. Viral signals might also accompany general B-cell activation as a byproduct.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

All three can broadly fit the observation that blood changes preceded clinical relapse. Narrowing the alternatives would require following the causal chain or evidence that changing a pathway affects an outcome. Describing alternative explanations clarifies what a next study would have to distinguish.

### The viral markers did not all move together

The paper did not find significantly higher EBNA2 transcript levels. The gp350 protein signal also did not align simply with results for the corresponding BLLF1 RNA. Detection sensitivity, cell state and sampling time are candidate explanations. Their plausibility alone does not resolve the discrepancy.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Keeping only markers that increased would imply a simpler and stronger result than the data support. The partial agreement and discordance leave questions about whether a full replication cycle, a limited program or another explanation accounts for the observations.

### Rare positive cells are difficult to interpret

For a rare population such as gp350-positive B cells, background signal and antibody-binding specificity matter. The authors report the absence of an Fc blocker and an EBV-negative cell-line control in the flow analysis. Those limitations affect interpretation of very small positive fractions even when a statistical difference is observed.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Parallel increases in a B-cell state and gp350 positivity also do not establish that all markers coexist in the same cells. The paper notes limits to colocalization and to direct clonal tracking of T- and B-cell receptors. Further evidence would be needed to connect these blood changes to immune responses in the brain.

## 7. What could change in practice?

### First, the question of what to follow

The observations motivate following pre-relapse immune states as well as inflammation after relapse. Later studies can distinguish whether an EBV-associated program initiates relapse, amplifies it or simply accompanies it. The study itself introduced no new treatment.

A clinical biomarker must do more than differ on average between groups. How well can an individual result classify future risk? Does it improve decisions beyond existing clinical information or imaging? Changes in age, treatment, infection and sample storage could also affect whether a fixed threshold transfers.

Consider a hypothetical test in 1,000 people. Suppose 50 will relapse during the specified period. A test detecting 80% of those events and classifying 90% of nonrelapsing people as negative would produce 40 true alarms and 95 false alarms. Among 135 positive results, about 29.6% would actually relapse. These are teaching assumptions, outside the measured performance of this study.

<Math display tex={String.raw`\mathrm{PPV}=\frac{\mathrm{true\ positives}}{\mathrm{true\ positives}+\mathrm{false\ positives}}=\frac{40}{40+95}\approx 0.296`} label="Hypothetical example: the positive predictive value of a relapse alert also depends on event frequency" />

Even with seemingly strong sensitivity and specificity, the meaning of a positive result depends on event frequency in those tested. The gene-expression signal here has not been validated as a personal warning system or a basis for changing medication or treatment.

### Read the data-sharing claims precisely

The paper states that raw and processed RNA data and deidentified metadata are available in GEO under GSE344578. It identifies analysis code in the RRMS-Biomarkers repository under an MIT license. Some additional data still had a pending Zenodo identifier in the paper. Whole-genome and potentially identifiable individual clinical data have access restrictions. Different resources therefore have different availability.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Some authors disclosed research support or consulting relationships with pharmaceutical and diagnostics companies. Disclosure alone does not invalidate a finding, but it matters when interpreting industrial interest in EBV-targeted treatments or biomarkers. Disclosure, reproducibility and clinical utility each require a separate assessment.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 8. What evidence to look for next

First, would the same signal appear in independent cohorts? Replication requires other patients, hospitals, treatment histories and sample-processing conditions. Additional statistical analyses of the same cohort do not supply that independence.

Second, prospective validation should fix the marker combination, alert threshold and forecast horizon before outcomes are known. Then investigators can apply the rule to new patients, compare with actual relapses and report false alarms and missed events. This differs from choosing the best-fitting marker after relapse.

Third, rare gp350-positive populations need stronger measurement evidence on negative controls, antibody specificity, marker co-occurrence within cells and the relationship between viral RNA and protein. Explaining their different trajectories would refine the meaning of reactivation in these observations.

Fourth, clinical outcomes must be connected to the proposed causal pathway. Evidence is needed on whether changes in virus-associated activity alter actual relapses as well as immune-cell states. Distinguishing MS-specific patterns from other autoimmune disease or general infection and immune activation will matter to clinical interpretation.

The companion [early gene-therapy trial in adolescent hemophilia B](/en/posts/2026-09-17-adolescent-hemophilia-b-news/) administered a treatment to people but had a small sample and no control group. Alongside this observational study it illustrates the range of evidence covered by “observed in humans.” The [Vera Rubin benchmark article](/en/posts/2026-09-17-vera-rubin-mlperf-news/) makes the related point that a large number must be read with its measurement conditions.
