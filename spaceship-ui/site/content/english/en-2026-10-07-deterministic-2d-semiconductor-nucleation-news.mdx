## The result in one sentence

Growing a semiconductor in a chosen region is one thing. Making it start at a chosen point inside that region is another. The researchers used strong etching at the edges to leave a single crystal nucleus near the center. They call this etching-flux-guided single-center nucleation (EF-SCN). MoS₂ is a thin semiconductor made of molybdenum and sulfur. Nucleation is the formation of the small seed from which a crystal grows.

## How this differs from earlier approaches

Area-selective growth defines where a material should grow. It does not necessarily fix the coordinates at which a crystal starts. If several nuclei form and the growing crystals meet, grain boundaries can form where crystal domains touch. Repeating growth regions of identical size can therefore give different results.

A [2015 seeded-growth approach](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms7128) used lithography, which patterns material with light, to place molybdenum precursor seeds at selected positions on a substrate. Multiple nuclei could still form in a single seed and merge into different crystal domains. Its supplementary information showed that the number of domains around a seed’s perimeter tended to rise with seed size. A [2023 geometrical-confinement approach](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05524-0) defined the growth region with a narrow trench. It exploited the interval in which the crystal from the first nucleus could fill that region before another nucleus formed.

The new study engineers how etching varies with position inside a growth region. Three questions distinguish these approaches: where is the region placed on the substrate, how many nuclei form in it, and where within it does nucleation begin? The studies did not rank performance at matched temperatures on the same equipment, and the earlier papers did not use the equations introduced below. The comparison with the 2023 work covers its abstract, public figure descriptions, and supplement. Details from its full Methods were unavailable.

Oxygen released from hafnium oxide (HfO₂) or zirconium oxide (ZrO₂) barriers etches material and suppresses nucleation near the edges. Etching removes material that has already formed. Its influence weakens toward the center. Matching growth conditions to the region’s size can therefore leave only a central area in which a nucleus can survive.

## Separate the nucleation position, crystal orientation, and device performance

A nucleus is the small starting point for a growing crystal. Area selection determines which region receives material. Single-center nucleation determines where the crystal starts within it. Growth from one nucleus in each region also leaves open whether crystals in different regions share an orientation. On amorphous SiO₂, the authors explain that each pattern can be crystalline even when orientations vary randomly between regions. Supplementary Fig. 8 shows orientation consistency within individual patterns.

## Which results were measured at 430°C, and which reached 10 μm?

Extended Data Fig. 4a–b shows central nucleation with a ZrO₂ barrier at 430°C. Panels c–d show separate results at 500°C, including a single-crystal flake about 8 μm across and growth filling a 10 μm region. These panels do not establish a 10 μm single crystal and peak mobility together at 430°C. Extended Data Fig. 4e shows a separate low-temperature EF-SCN demonstration on a 2 cm substrate. It should be distinguished from the 400 HfO₂ patterns in main Fig. 2e–g, which have their own sample, conditions, and denominator.

Main Fig. 2 also combines different kinds of evidence. Panel a models oxygen concentration and the formation, etching, and net reaction rates. Panel b uses X-ray diffraction (XRD) to examine HfO₂ structure. Panel c uses X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to follow its atomic ratios over time. Panel d shows optical and SEM images at nine positions on amorphous SiO₂. Panels e–g show 16 regions with 25 patterns each on a 2 cm substrate, or 400 growth sites. The reported single-crystal yield above 99% applies to those growth sites. It does not measure the electrical pass rate of transistors or exhaustive yield on a 300 mm wafer.

## Why can a growth region be too small or too large?

<NewsFigure media="nucleation-current-fig3" lang="en" />

Open the original to enlarge the individual panels. The green curve in a models nucleus formation, blue represents etching-induced suppression, and red is the difference. The SEM images in b–c show regions with no nucleus, one surviving nucleus, and multiple nuclei. Panel d compares the fractions with a single nucleus and with a central nucleus as a function of region size. Panel e fixes the size at 3 μm and varies the flow of the sulfur source, diethyl sulfide (DES), measuring the average number of flakes per pattern. These comparisons vary different parameters. Panel f is a diffusion–reaction simulation, and its iterations do not represent elapsed process time.

Etching from the barrier is strongest near the edges. In a narrow region, suppression zones from opposite edges can overlap and leave no place for a nucleus. Increasing the width can leave an active center. A wider center makes several nuclei more likely. This qualitative example explains the relation between suppressed and active widths. It does not calculate the critical width of a real pattern.

Three equations connect those observations. The first describes oxygen concentration as a function of distance from the barrier. The second subtracts oxygen-driven etching from nucleus formation to give a net reaction rate. The third defines the critical distance at which the rates balance and the net rate is zero. The paper uses a diffusion–reaction model and kinetic Monte Carlo simulations to compare zero, one, and multiple nuclei. In Supplementary Note 3, R_growth denotes the reaction rate associated with forming a nucleus. The lateral growth rate of an existing crystal is a different quantity.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="nucleation-oxygen-profile" title="How does oxygen concentration change away from the barrier?" tex={String.raw`C_{O_2}(x)=C_0\exp(-kx)`}>

This is the final expression of Equation (6) on page 29 of official Supplementary Note 3. It models the spatial concentration profile. It is neither a new diffusion equation nor a complete set of measured concentrations. Here x is the distance inward from the barrier edge, C₀ is oxygen concentration at x = 0, and C_O₂(x) is the concentration at position x. The two concentrations have the same units, and the exponent −kx must be dimensionless. The absolute concentration unit was unconfirmed, so no unit such as mol/m³ is assumed here.

The model assumes that some oxygen moving inward escapes upward. It takes the oxygen flux and concentration to approach zero far away. Fick’s first law connects flux to the concentration gradient and gives the exponential form. With positive k, the concentration falls as x increases. This explains why a nucleus has a better chance of surviving in the center.

Equation (1) in the main text uses A/(Dk) as the prefactor for the same spatial profile. A is the oxygen-flux constant along x and D the oxygen diffusion coefficient. Identifying the concentration at x = 0 as C₀ gives C₀ = A/(Dk). Supplementary Equation (6) gives both forms of the same model.

This is a steady-state expression. The barrier’s oxygen supply can decline over time, and Equation (7) includes a separate time factor f(t). A spatial profile at one instant cannot establish that suppression stays the same throughout the process.

The units of k require care. If x is physical distance, k must have units of inverse length. The preceding discrete flux equation, Equation (2), describes k as a fraction escaping upward. Until the relation between the discrete interval size and the continuous coordinate is clear, inserting a numerical k to obtain a suppression distance would be unjustified. The form of the equation can still be interpreted.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="nucleation-net-rate-balance" title="How do nucleus formation and etching compete?" tex={String.raw`R(x,t)_{\mathrm{Net\ growth}}=G_i-B C_0^m\exp(-mkx)f(t)^m`}>

This is Equation (2) in the main paper. R on the left is the net reaction rate at position x and time t. Here “growth” means **the difference between the rate of nucleus formation and the rate of removal by etching**. The lateral extension of a previously formed crystal is a different process. The first term, Gᵢ, is a constant formation rate in the model. The complete product that follows is the oxygen-driven etching rate.

B is an etching rate constant, C₀ is the oxygen concentration at the barrier edge, and m is the reaction order with respect to oxygen. The order specifies the power to which the concentration is raised. The spatial factor exp(−mkx) weakens with distance from the barrier. The time factor f(t) accounts for a declining oxygen supply. The paper does not supply a specific function or numerical coefficients for f(t) here.

For a fixed Gᵢ, increasing the etching term lowers the net rate and decreasing it raises the net rate. This balance explains how nuclei can be suppressed at the edges and survive farther inside. Its sign alone cannot guarantee exactly one nucleus or a yield above 99%. Pattern size, supply conditions, time dependence, and stochastic outcomes all matter.

Dimensional consistency requires both terms to have the same rate units. The main text gives neither an absolute unit for the rate nor numerical values for all coefficients, so assigning s⁻¹ or μm/s would be unwarranted. The derivation and assumptions are in [official Supplementary Note 3](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-026-11095-1/MediaObjects/41586_2026_11095_MOESM1_ESM.pdf).

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="nucleation-critical-distance" title="Where is the boundary between the suppressed edge and the active interior?" tex={String.raw`x_{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac{1}{mk}\ln\!\left(\frac{B C_0^m f(t)^m}{G_i}\right)`}>

This is Equation (13) on page 30 of official Supplementary Note 3. The critical distance x_crit is the position where the formation and etching terms in the previous equation balance, giving R = 0.

The natural logarithm ln inverts the exponential. Its argument is the ratio of the oxygen-driven etching rate at the edge to the formation rate, which is dimensionless because the two terms have the same units. B, C₀, m, f(t), Gᵢ, and k retain their earlier meanings. The expression requires a nonzero mk and a positive logarithm argument. The resulting boundary must also lie inside the actual growth region.

Supplementary Equation (14) connects the boundary to the width in which nucleation is possible. In a model with an equal suppression width x_crit at each edge, the active width is the full width minus the two suppressed intervals. Increasing pattern size does not automatically preserve an area that supports only one nucleus. Applying that width model directly to nucleus counts in arbitrary triangular and linear patterns would require separate validation.

A boundary in micrometers cannot be calculated without justified values for the coefficients and time factor. The actual single-crystal yield comes from the experimental counts discussed above.

### Check your understanding

Do a single-crystal yield above 99% and a peak mobility of 117 cm²/(V·s) have the same denominator? The yield counts 400 growth sites on a 2 cm substrate. The mobility is a separately reported maximum. The average and relative standard deviation for 50 devices come from yet another set of measurements. Each quantity describes a different aspect: crystallinity at a growth site, peak device performance, or array uniformity.

## Reading the average and variation alongside peak mobility

A field-effect transistor (FET) uses gate voltage to control current in a semiconductor channel. Mobility describes how readily charge moves when an electric field is applied.

<NewsFigure media="nucleation-current-fig4" lang="en" />

Panel a is a structural schematic and b is an SEM image. Panel c shows transfer curves of current versus gate voltage. Panel d shows the distribution of mobilities in 16 devices. The highest value in one device and the distribution across several devices answer different questions. Panels e–i show a separate self-aligned contact structure and measurements. The 50 back-gated devices discussed below form another array.

Peak mobility alone tells little about whether many devices behave similarly. Think of the highest grade in a class versus the distribution of all grades. The analogy illustrates only the difference between a maximum, a mean, and a spread.

A separate experiment in Supplementary Note 8 examined 50 back-gated transistors on a 2 cm SiO₂ substrate. The semiconductor channel through which current flows was 2.4 μm long and 5 μm wide. The reported results at a source–drain voltage of 1 V are as follows.

| Measurement | Mean across 50 devices | Relative standard deviation across devices |
|---|---:|---:|
| Field-effect mobility | 8.56 cm²/(V·s) | 10.45% |
| On current per unit width | 4.69 μA/μm | 14.66% |

Relative standard deviation (RSD) expresses the spread as a fraction of the mean. A smaller RSD indicates less variation relative to the mean. The value 10.45% is neither a defect rate nor a limit within which every device must fall. The current is reported in μA/μm because it is normalized by channel width to compare devices of different widths.

The mean mobility of 8.56 and peak of 117 come from different measurement sets. Their difference cannot be interpreted as a percentage drop in process performance. The paper reports both a high peak and a distribution for a separate 50-device array. Those measurements alone do not establish whether the 50 represent every device on the substrate or what fraction of finished chips would pass.

**Check your understanding.** Does a small RSD guarantee high mobility? The mean describes the performance level, while the RSD describes relative spread around it. Together they distinguish how well devices perform from how similarly they perform.

## The strongest evidence

- Single-crystal MoS₂ reached **up to 10 μm in linear patterns**. The triangular-pattern result was about 7 μm.
- **Single-crystal yield exceeded 99% across 400 growth sites on a 2 cm substrate.** Electrical device yield is a separate measure.
- A separate low-temperature process showed central nucleation at **430°C**.
- Peak FET mobility reached **117 cm²/(V·s)**. The array mean and variation came from a separate measurement set.
- The team integrated several transistors on one shared single crystal (Extended Data Fig. 8).
- They demonstrated lateral heterojunctions joining different materials side by side and self-aligned contacts using the growth position to align the electrodes.

### What did the simulations show, and what did they assume?

The diffusion–reaction simulations in Supplementary Note 7 ran 500 updates for each of ten random seeds per condition. Those updates do not represent 500 seconds of processing. The diffusion step uses arbitrary time units. Varying adsorption and growth probabilities illustrates when zero, one, or several nuclei remain. The model does not calculate reaction barriers and temperature-dependent rate constants from first principles to predict fabrication time.

The simulations assigned the same crystal orientation to every new nucleus from the outset. An orientation shared by merged simulated crystals therefore cannot independently establish spontaneous alignment. Experimental diffraction and atomic imaging provide the evidence for orientation. Comparing simulations with and without oxygen flux helps explain the spatial distribution of nucleation. It does not test assumptions built into both simulations. [Official Supplementary Note 7, PDF pages 35–39. The text was reviewed. The videos were not.]

## Main limitations

The barrier has a finite oxygen supply. Supplementary Note 4 describes how spatial selectivity weakens as oxygen in HfO₂ is depleted. Bilayer nuclei eventually appear at random positions on an existing monolayer. Conditions for creating one central nucleus initially are distinct from those for sustaining selectivity during long growth. The rapid oxygen decrease observed in the first ten minutes at 750°C cannot be applied as the timetable at every temperature.

Larger regions need more than a geometric scale-up. Supplementary Notes 5–6 explain how a wider active center leaves room for multiple nuclei. Changing the sulfur precursor DES primarily controlled nucleus count. Changing the molybdenum precursor MHC primarily controlled lateral growth rate. In a small region, suppression may eliminate all nuclei. In a large one, it may allow several. Precursor supply therefore has to match region size. Extending single crystals to tens of micrometers is a projection conditional on sufficient lateral growth, with yield at that scale still to be demonstrated. [Supplementary Notes 4–6, PDF pages 31–34, Fig. 12 on page 14, and Fig. 14 on page 16.](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-026-11095-1/MediaObjects/41586_2026_11095_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)

A 10 μm single crystal in a linear pattern and an array on a 2 cm substrate are separate achievements. Together they do not establish a 2 cm single crystal or manufacturing on 200–300 mm wafers. The process window, oxygen release, pattern geometry, and precursor supply all need to be matched.

The peak mobility of 117 cm²/(V·s) describes a particular device. It cannot establish contact resistance, threshold-voltage variation, or yield throughout a large array.

Several authors disclosed roles as founders, employees, or directors of TDS Innovation, a company working on 2D semiconductor materials, processes, and equipment.

## Why the result matters

Using 2D semiconductors in device arrays requires reproducibly growing crystals of similar shape at intended coordinates. EF-SCN aims to control **the position where a nucleus forms inside each selected growth region**. Its applicability requires testing combinations of material, substrate, precursor supply, and barrier conditions.

## What would justify a fresh assessment?

- Does spatial selectivity persist at wafer scale?
- Do device yield and variation meet semiconductor-manufacturing requirements?
- Can the process be integrated with existing circuits without transferring the material to another substrate?
- Is the result reproducible for other 2D materials?
- Can independent groups reproduce it?
