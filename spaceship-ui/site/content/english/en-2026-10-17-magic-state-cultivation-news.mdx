## 1. In one sentence

**Google Quantum AI ran magic state cultivation on its Willow superconducting processor and reduced logical error roughly fortyfold relative to state injection alone. Only 8% of attempts survived selection in the characterization circuit. Practical algorithms still require lower error and scaling to larger codes.**

## 2. Why it is difficult

A physical qubit is an actual quantum device. A logical qubit uses multiple physical qubits to protect information while checking for errors. Error correction measures traces of faults to preserve logical information even while the individual devices remain imperfect. Here, state injection puts a prepared state into a logical qubit. Postselection discards runs that fail checks and uses only the remainder.

Surface codes can protect logical qubits, but direct implementation of the non-Clifford operations needed for universal computation is expensive. One approach prepares high-purity magic states and consumes them in gate teleportation. Repeated distillation of those states can require a factory with a large physical-qubit and time budget. The paper puts the required state error at about <Math tex={"10^{-10}"} /> for factoring RSA-2048 and <Math tex={"10^{-6}\\sim10^{-8}"} /> for some quantum simulations and optimization problems.

## 3. Basic concepts

The target is a logical T state prepared by a rotation about the Y axis. The vertical bar and angle bracket denote a quantum state. The equation applies a rotation operator to the prepared reference state.

<Math display tex={"|T\\rangle=e^{-i\\pi\\hat{\\sigma}_y/8}|0\\rangle"} />

Here <Math tex={"|0\\rangle"} /> is the reference state, <Math tex={"i"} /> is the imaginary unit and <Math tex={"\\hat\\sigma_y"} /> is the Pauli operator along the Y axis. The angle and operator in the exponent are dimensionless. The paper uses a Y-axis rotation convention. Some textbooks define a T gate using a Z-axis rotation, so check the convention before combining those expressions (arXiv v1, Section II).

Cultivation acts as a filter. It measures the logical Hadamard observable <Math tex={"\\hat H_L"} /> and retains runs that pass the test for the desired eigenstate. Runs with detected errors are discarded. Higher fidelity comes at the cost of a lower probability of retention. State infidelity and the fraction of attempts retained measure different things. Below, <Math tex={"\\epsilon=1-F"} /> denotes infidelity. The error measured over the entire kickback tomography (KT) circuit is a separate quantity.

Fidelity <Math tex={"F"} /> is a dimensionless measure of how closely a state matches the target. The retainment rate is the fraction of attempts left after selection. These quantities refer to different populations. In a **hypothetical calculation**, a retainment rate of 8% over 1,000 attempts would leave about 80. High quality among those 80 says little about the discarded attempts.

## 4. Limits of existing methods

The prevailing approach, distillation, combines several lower-quality magic states in a logical circuit to make one better state. It is powerful but requires repeated rounds and a large factory. Simple state injection costs less, but a single physical error can propagate into a logical error. A 2024 cultivation proposal called for repeated checks, code growth and stabilization after injection, followed by an escape step that moves the prepared state into a computation code. The broader approach can therefore grow beyond a fixed, small filter. This particular hardware experiment tested cultivation in a distance-3 color code followed by transfer to a distance-5 surface code.

## 5. The three stages

First, inject the noisy state of one physical qubit into a logical state of the distance-3 color code. Second, use phase kickback on a helper qubit to check <Math tex={"\\hat H_L"} /> twice, retaining only runs with no stabilizer or flag detector events. A time-reversed circuit reverses the color-code stabilizers while catching additional errors. Third, graft the surviving state onto a larger lattice compatible with a distance-5 surface code and run quantum error correction (QEC) cycles.

## 6. The circuit and its central identity

Measuring an observable and applying a gate are distinct operations. Here H_L is a logical observable whose eigenvalue is read out through phase kickback. Runs survive when they give the desired +1 result and pass the error checks. The H below is a decomposition of the single-qubit gate used to implement that measurement circuit. Reproducing the full measurement requires more than the identity itself.

<Math display tex={"\\hat H=\\hat T^{\\dagger}\\hat X\\hat T,\\qquad \\hat T=e^{-i\\pi\\hat\\sigma_y/8}"} />

This **operator decomposition identity** appears in Section I of the public version (PDF page 2). Read the operators from right to left. Rotate with <Math tex={"\\hat T"} />, apply the bit-flip operator <Math tex={"\\hat X"} /> and rotate back with the inverse <Math tex={"\\hat T^{\\dagger}"} />. The net operation equals the Hadamard operator <Math tex={"\\hat H"} />. The target-state definition, this circuit decomposition and the error estimate each serve a distinct purpose.

The low-distance color code applies this single-qubit Clifford operation transversally, limiting error propagation. A successful state escapes into a surface code with lower idle error. The stages covered here run from state injection through cultivation and flag checks, QEC, kickback tomography and grafting.

## 7. Experiment and results

Kickback tomography found a logical error of <Math tex={"4.4(2)\\times10^{-3}"} /> with injection alone. With cultivation, the overall circuit error fell to about <Math tex={"1\\sim2\\times10^{-4}"} />, a roughly fortyfold improvement. The abstract reports a state fidelity of 0.9999(1). The circuit used 216 physical two-qubit gates and 54 measurements. Around the target angle θ = π/4, the Figure 3 KT circuit used for this characterization retained about 8% of attempts. This fraction applies to a circuit with two additional cultivation rounds and three error-correction cycles. Other cultivation designs can have different production yields.

In the control without QEC cycles, the error was roughly 100 times higher. Four color-code QEC cycles improved logical performance by about a factor of 100 even though each added 30 two-qubit gates and six measurements. Figure 4 shows a 54-qubit grafting experiment transferring the cultivated state to a distance-5 code. After one additional cycle, finite cycle error moved the Bloch vector toward the origin.

## 8. Counterevidence

The 8% retainment near T-state injection applies to the entire KT characterization circuit with two extra cultivation rounds and three error-correction cycles (public version, Section II). A production factory has a broader set of costs. Error over the complete KT circuit, including its readout, serves to estimate an upper bound on error in the prepared state. The two errors need not be equal.

A small error is itself difficult to measure. Section II of the public version puts both the size of the KT error bound and the uncertainty in its estimate at about <Math tex={"10^{-4}"} />. The reported 0.9999 therefore carries uncertainty and cannot give the success probability of a complete quantum computer.

A fidelity of 99.99% does not establish successful execution of a useful large-scale algorithm. The researchers found that standard transversal tomography could overestimate purity under coherent noise. They introduced kickback tomography to use the cultivation circuit itself as the measuring instrument. The roughly fortyfold improvement uses injection alone as its baseline. A comparison with a complete distillation factory would require its total space–time cost at the same target error.

### Why do more measurements fail to resolve the problem?

Tomography estimates a quantum state by combining measurements along different directions, here X, Y and Z. When runs with detected errors are discarded, the surviving population can vary with measurement direction. Combining components from different populations as if they were coordinates of a single state can introduce bias. Taking more samples addresses statistical fluctuations, while the selection bias remains.

In a **theoretical counterexample** in supplementary Section IX, about 82.0% of X measurements, 94.7% of Y measurements and 100.0% of Z measurements survive. Combining the retained results gives state coordinates outside the physically allowed region. These fractions illustrate direction-dependent selection. The 8% retainment measured on Willow comes from a different setting.

**Check your understanding.** Would taking more measurements with the same biased selection solve the problem? Random error might shrink while the directional components would still come from different populations. The researchers therefore chose a single self-consistent measurement using the cultivation circuit. This explanation follows supplementary Sections IX–X of the public version. ## What each public circuit file represents

The data repository’s README distinguishes the experimental Cirq circuits from the Stim circuits used for analysis. The Stim representation changes non-Clifford operations and other details to support analysis of detector regions and error models. Running an arbitrary published file therefore cannot by itself reproduce the hardware experiment.

Raw observables and observables following decoding and postselection also reside in separate files. The postselected results alone cannot reveal how many attempts were discarded. To check both error and retainment, connect measurement records, detector events and processing rules.

Even the examples in the public `Example_usage.ipynb` use different selection rules for different figures. For Figure 2, cell 9 rejects a run if any of its detectors records an event. For Figure 4, cell 16 selects cultivation detectors identified by four-component detector coordinates in the Stim circuit, then keeps runs without events at those detectors. The selection thus covers different sets of events. Cell 17 also describes its result as postselected but undecoded.

## 9. Limitations

The KT characterization setup discarded about 92% of runs during selection. Other cultivation circuits and complete factories may have different discard rates. The paper reports that device drift produced fluctuations in logical error on the order of <Math tex={"10^{-4}"} /> over about an hour. Retainment in the grafting experiment varied by about 10% with cycle count. The experiment used a single architecture, Google’s 105-qubit Willow. Independent hardware reproduction, real-time T-gate consumption, larger code distances, a matching decoder and magic-state supply rates at application scale remain unverified. Google built the device and funded and conducted the research.

## 10. What changed in practice

Cultivation progressed from a proposal to save resources to a working sequence on superconducting hardware. The team moved a state from a color code to a surface code and used a control experiment to expose a weakness in conventional tomography. These results do not establish when a practical quantum computer will be available.

## 11. When to revisit

Reassess after reproduction on an independent processor, a logical T-state throughput measurement including retainment, grafting to larger code distances, real-time gate teleportation, a full qubit–time comparison of cultivation and distillation at matching fidelity, or independent analysis of the final Science version and public data.
