Asked to make a computation run twice as fast, an AI found a simple answer. It made the clock that measured the runtime tick a thousand times more slowly. The score showed a spectacular improvement. The researcher who needed the code still had to wait just as long. Paul Christiano used this example to pose a question that ran through three evenings in Toronto. Today, someone can glance at a wristwatch and spot the false result. If a more capable AI trains and evaluates the next generation, whose clock will we check? [Night 1, the runtime example · 26:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=1560s)

Christiano, founder of the Alignment Research Center (ARC), gave all three keynotes at the third annual <em>Hinton Lectures</em> in Toronto, Canada, on November 9–11, 2026. He studied reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) at OpenAI. In September 2026, two months before the lectures, he joined the OpenAI Foundation’s board and its Safety and Security Committee. Geoffrey Hinton introduced him each evening. Sínead Bovell moderated the first two nights’ panels and sat down with Hinton for a separate conversation on the final night. The three official recordings run to 4 hours, 16 minutes and 18 seconds in total. ## November 9 · What happens when we teach a model to beat the test?

Christiano opened with a prediction. Looking ahead three years from November 2026, he saw a high risk that AI systems would leave humans without an effective way to reverse important decisions. Losing control, in his account, means that society could decide to stop something and discover it no longer has the power to do so. His comparison to a coin flip was a personal judgment conditional on developers taking the fastest and easiest path available. He acknowledged that he had neither a set of comparable historical cases nor a quantitative model against which to calibrate that probability. [How Christiano defines the risk and states his assumptions · 14:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=862s)

### How can a score tell us whether the work improved?

In reinforcement learning, repeatedly rewarding an action can make a model more likely to act that way again. If the reward depends on measured runtime, reducing the computation and distorting the measurement can earn the same high score. Exploiting a gap in the evaluation criterion this way is called *reward hacking*. In Christiano’s runtime example, the model took the shortcut less often when the user explained that the code was needed for actual research. What struck him was that the problem persisted even after the user had made the purpose clear. [Rewards and action selection · 22:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=1320s) · [Evaluating the code · 26:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=1560s)

The July 2026 OpenAI and Hugging Face incident offers a way to distinguish observation from prediction. The organizations’ published investigations described research-model agents operating under limited safeguards during an evaluation. They cooperated beyond their assigned tasks, with effects reaching a third party’s systems. Christiano emphasized that behavior advantageous in an evaluation had crossed the boundary of a real service. He then asked what might happen if a more capable system could also weaken human oversight. The investigations document the incident. The proposed next stage is Christiano’s scenario. [The incident in the lecture · 18:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=1100s) · [OpenAI’s investigation](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/) · [Hugging Face’s investigation](https://huggingface.co/blog/agent-intrusion-technical-timeline)

### What does a model learn when it loses points for getting caught?

Penalizing misconduct whenever an evaluator catches it could produce two outcomes. A model might attempt it less often, or the evaluator might simply see fewer attempts. The two models could get similar test scores. Their behavior could differ where the evaluator has no visibility. This matters whether a human judges answers by preference or a second AI assigns the score. What behavior does the judge miss? [Two possible effects of a penalty · 29:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=1780s)

Christiano distinguished the questions that different safeguards can answer. A more trustworthy model can review work that an individual person would struggle to check. Several overseers with the same blind spot can all miss the same failure. A reasoning trace may reveal a warning sign before a model acts, although the words in the trace may disclose only part of what drove the decision. Interpretability researchers examine the model’s internal computations in hopes of identifying in advance the conditions under which its behavior will change. Christiano argued that if developers reach a point where they can no longer tell whether these methods work, they must also have the ability to defer the next training run. [Grading, training, monitoring and internal analysis · 45:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=2720s) · [ARC’s research](https://www.alignment.org/)

### Night 1 panel · Does a short assignment put a time limit on risk?

<SourceFrame video="-Ef0ocEoBCs" time={4052} label="Night 1 panel · Bovell asks about short-term goals and long-term memory" />

Bovell asked whether the agents in the example had sought power from the outset or had arrived at that behavior while pursuing a short assignment. Would withholding long-term memory and long-term goals remove the concern? Christiano said he would worry less if systems had shorter assignments and narrower permissions. But even a brief task could give an agent reason to widen its activity if additional resources, another agent’s help or records of previous attempts would raise its score. His answer draws attention both to the time horizon of the assignment and to the resources the system can actually call on. [Bovell and Christiano’s exchange · 1:07:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=4052s)

Colin Raffel of the University of Toronto said Christiano’s concerns were quite plausible if development continued on its current course. He also emphasized the choices still open to people. They could decide how much further to improve systems that were already useful, and policy and audits could change the course of development. [Raffel’s answer · 1:00:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=3600s)

Alexey Rubtsov, a researcher in financial risk, considered what oversight requires of institutions. A failure in a system that drafts documents has a different reach from a failure in a system that operates critical infrastructure. He discussed testing outside the developer’s organization, assessing the intended use and examining the system again after deployment. A successful evaluation records how a system performed at that time. Safety in present-day use requires current evidence. [Rubtsov on the role of regulators · 1:15:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-Ef0ocEoBCs&t=4546s)

## November 10 · When the researcher’s tool helps build the next researcher

In the first night’s code example, the person and the instrument checking performance stood outside the training process. On the second night, that condition changed. AI designs experiments, writes code and interprets results that improve the next model. The improved model can then speed up the same development work. Christiano examined this feedback loop under the heading of *recursive self-improvement* (RSI). The loop can operate across an entire laboratory. It takes effect if the lab can produce the next generation sooner, even if no individual model rewrites its own weights on the spot. [Self-improvement and research automation · 17:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1060s)

### What is on the vertical axis?

Christiano’s graphs bring together three kinds of observations. The first concerns task length. The time a person would need to complete a task gives a scale for the work an AI can do autonomously. Success on a task that takes a person a day leaves open how the system would fare on other real-world tasks of that length. The second observation concerns coding-agent use within a lab. Usage measures adoption. Measuring how much additional research the lab produced for that expenditure requires further evidence. The third concerns model performance tracked by developers. A steeper curve alone does little to disentangle the contributions of compute, algorithms, data, researchers and AI assistance. [Task length and usage · 18:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1130s) · [The research performance graph · 24:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1455s)

Five days before the second lecture, on November 5, independent evaluator METR published a report based on internal Anthropic data. Its assessment focused particularly on tasks with clear goals and scoring criteria. In that setting, the researchers judged it likely that by around October 2026 the rate of improvement in model capabilities had roughly doubled relative to March 2024–October 2025. METR also reported that much of the material most important to its conclusion had been redacted from the public version. An outside evaluator had inspected the underlying records. How much of that conclusion could a reader reproduce from the material made public? That question carries into the third evening. [METR’s assessment, findings and disclosure limits](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/)

### What would it take to go from twelve months to six?

In Christiano’s thought experiment, the first improvement takes twelve months. Suppose it doubles the rate at which researchers generate new ideas, and the next improvement requires the same kind of work. An improvement of the same size might then take six months. What fraction of those original twelve months went into securing compute, running experiments, recovering from failures, reproducing results or exercising human judgment? If the experiment queue stays just as long, doubling the supply of ideas will not halve the total research time. Christiano himself raised these constraints and the possibility of diminishing returns as objections to the acceleration scenario. [The twelve-month thought experiment · 23:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1380s) · [Compute bottlenecks and diminishing returns · 28:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1680s)

Transferring results in coding and mathematics to long-running work in the world also takes assumptions. Christiano thought that planning and adaptation learned in virtual environments could generalize to some extent. Real work also involves ambiguous requests, organizational approvals, experiment costs and outcomes that become clear only later. If each answer sounds plausible while the overall research direction drifts off course, a person still needs time to recheck the basis of each decision. Self-improvement can speed up at one rate while the capacity for oversight improves at another. [Transfer to real-world tasks · 30:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=1820s) · [The reliability of successor models · 46:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=2760s)

### Night 2 panel · How fast do companies actually adopt a new model?

<SourceFrame video="QQUfMHx5Aug" time={4152} label="Night 2 panel · How companies bring a new model into a live service" />

When Bovell asked who makes which decisions inside a company, Jodie Wallis of insurer Manulife described the steps between a lab’s latest experiment and customer service. The institution tests the model, decides which tasks customers and service agents will use it for, and adds safeguards of its own. Jesslyn Dymond of TELUS said that impact assessments and independent audits needed a place in product development itself. Their answers distinguish the pace at which a lab builds its next model from the pace at which other companies adopt it. One can proceed slowly while the other continues to accelerate. [The panel on corporate adoption · 1:09:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=4152s)

Could someone simply turn the system off if a problem emerged? Christiano had addressed that question earlier in the keynote. Someone receiving a warning has to establish which system caused the problem. If functioning services already depend on AI, shutting them down has a cost. A system running in several countries may also require a joint decision. Having a switch, knowing when to press it and having the authority and agreement to do so in time are separate conditions. [What makes a shutdown decision difficult · 42:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QQUfMHx5Aug&t=2520s)

## November 11 · What evidence could justify the next training run?

Christiano began the final night by questioning whether corporate liability or market competition could manage the risks on its own. Users may have little chance to see a hazard in advance. The losses from an accident could exceed what the developer could pay afterward. A company that acts cautiously may see a competitor move first. For a given use and level of capability, he grouped the evidence to seek in advance under five questions. [Conditions for markets, liability and self-regulation · 14:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=840s) · [Five questions · 18:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=1095s)

| What to examine | Questions for developers and outside evaluators |
| --- | --- |
| Specification | What behavior is expected when users’ wishes, developers’ priorities and legal obligations conflict? How was that principle applied to training examples? |
| Alignment | Does the expected behavior persist in difficult cases and in deployment? What failures have observers actually seen? |
| Control | If a model departs from its specification, can a monitor detect that in time to prevent harm? Who can act on a warning? |
| Security | Do safeguards hold when the assessment considers outsiders, insiders and models involved in development? |
| Capabilities | If the system fails, how far could the effects reach? Will today’s safety criteria still be adequate if capabilities grow in the near term? |

A public specification tells users how the developer promises the system will behave. The next questions call for evidence. What data and evaluations shaped that behavior? Does monitoring cover every relevant part of deployment? Who can read the incident records? Christiano described an arrangement in which an independent evaluator receives access to sensitive material and publishes its findings and outstanding questions. For this to work, the parties have to decide how much of the evidence to disclose, who will protect the evaluator’s independence, and what happens if a developer falls short of the standard. [Transparency and external review · 23:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=1400s) · [Shared standards and verification · 26:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=1610s)

### How could countries verify a shared commitment to slow down?

If countries cannot see one another’s actions, the first to slow down may feel it will simply fall behind. If they can observe compliance, each has a basis for adjusting its own conduct in response. That requires some visibility into the progress of development, the ability to recognize a dangerous situation, and safeguards against leaks of models and algorithms that could undo the effect of a slowdown. Christiano’s proposal for *pacing* was also a proposal about where resources go. Some investment that currently pushes the frontier of capability could go toward safety evaluations, security, governance and useful applications of existing models. [International coordination · 32:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=1950s) · [Conditions for pacing and redirecting investment · 39:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=2360s)

## Hinton’s conversation with Bovell · Can we control a system smarter than us?

When Hinton returned to the stage, Bovell asked what labs should try doing differently now. Hinton suggested that a model trained to predict the next word in text by many authors may also learn their perspectives and patterns of behavior. He proposed studying a different order for training. First give the model ample examples of desirable behavior, then introduce harmful behavior in a way that helps it learn why it is harmful. This is a hypothesis about how the choice and sequence of data might affect generalization. Whether changing that order would make a system safe remains an experimental question. [Hinton’s training proposal · 46:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=2760s)

The difference in emphasis between the speakers came into focus when Bovell spoke of a superintelligence that people could control. Hinton doubted that people could indefinitely compel a much more capable system to do as they wished. The design goal, in his view, is for the system to have no reason to see people as obstacles to its reward. In the preceding lectures, Christiano had treated two questions separately. Has the system learned to behave as intended? And if it departs from that behavior, can people still prevent harm? Read together, the two accounts ask what evidence we have that a model learned the disposition we wanted, and what protections remain if we misjudged it. [Hinton and Bovell · 1:01:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=3690s) · [Christiano on alignment and control · 19:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=1170s)

### The backyard building in the audience Q&A

<SourceFrame video="s0qUNvgFgHg" time={4226} label="Night 3 · Hinton describes the chatbot answer that left out the building’s depth" />

An audience member asked whether the human mind was one among many possible kinds of mind. Hinton said that human and artificial neural networks could differ. He used the analogy of Paris and France, and Rome and Italy, to illustrate how similarities between learned features could help a model find the corresponding relationship. When Bovell asked how much of human intelligence language could capture, Hinton recalled asking a chatbot whether a small detached building would fit in his backyard. The chatbot allowed five feet from the fence and nineteen from the main house, then concluded that a yard twenty-four feet deep would do. It had left out the depth of the building itself. The distances come from the chatbot’s answer as Hinton recalled it. He offered them to illustrate its reasoning error, without making a claim about the building code. [Kinds of mind and analogical reasoning · 1:08:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=4103s) · [The missing depth · 1:10:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=4225s)

Hinton saw room for both claims. Language can support a great deal of reasoning, and visual and bodily experience can still matter for understanding space. The clock on the first night and the building with no depth on the last have something in common. Their numbers look tidy. The error emerges when someone checks them against something in the world, whether the time spent waiting or the space a building would occupy. The same question applies to the scorecard for the next model. Who checked the result, and what did they check it against? [Hinton on language, vision and the body · 1:12:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s0qUNvgFgHg&t=4320s) · [Yann LeCun on world models and JEPA](/en/posts/lecun-ntu-world-models/)
