Finding a material that emits at the desired wavelength and making it into an electrically driven device are different tasks. Erbium ions (<Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{Er}^{3+}`} />) can emit near-infrared light at about 1.54 μm, but they absorb light weakly on their own. An electrical device also needs to transport charge to the emitting sites. Here, <strong>chromium (<Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{Cr}^{3+}`} />) absorbs and delivers energy, the semiconductor host helps transport charge, and a surface treatment reduces losses</strong>. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<small>This review uses the full PDF and supplementary information for the peer-reviewed accepted manuscript released by Nature Communications on September 19, 2026. The equation numbers refer to this article’s worked explanations. Emission decay time, stability under continuous light exposure, and operating lifetime under electrical drive are treated as separate measurements.</small> <a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

## 1. Why emission at 1.54 μm is only the starting point

A wavelength of 1.54 μm is 1,540 nm, beyond visible light in the near-infrared region relevant to optical communications. Transitions between erbium’s internal energy levels can produce a narrow emission band near this wavelength. Making a bright, efficient source requires delivering enough energy to the excited state and letting it radiate before heat or defects dissipate the energy. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The paper studies <Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{Cs}_3\mathrm{CrBr}_6:\mathrm{Er}^{3+}`} /> nanocrystals. Er after the colon denotes an emitting ion incorporated into the host crystal. Chromium already present in the host acts as a sensitizer, taking up energy over a broad wavelength range and transferring it to Er. The host is also a semiconductor, enabling pathways for carrying charge into the emitting layer. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The researchers attached an organic molecule called pBBA to the surface. Nanocrystals have a high proportion of surface atoms. Incompletely bonded sites on their surfaces can provide routes for energy loss. Surface passivation suppresses these defect-related pathways. Chromium sensitization and pBBA passivation address different parts of the energy flow. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

<CandidateEquation id="erled-photon" lang="en" title="How much energy does one 1.54 μm photon carry?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}E_\gamma&=\frac{hc}{\lambda}\\&\simeq\frac{1.240}{1.54}\,\mathrm{eV}\\&\simeq0.805\,\mathrm{eV}\end{aligned}\tag{1}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`E_\gamma`} /> denotes the energy of one photon, <Math tex={String.raw`h`} /> Planck’s constant, <Math tex={String.raw`c`} /> the speed of light, and <Math tex={String.raw`\lambda`} /> the wavelength. Using <Math tex={String.raw`hc\simeq1.240\,\mathrm{eV}\,\mu\mathrm m`} /> allows wavelength in μm to give energy in eV. Because wavelength is in the denominator, a longer-wavelength photon carries less energy.

Substituting 1.54 μm gives about 0.805 eV. Entering 1.54 nm would give a photon energy a thousand times larger. The units are part of the calculation. This example uses physical constants to convert a wavelength into energy and makes no estimate of the paper’s measured efficiency.

An electrical device also has charge-injection requirements and losses across several layers. Dividing photon energy by elementary charge does not determine the actual turn-on voltage. The voltage reported in the device experiment measures a different quantity.

</CandidateEquation>

## 2. Figure 1: locating erbium in the material

Panels 1a and 1b illustrate the crystal before and after erbium incorporation. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and lattice images that follow show the particles’ shape and interplanar spacing. The reported mean size of the plate-like particles changes from about 19.1 to 19.6 nm, while the compared lattice spacing increases from 0.291 to 0.296 nm. These measurements track structural changes that accompany incorporation. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The elemental maps in Figure 1g–j use different colors for Cs, Cr, Br, and Er. Look for the Er signal coinciding with a particle. Color intensity does not establish equal Er concentration everywhere or directly give the atomic fraction. Mapping spatial distribution and quantitatively measuring composition answer different questions.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) in Figure 1k probes the periodic crystal structure, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in 1l provides information about elemental chemical states. Combining these observations, the authors associate Er incorporation with Cr sites in the host lattice. <strong>This establishes how the materials being compared in later optical tests differ.</strong> Light-emission efficiency is measured later. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Concentration labels likewise require care. The researchers varied the amount of Er supplied when preparing each sample. ICP-OES measurements found that actual incorporation was lower than the feed ratio. Values such as 0.3%, 1.2%, and 4.8% below describe nominal feed conditions. They do not mean exactly that fraction of sites in every nanocrystal was replaced. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

## 3. Figure 2: connecting absorption, emission, and energy transfer

<NewsFigure media="erled-fig-2" lang="en" />

Figure 2a compares absorption and optical energy ranges. Panel 2b shows where optically excited samples emit. Er incorporation adds narrow emission at about 1,545 nm alongside broad visible emission from the host. The Er signal initially increases with feed concentration and then decreases at higher concentrations. How energy moves between ions and where it is lost matter as well as the amount of Er. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<strong>Figure 2c is a photoluminescence excitation (PLE) measurement: detection wavelength is fixed while excitation wavelength varies.</strong> The detector monitors 1,545 nm emission while the experiment scans the color of the incident light. The horizontal axis is thus the input wavelength. Features around 400, 600, and 800 nm support the interpretation that absorption associated with Cr helps excite Er. The emission and excitation spectra address opposite sides of this process. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Figure 2d shows how the Cr energy levels change with the crystal field. The arrangement of surrounding ions affects electronic energies and supplies intermediate states that can receive energy and transfer it to another ion. Read the relative levels alongside the absorption features in 2a and 2c. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<strong>Figure 2i brings the energy-transfer paths together.</strong> On the right are the host’s ground state (GS), free exciton (FE), and self-trapped exciton (STE). The Cr levels are in the center and the Er levels on the left. Upward arrows denote excitation by energy absorption. Connections between centers denote transfer, and downward emission arrows show energy leaving as light. A transition from a low-lying excited Er level to its ground level gives emission near 1,545 nm. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The paper discusses several routes by which host excitation and Cr-centered absorption lead to Er emission. An “energy relay” captures the idea, provided it leaves room for more than one direct Cr-to-Er step. A comparison host without Cr and time-resolved measurements provide additional evidence of chromium’s role. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

## 4. The time axes help explain the brightness change

Figures 2e and 2f both plot emission decays, but the detection wavelengths and time scales differ. Panel 2e follows host emission near 460 nm over nanoseconds (ns). Panel 2f follows Er emission at 1,545 nm over milliseconds (ms). Calling both a “lifetime” without units would mix scales that differ by a factor of a million. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

With increasing Er feed, the host decay in 2e shortens from about 3.64 to 2.25 ns. The authors associate that change with an additional route for host energy to reach Er. In 2f, Er decay increases from 2.16 ms at low feed to 4.76 ms at 1.2%, then drops to 1.73 ms at 4.8%. The interpretation is competition between reduced defect losses at low concentration and concentration quenching at high concentration. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

A faster decay has more than one possible interpretation. A donor may decay faster because it transfers energy efficiently. An emitting center may decay faster because it loses energy without emitting. Ask <strong>which center was observed and where the departing energy went</strong>. That is why the paper combines absorption, emission, and time-resolved measurements.

<CandidateEquation id="erled-rates" lang="en" title="How can a decay rate be divided between light emission and loss?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}k_r&=\frac{\Phi}{\tau}\\k_{nr}&=\frac{1-\Phi}{\tau}\end{aligned}\tag{2}`}>

Here <Math tex={String.raw`\Phi`} /> abbreviates the PLQY in the paper’s Equations (3) and (4). <Math tex={String.raw`k_r`} /> is the radiative transition rate, <Math tex={String.raw`k_{nr}`} /> the nonradiative rate, and <Math tex={String.raw`\tau`} /> the effective emission decay time. Enter <Math tex={String.raw`\Phi`} /> as a fraction between zero and one. The total decay rate is <Math tex={String.raw`1/\tau`} />, and the fraction producing a photon is <Math tex={String.raw`\Phi`} />. The remaining fraction is therefore <Math tex={String.raw`1-\Phi`} />.

For a hypothetical sample with <Math tex={String.raw`\Phi=0.40`} /> and <Math tex={String.raw`\tau=1.0\,\mathrm{ms}=0.001\,\mathrm{s}`} />, the radiative rate is 400 s⁻¹ and the nonradiative rate 600 s⁻¹. Their sum is 1,000 s⁻¹. Its reciprocal is the original decay time, 0.001 s. The units and sum show why the calculation divides by time and subtracts the yield from one.

The example provides no new measurement of a transition rate in this paper. To apply it, pair yield and decay time from the same sample under the same excitation conditions. Mixing a film with a solution or treating a complex multichannel decay as one fully resolved transition rate would exceed the model’s scope.

</CandidateEquation>

Temperature-dependent measurements also probe where the energy goes. Figure 2g–h compares thermal quenching as emission decreases with temperature. The authors fit an Arrhenius expression that activates a loss pathway as temperature rises. Each fitted energy belongs to a particular sample and emission band. It cannot stand in for a single thermal-stability value across all samples. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<small>In Equation (1) of the accepted manuscript, the definitions of the donor decay-time symbols for undoped and doped samples may reverse the order used in the equation. This article does not silently amend the labels or recalculate transfer efficiency. It follows the measured decay curves at each detection wavelength and Equations (3) and (4) in the manuscript.</small>

## 5. Surface treatment reduces loss after energy transfer

Even when Cr delivers excitation to Er, surface defects can divert energy and reduce emission. The authors added para-bromobenzoic acid (pBBA) as a surface ligand. The molecular drawing, infrared spectra, and XPS changes in the paper’s Figure 4 support ligand binding. This treatment targets loss through a different mechanism from adding more erbium. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The optimal sample’s peak PLQY rose from 38.5% before treatment to 47% after treatment. The emission decay in the compared films also increased from 1.04 to 1.24 ms. The authors interpret the changes using radiative and nonradiative rates and defect-density analysis, connecting the surface chemistry to energy loss beyond a visual comparison of sample brightness. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

<CandidateEquation id="erled-plqy" lang="en" title="Does a PLQY of 47% count all the incident light?" tex={String.raw`\Phi=\frac{N_{\gamma,\mathrm{emitted}}}{N_{\gamma,\mathrm{absorbed}}}\tag{3}`}>

The numerator counts emitted photons and the denominator counts photons actually absorbed by the sample. Photons that were reflected or transmitted without absorption are excluded from the denominator. Multiplying the ratio by 100% gives a percentage.

Suppose the incident beam delivers 10,000 photons, of which 4,000 are absorbed and 1,880 are emitted. Then <Math tex={String.raw`1880/4000=0.47`} />, giving 47% PLQY. Using incident photons as the denominator gives <Math tex={String.raw`1880/10000=0.188`} />, or 18.8%. The values differ because they answer different counting questions.

Photon number and optical energy are different quantities. If the sample is excited by short-wavelength light and emits at a longer wavelength, an emitted photon carries less energy than an absorbed photon. A 47% photon-number yield therefore does not give a 47% energy yield. Electrical efficiency requires examining charge injection separately.

</CandidateEquation>

The paper also tests stability under continuous illumination. After 48 hours under a 404 nm laser at 14.35 mW cm⁻², the treated sample retained 84.3% of its initial emission and the untreated sample retained 47.3%. This is <strong>material stability under optical excitation</strong>. The operating lifetime of an electrically driven LED is a separate experiment below. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

## 6. Figure 5: producing light under electrical drive

<NewsFigure media="erled-fig-5" lang="en" />

Figure 5a shows the LED stack. On the transparent bottom-electrode side, ZnO/PEI-based layers and LiF supply electrons. The nanocrystal emitting layer lies in the middle. On the other side, TCTA/MoO₃-based layers supply holes next to the metal electrode. Panel 5b shows energy levels and injection directions. <strong>The layers provide a route for electrons and holes to reach the emitting material.</strong> <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Panel 5c shows the characteristic electroluminescence spectrum near 1.54 μm across different applied voltages. The excitation now comes from electrical injection. Panel 5d shows current density versus voltage. After surface treatment, the turn-on voltage falls from about 7.2 to 6.7 V. Check the logarithmic scale before comparing large and small currents on the same plot. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Panel 5e plots external quantum efficiency (EQE) against current density. The text and caption report that the peak rose from 1.14% in the control to 3.26% in the treated device. Efficiency varies along the drive curve. The peak at one point and the efficiency at the current required for sustained use can differ. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<small>The orange annotation in original Figure 5e appears to read 3.24%. The text, caption, and supplementary statistics report 3.26%. The table follows the text and statistics, and the original image is unchanged.</small>

<CandidateEquation id="erled-eqe" lang="en" title="How does the denominator of 3.26% LED EQE differ from that of PLQY?" tex={String.raw`\mathrm{EQE}=\frac{N_{\gamma,\mathrm{out}}}{N_{e,\mathrm{in}}}\times100\%\tag{4}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`N_{e,\mathrm{in}}`} /> counts electrons injected from the external circuit in a given interval, and <Math tex={String.raw`N_{\gamma,\mathrm{out}}`} /> counts photons leaving the device during the same interval. EQE starts with injected charge, unlike PLQY, which starts with absorbed photons. Carriers must reach the emitting layer, recombine with limited losses, and produce light that escapes the device.

For illustration, 326 external photons from 10,000 injected electrons gives 3.26% EQE. These counts explain the ratio rather than reconstructing raw experimental data. Subtracting 3.26% EQE from 47% PLQY cannot isolate any one loss, since the two experiments use different inputs, excitation routes, and output boundaries.

The rise from 1.14% to 3.26% is 2.12 percentage points. The ratio is <Math tex={String.raw`3.26/1.14\simeq2.86`} /> times the initial value, a different statement from an increase of 2.86%. Calculating electrical energy conversion would also require voltage and photon energy.

</CandidateEquation>

## 7. Peak performance, device-to-device variation, and operating life

Repeatability cannot be judged by one maximum. The text and Supplementary Figure 22 report EQE statistics for <strong>12 independently fabricated devices</strong>. The mean is 2.77%, the standard deviation 0.36 percentage points, the minimum 2.32%, and the maximum 3.26%. The 3.26% figure is the best device. The stated standard deviation is neither a standard error of the mean nor a confidence interval. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Figure 5f gives <Math tex={String.raw`T_{50}`} />, the time during electrical operation for emission to fall to half its initial level. At a constant 100 mA cm⁻², the control reaches this point in 43 minutes and the surface-treated device in 138 minutes. At 400 mA cm⁻², the treated device’s <Math tex={String.raw`T_{50}`} /> shortens to 36 minutes. The authors connect this change to annihilation processes at high injection and to Joule heating. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Actual operating lifetime was measured. The next question is <strong>whether the demonstrated stability over minutes and hours carries over to longer service and communication drive conditions</strong>. The 48-hour optical exposure does not establish a 48-hour LED operating life, and a millisecond excited-state decay does not say how long a device can run.

## 8. What would an optical-communication source need?

The design links three functions. Chromium provides broad absorption and an energy-transfer route, the semiconductor host supports charge transport, and pBBA reduces nonradiative surface losses. The paper reports little electroluminescence from the comparison host without Cr, and a lower EQE with Cr but without passivation. The controls show that the two interventions address different problems. <a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a> <a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

Further tests should examine modulation speed, optical coupling, power, operating life at the required drive current, and variation across fabricated devices. In particular, millisecond emitter dynamics and the response of an actual high-speed communications link require separate measurements. The LED curves alone do not give a data rate or error rate for a link integrated with silicon photonics.

The lead-free composition is one feature of the material choice. Assessing a product’s safety, environmental impact, and manufacturing suitability also requires the full stack, solvents, residues, encapsulation, operating life, and disposal conditions. Those are additional engineering questions beyond the material result.

Read beyond the numbers 47 and 3.26. <strong>Where is energy absorbed, which center receives it, which loss is reduced, and how efficiently does injected charge finally produce light?</strong> Figure 1 identifies the material, Figure 2 traces energy transfer and its time scales, and Figure 5 shows the electrically driven result.
