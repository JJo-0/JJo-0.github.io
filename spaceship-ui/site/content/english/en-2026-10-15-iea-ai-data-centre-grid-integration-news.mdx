> **Institutional policy and power-system analysis.** This article reviews the IEA report published on October 14, 2026. The review covered the summary and selected sections on connections, procurement, and stability. The report is a policy survey, and this review did not examine every table and figure in its 80 pages.

## 1. Main finding

**Meeting AI data centers' electricity needs requires identifying which proposed loads will actually materialize and designing connection terms, tariffs, and fault response together. Additional generation alone cannot solve the connection problem.**

## 2. Why planning is difficult

Grids are planned years ahead, whereas a data center can be built in one to two years. Requested connection capacity also differs from actual demand. A developer can apply in several places or reserve capacity before securing customers and workloads. Overestimating demand leaves infrastructure unused. Underestimating it creates queues and bottlenecks.

The IEA surveyed more than 34 jurisdictions representing about 90% of global data center capacity. It identified 134 policies and regulations, roughly 40% announced or implemented in the preceding 12 months. This captures policy changes at a point in time. It does not measure the causal effect of those policies.

## 3. Connection terms

A firm connection generally gives a customer access to its contracted capacity without routine restrictions. A non-firm connection lets the grid operator restrict consumption under specified conditions such as congestion, faults, or particular hours. In return, the customer may connect sooner or pay less for network access. With phased access, contracted capacity grows as buildings and servers come online.

Grid headroom depends on how much power can reach a location at a particular time under given transmission and contingency limits. Two 100 MW loads can impose different costs depending on location, overlap with peak demand, and willingness to be interrupted.


Two questions clarify the difference: **When can access be limited? How much capacity is allocated at the start?** Page 45 distinguishes four arrangements.

| Arrangement | What the contract changes |
|---|---|
| Conditional curtailment | Available capacity can fall during specified events such as congestion or a fault. |
| Time-based access | Capacity is guaranteed during defined hours or for a specified share of the year. |
| Dynamic limit | The permitted load changes with observed or forecast network conditions. |
| Phased access | Contracted capacity increases as demand grows or network upgrades are completed. |

The first three are non-firm arrangements. Phasing describes the schedule of capacity increases. The contract must separately specify whether a customer can be curtailed at a given time.

## 4. Limits of existing arrangements

A first-come, first-served queue can favor an early application over a project ready to build. An annual power purchase agreement (PPA) can match annual consumption without matching the hour or location in which a data center draws power. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) alone misses cooling water, upstream water consumption in electricity generation, and the efficiency of servers and models.

Cost allocation is also difficult. A dedicated line or substation can often be traced to the load that required it, but other customers also use wider network reinforcements. Shallow connection charges spread more of these shared costs across network users. Deep charges allocate more to the new load. Deciding what is fair requires a counterfactual and a policy judgment.

## 5. Three steps for planning connections

First, disclose location, size, commissioning date, expansion stages, and expected load profile. Deposits, customer contracts, and milestones can help distinguish mature projects. Second, offer connection terms that fit the need, including conditional curtailment, time-based rights, dynamic limits, or phased access. Third, use minimum charges, long-term contracts, exit fees, and technical performance requirements to allocate upgrade costs and stability risks.

## 6. System quantities to track

The report traces a chain of decisions and measurements rather than proposing a single equation.

`Requested load (MW) → project credibility and maturity → local grid headroom (MW) → firm or non-firm access → hours curtailed and actual use (MWh) → upgrade costs and stability outcomes`

Non-firm access can use otherwise idle grid headroom, but curtailment can conflict with uptime commitments. The IEA estimates 750–900 GW of potential additional hosting capacity worldwide. This is the grid's potential ability to accommodate queued projects. The Grids section of *Electricity 2026*, cited on page 45, covers generation, storage, and large loads. The estimate does not measure completed generation or capacity reserved for data centers alone. This review did not reproduce the calculation from raw data.

## 7. Reported figures

Data center operators signed PPAs covering 45 TWh in the first half of 2026, about 20% more than in the same period a year earlier. Around 70% was in the United States. The report values a year's lease of 1 GW of AI-optimized data center capacity at $20–30 billion, about 100 times its comparison cost for a battery of equivalent power capacity. Pausing computation can therefore be an expensive form of demand response.

During a July 2026 transmission fault in Northern Virginia, 3.8 GW of data center load transferred to on-site backup power. To give a sense of scale, the report cites the eastern grid's 3.875 GW resource-loss benchmark. These values describe neither the size of a blackout nor an observed generator loss. Abruptly losing a large load can also disturb frequency and voltage.

## 8. Evidence that qualifies the case

Non-firm connections are not necessarily cheap. Investment risk and disputes can grow if contracts leave curtailment priority, notice, maximum duration, or the route to firm access unclear. Shifting or delaying computation depends on customer contracts and software architecture. The report observes that active management of compute loads remains uncommon.

Queue size does not measure what will be built. Letters of intent covering 45 GW of small modular reactors are also not committed capacity, and commercial operation is not expected before the 2030s. Distinguish announcements and contracts from physical power flows.

### On-site generation proposals and operating capacity

Page 60 puts the global pipeline of on-site gas generation for data centers at about 210 GW. Roughly 190 GW was proposed and 17 GW under construction. The stage totals are rounded. Subtracting them from 210 GW cannot establish operating capacity. The report also notes turbine supply delays and uncertainty over which plans will proceed.

A site can operate off-grid, combine local generation with grid supply, or begin in island mode and connect later. Future interconnection still entails connection and operating requirements. Page 61 notes that meeting a high reliability target can require more generation capacity than the site's continuous load. Rated capacity does not guarantee availability at every hour.

**Reader check.** Does announcing on-site generation resolve fuel, equipment, and reliability risks? It changes the bottleneck. Project delivery, equipment supply, and operating conditions still require evidence.

## 9. Limits of the evidence

This is a comparative policy survey and IEA analysis. Queue definitions and large-load thresholds differ across jurisdictions. The review did not locate the calculation code and raw inputs for the 750–900 GW estimate or verify that the full 134-policy dataset could be reproduced. External reviewers included people from Microsoft, AWS, grid operators, and equipment companies. The report attributes its final views to the IEA Secretariat.

## 10. What is changing

Data centers increasingly face obligations covering connection conditions, backup power, flexibility, and grid stability. This shifts the policy question from how many gigawatts developers request to when the load will materialize, who bears the cost and risk, and how the facility will respond to faults.

## 11. What would change the assessment?

Revisit the analysis when actual curtailment hours and uptime impacts become available, when the 750–900 GW calculation is released, or when the policy dataset is updated. Implementation of large-load ride-through standards and evidence on household rate impacts also matter.

## Checking the interpretation

Is connecting 100 MW the same as supplying 100 MWh? MW measures power at an instant. MWh measures energy over time. A constant 100 MW for two hours uses 200 MWh. Real contracts can limit access by time and location, so connection capacity alone cannot determine annual consumption or bill savings.

Does switching a data center to backup power always ease grid operation? A large load disappearing abruptly changes the balance between supply and demand. The 3.8 GW example illustrates a stability issue. It does not measure outage damage.

**Reader check.** Does phased access automatically require curtailment during congestion? Check the expansion schedule and curtailment clauses separately.
