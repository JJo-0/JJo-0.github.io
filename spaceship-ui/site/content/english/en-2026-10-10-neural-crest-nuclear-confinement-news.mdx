## In one sentence

The researchers investigated why substantial nuclear deformation during embryonic cell migration through confined tissue did not coincide with increased DNA damage in their observations, examining LaminB2 regulation and damage responses.

## Why does the distinction matter?

Does a deformed nucleus necessarily indicate nuclear envelope rupture or DNA damage? The abstract compares tissue confinement and nuclear deformation across embryonic locations and describes genetic and mechanical perturbation of surrounding tissue to reduce confinement. The authors also report functional experiments examining LaminB2. The candidate review distinguishes observing migration from identifying the conditions under which cells adapt to deformation.

## Evidence and comparison conditions

The abstract reports nucleocytoplasmic leakage without detectable nuclear envelope rupture or increased DNA damage. Leakage was observed as a change in the distribution of a fluorescent marker expected to remain inside the nucleus. That observation alone cannot establish a tear in the envelope.

The nuclear lamina is a protein scaffold surrounding the nucleus. LaminB2 is one of its constituents. The accepted manuscript compares reduced and sustained LaminB2 expression. Reducing it produced no significant difference in mean nuclear circularity, but deformations lasted slightly less time. With sustained expression, nuclei deformed more and remained deformed for longer. These results do not support a simple assumption that more LaminB2 is better.

In Figure 6, distinguish circularity in D and H from duration in F and J. Circularity describes roundness. Duration describes how long the deformed state persists. H compares 30 negative cells from eight embryos with 37 positive cells from seven embryos. Cell counts differ from the number of independent embryos or patients. The Mann–Whitney test yielded p&lt;0.0001 for this comparison. The p value gives neither the size of the effect nor evidence for efficacy in humans.

## Why use different markers for leakage and rupture?

A modest decrease in nuclear fluorescence has more than one possible explanation. A gap might have opened in the envelope, or transport between nucleus and cytoplasm might have changed. The researchers therefore used more than a single nuclear fluorescence marker to assess rupture.

The accepted Results describe additional checks using BAF and cGAS. These protein markers can change localization when envelope damage exposes DNA. First, the team verified that the markers responded in a damage-induced positive control. They then looked for corresponding local signals in migrating cells undergoing large deformations. Those observations yielded no signal supporting rupture. Electron microscopy provided additional observations.

A positive control changes the interpretation of an absent signal: the assay demonstrated an ability to respond before it was applied to the phenomenon of interest. The controls did not establish detection at every time, scale or condition. Changes to nuclear pores remain a possible interpretation of the observations, rather than a complete account of causation.

**Check.** Can the absence of a rupture marker alone establish assay performance? Look for a responsive control and check where and when the observation was made. The relevant evidence appears in the accepted manuscript's Results on nucleocytoplasmic leakage, Figure 4 and Extended Data Figure 4.

## Could the surrounding tissue simply have been soft?

DNA damage was tested separately from envelope rupture. The team used a fluorescent 53BP1 marker that can concentrate at double-strand breaks, confirming punctate signals in damage-induced embryos. Many live embryonic cells had no trackable puncta, so the researchers also analyzed the brightest signal divided by mean signal. This ratio measures spatial concentration of fluorescence. It does not count all DNA breaks.

The in vivo analysis examined signals for up to one hour on either side of maximum nuclear deformation and responses for up to 60 minutes afterward. This window extends beyond the brief moment of deformation but leaves later responses and lifelong genome stability untested. The stiff-pillar culture described below was a separate comparison using the same indicator. [Accepted manuscript, lines 323–360, Figure 5D–F. Culture comparison, lines 370–386, Figure 5J–L.]

To test whether relatively soft embryonic tissue explained the observations, the accepted manuscript describes atomic force microscopy of trunk tissue in living embryos and a separate culture experiment in stiff PDMS pillars. PDMS is a silicone-based polymer used for microstructures. The gap between pillars was 3 µm. Cells in confinement were compared with a flat, two-dimensional culture condition.

Nuclei deformed more in the confined culture. The authors report no increase in normalized maximum 53BP1 intensity and no simple correlation within 60 minutes after peak deformation. Testing a different mechanical environment addressed an alternative explanation. It cannot establish absence of DNA damage in all materials, loads or cell types.

Material stiffness and the force on a particular nucleus are different quantities. A material stiffness measure alone cannot specify nuclear compression force or deformation energy. Pillar geometry, contact, movement and time also matter. This section compares the accepted Results subsection “Stiff in vitro confinement” with the description of Figure 5G–L. It supplies no independent force measurement or replication.

### Reading the shape, damage and time axes of Figure 5

G diagrams the pillar culture and H shows the nuclei. The vertical axis in I is mean nuclear circularity, and in J it is normalized maximum 53BP1 intensity. Increased deformation and a change in a damage marker are separate observations. The comparison used 22 cells in 2D culture and 25 between pillars, across three independent experiments. The 47 cells do not constitute 47 independent experiments.

The nonsignificant result in J indicates that this analysis did not detect a difference. It is not an equivalence test ruling out every possible difference. K aligns time to peak deformation at minute zero and shows circularity and marker intensity on separate vertical axes. Compare their time courses without comparing line heights across different scales and normalized quantities. [Accepted manuscript, Figure 5, PDF pages 25–26.]

**Check.** Does a stable damage marker in a stiffer environment guarantee the same observation indefinitely? The result describes particular cells, confinement geometry, marker and time window. It does not rule out an effect under other conditions.

## How was deformation duration counted?

A nucleus is three-dimensional, but the shape measurement begins with two-dimensional maximum-intensity projections of z-images from the nuclear channel. Projection combines depth information in one plane. StarDist then segmented nuclei and the same nuclei were tracked over time. The resulting circularity measures neither total three-dimensional volume nor mechanical strength of the envelope.

The Methods use 0.81, the mean circularity across migrating cells from all regions, as a reference. Consecutive time points below it define a deformation event whose duration can be measured. This is an event-classification rule for the imaging dataset. It defines no biological threshold for DNA damage, which was assessed separately.

Mean duration per nucleus was calculated for nuclei with at least two deformation events. Analyses aligned to peak deformation excluded cells whose minimum circularity occurred at the first or last tracking time point. This distinction matters when the observation ends before recovery can be seen or begins after onset. That explanation interprets the analytical scope rather than identifying a separately tested result about the exclusion rule.

**Compare like with like.** Can event counts from briefly and extensively tracked nuclei be compared directly? Track lengths varied because of division, departure from the field and occasional cell death. Daughter cells received separate tracks. Time points, cells and embryos are different denominators.

## Limitations and counterevidence

Embryonic observations cannot establish how adult human cells or tumors respond. Changes in expression of DNA repair pathways alone cannot show that any particular pathway is sufficient for protection. Reducing LaminB2 also produced developmental changes involving the head, eyes and body axis, complicating attempts to isolate a particular nuclear recovery effect. The accepted Methods state that data collection and analysis were not blinded to condition.

The sustained-expression experiment reported several damage readouts. Mean and distribution of γH2AX and live-cell 53BP1 signals describe different observations. Some signals increased slightly, but the accepted manuscript found no simple correlation between degree of deformation and damage signal. Neither complete prevention of DNA damage by LaminB2 nor inevitable damage from nuclear distortion follows from the study.
