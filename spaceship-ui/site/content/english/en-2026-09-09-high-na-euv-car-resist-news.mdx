## Building a chip is like building a tiny city in layers

A city needs roads and connections as well as buildings. On a chip, the devices that control electricity, the wires that connect them, and the connections between layers must all occupy precise positions.

These features are too small to draw or cut individually. Instead, manufacturers use <strong>lithography to transfer circuit patterns with light</strong>. They coat a surface with a light-sensitive material, expose the circuit pattern, and use development and etching to leave the desired structure. [ASML’s guide to chip fabrication](https://www.asml.com/en/company/stories/2021/semiconductor-manufacturing-process-steps)

Parts of the process resemble photographic printing. The finished product is a three-dimensional structure through which electricity can flow.

### Three terms for following the process

A <strong>mask</strong> holds the original circuit pattern. A <strong>photoresist</strong> responds to light so that the pattern can be developed. <strong>Etching</strong> selectively removes material according to that pattern.

Even an excellent exposure tool cannot rescue a pattern that blurs in the resist or collapses during etching. Picture a high-end printer working with ink that bleeds and paper that tears.

<NewsFigure media="car-pattern" priority lang="en" />

### Drawing a pattern with light and transferring it into a material

Exposed resist is an intermediate layer. Development leaves different regions of the resist depending on how they reacted to light. Etching transfers that pattern into the material below. Metal filling and subsequent layers may still be needed before anyone can measure an electrical connection. A process micrograph needs a label identifying the stage at which it was taken.

A claim about a “22 nm” structure is easy to misread without that information. A line in resist, the etched feature, and the final metal wire can differ in width and shape as material is removed or reacts. Controlling the change between those stages is part of process engineering. [Manufacturing steps](https://www.asml.com/en/company/stories/2021/semiconductor-manufacturing-process-steps)

## What changes with High-NA EUV?

**EUV** stands for extreme ultraviolet light, which has a very short wavelength. The optical system and the wavelength together determine which fine features it can resolve. <strong>Numerical aperture (NA)</strong> describes the range of angles from which the optics can collect light. All else being equal, a higher NA helps separate finer features. [How resolution works](https://www.asml.com/en/technology/lithography-principles/rayleigh-criterion)

High-NA EUV raises the numerical aperture from 0.33 to 0.55. The wavelength stays in the same EUV band. It is the optics that gain resolving power. [Why introduce High-NA EUV?](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/case-high-na-euv-unlocking-next-era-chip-manufacturing)

That raises a practical question. **If the tool can project a finer image, can the material record it faithfully?**

### Focus and resist response at smaller dimensions

Even if the optics resolve finer detail, the wafer surface still has to remain in focus. Wafer height and position, mask geometry, resist thickness, and material response all matter. A larger numerical aperture alone does not widen every tolerance in the process.

Light interacting with material in a tiny volume also introduces statistical variation. With enough exposure on average, a rare break or bridge can still occur locally. Achieving the target mean linewidth and printing every location correctly are different measurements.

For that reason, resolution, exposure dose, line-edge roughness, and defects belong in the same assessment. Improving one by shifting the operating conditions too far may reduce throughput or complicate the next stage. imec emphasizes joint optimization of exposure and etching along with the resist. [imec announcement](https://www.imec-int.com/en/press/imec-demonstrates-extension-chemically-amplified-resists-high-na-euv-lithography)

## How far can an established family of resists go?

imec reported that by optimizing the process with material suppliers, it could produce lines at 22 nm pitch and vias with 28 nm center-to-center spacing in a single High-NA EUV exposure using a <strong>chemically amplified resist (CAR)</strong>. A via is an opening that provides a connection between layers.

“An established resist family” and “a single exposure” are important qualifications. The work tested how far a familiar class of material could extend on new equipment. imec linked the result to the possibility of retaining CAR for some layers at the A14 and A10 logic nodes. [Official September 8 announcement](https://www.imec-int.com/en/press/imec-demonstrates-extension-chemically-amplified-resists-high-na-euv-lithography)

### What does 22 nm measure?

<strong>Pitch</strong> is the spacing of a repeated pattern. For a regular line pattern, it can be measured from the center of one line to the center of the next.

Imagine lines 11 nm wide alternating with spaces 11 nm wide. The pitch is 22 nm. This example defines the term. It does not establish that every feature in the announcement was exactly 11 nm wide.

**A 22 nm pitch, a transistor’s gate length, and a process-node name describe different things.** Only the first is the repeat spacing measured here.

### Logic layouts ask more of a process than parallel lines

Repeated lines are useful for comparing basic process performance. Logic layouts also contain turns, branches, line ends, and connection holes in different surroundings. A process must hold the intended shape as orientation and neighboring features change. One regularly spaced grating covers only a part of that design space.

imec showed 22 nm-pitch serpentine and fork structures, SRAM and place-and-route patterns, and random logic vias. It also reported tip-to-tip spacing (T2T) between line ends and center-to-center spacing (C2C) between holes. All are lengths, but combining them into a single ranking of “smallest dimensions” would obscure what was measured. [Results and captions](https://www.imec-int.com/en/press/imec-demonstrates-extension-chemically-amplified-resists-high-na-euv-lithography)

### ADI and AEI identify when the image was taken

After-development inspection (ADI) shows the developed pattern. After-etch inspection (AEI) shows what happened when it was transferred to the material below. Two small abbreviations in the captions identify the level of evidence each image supplies.

The announcement discusses earlier metallized electrical test structures at 28 nm pitch alongside the new 22 nm-pitch patterns. Testing continuity along long electrical structures can reveal the consequences of rare defects. Those prior electrical results cannot automatically be assigned to every smaller pattern in the newer images. [Scope of the earlier and newer results](https://www.imec-int.com/en/press/imec-demonstrates-extension-chemically-amplified-resists-high-na-euv-lithography)

## Why does continuing to use a resist family matter?

For a fab, changing materials involves more than changing a name on an inventory sheet. Engineers have to identify a stable operating window, check compatibility with the rest of the process, and establish how to inspect and control it.

A familiar material family on new equipment gives manufacturers another route through the transition. Formulations may still need to change, and transition costs remain. The announcement emphasizes joint optimization of the material, exposure, and etching.

Printing more of a pattern in one exposure could reduce the work of splitting it across exposures and aligning the pieces. A simpler flow and a measured cost reduction are different claims. **Fab data would be needed to quantify the savings.**

The image above is from imec’s announcement. The dark lines on the gray background are the features to inspect. Do they run continuously, and do adjacent lines stay separate?

### Familiar materials still need qualification under new conditions

An established resist family brings experience in manufacturing and inspection. Different optics and smaller dimensions can change its behavior, much as a familiar paper responds differently to another ink or printing speed.

imec identifies opportunities to retain chemically amplified resists (CARs) for some critical logic layers. Another resist or process may remain preferable at a different layer or pitch. The result expands the available choices.

Single patterning can avoid dividing a specific pattern into multiple exposures. A complete chip still requires many layers and process steps. The relevant question is which pattern on which layer can be produced in one exposure.

## What does a good micrograph tell us about a chip?

A clear resist pattern is one necessary piece of evidence. The shape just after development, the shape after etching, and the electrical behavior after metal fill belong to successive stages.

Imagine two fabs spending the same amount to make 100 chips. If one gets 90 working chips and the other gets 50, their costs per usable chip differ. Those numbers are illustrative. <strong>Yield</strong> is the fraction of manufactured products that work as intended, and it matters if shrinking features is to pay off.

imec had previously shown smaller pitches with a different resist family. The question here is **how far CAR can be extended**, rather than which experiment holds the record for smallest High-NA EUV pattern. [Earlier High-NA results and context](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/case-high-na-euv-unlocking-next-era-chip-manufacturing)

### Rare defects matter when the patterned area grows

A line may look intact in a small image yet break or bridge to another line when repeated over a much larger area. A single such event can disable a circuit. Defect density and electrical pass rate therefore complement representative micrographs.

A best-case image also says little about tolerance to changes in dose and focus. If a small departure causes failure, maintaining quality throughout a fab becomes difficult. A useful follow-up would show the range over which the same quality can be repeated.

When reading a micrograph, record its scale, process stage, pattern type, and inspected area. Two grayscale images can represent very different tests. Clock frequency, power use, and manufacturing cost require measurements beyond the image.

## What should the next report show?

Look for the same pattern quality over a larger area, counts of defects and electrical failures, and measured changes in the time and cost of the full process.

The news is **progress in matching materials and processes to an advanced exposure tool for manufacturing**. It marks a step on the way to a chip release. Knowing which step makes the pitch figure much easier to interpret.

Conference data could show pattern variation across conditions, dose and focus latitude, electrical continuity after etching, and defect counts. Later evidence would include adoption in a customer product layer and whether productivity gains survive the other costs. The public announcement and official background material support the account here. Unreleased conference tables supply no numbers for this comparison.

An improvement at one stage can matter to the industry. Identifying where it occurred, from making a pattern in resist to transferring it and testing the electrical path, makes its value clearer.

### What does a process window measure?

A process window describes the range of operating conditions that repeatedly produces an acceptable structure. At fixed dose, vary focus. At fixed focus, vary dose. A single good micrograph leaves open whether a fab could reproduce it despite small routine variations in both.

Exposure dose relates to the light energy delivered to the resist. Higher dose may stabilize a signal but can cost tool time. Lower dose raises the demands on materials and process control. The announcement does not report every dose-dependent defect distribution needed to identify the economically best material for a particular condition.

Mean linewidth and local edge variation also differ. A line can have the right average width yet narrow dangerously or break at one location. A pattern image and an electrical continuity test over a long conductor offer complementary evidence.

Keep ADI and AEI labels attached to their numbers. Transfer into the next material may change the line width and corners. Omitting that distinction makes it difficult to tell whether a gain came from exposure, resist response, or pattern transfer.

### What extending an established process can achieve

Extending a resist family could preserve manufacturing knowledge and established equipment. Retaining the family still allows changes in its formulation and operating conditions. Transition cost and the finest demonstrable feature both matter.

For the circuit layer in question, how much latitude remains after accounting for defects, throughput, the etched feature, and inspection? That question distinguishes a manufacturing opportunity from a single record pitch.
