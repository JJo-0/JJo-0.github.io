## Building a chip is like building a tiny city in layers

A city needs roads and connections as well as buildings. On a chip, the devices that control electricity, the wires that connect them, and the connections between layers must all occupy precise positions.

These features are too small to draw or cut individually. Instead, manufacturers use <strong>lithography to transfer circuit patterns with light</strong>. They coat a surface with a light-sensitive material, expose the circuit pattern, and use development and etching to leave the desired structure. [ASML’s guide to chip fabrication](https://www.asml.com/en/company/stories/2021/semiconductor-manufacturing-process-steps)

Parts of the process resemble photographic printing. The finished product is a three-dimensional structure through which electricity can flow.

### Three terms for following the process

A <strong>mask</strong> holds the original circuit pattern. A <strong>photoresist</strong> responds to light so that the pattern can be developed. <strong>Etching</strong> selectively removes material according to that pattern.

Even an excellent exposure tool cannot rescue a pattern that blurs in the resist or collapses during etching. Picture a high-end printer working with ink that bleeds and paper that tears.

## What changes with High-NA EUV?

**EUV** stands for extreme ultraviolet light, which has a very short wavelength. The optical system and the wavelength together determine which fine features it can resolve. <strong>Numerical aperture (NA)</strong> describes the range of angles from which the optics can collect light. All else being equal, a higher NA helps separate finer features. [How resolution works](https://www.asml.com/en/technology/lithography-principles/rayleigh-criterion)

High-NA EUV raises the numerical aperture from 0.33 to 0.55. The wavelength stays in the same EUV band. It is the optics that gain resolving power. [Why introduce High-NA EUV?](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/case-high-na-euv-unlocking-next-era-chip-manufacturing)

That raises a practical question. **If the tool can project a finer image, can the material record it faithfully?**

## How far can an established family of resists go?

imec reported that by optimizing the process with material suppliers, it could produce lines at 22 nm pitch and vias with 28 nm center-to-center spacing in a single High-NA EUV exposure using a <strong>chemically amplified resist (CAR)</strong>. A via is an opening that provides a connection between layers.

“An established resist family” and “a single exposure” are important qualifications. The work tested how far a familiar class of material could extend on new equipment. imec linked the result to the possibility of retaining CAR for some layers at the A14 and A10 logic nodes. [Official September 8 announcement](https://www.imec-int.com/en/press/imec-demonstrates-extension-chemically-amplified-resists-high-na-euv-lithography)

### What does 22 nm measure?

<strong>Pitch</strong> is the spacing of a repeated pattern. For a regular line pattern, it can be measured from the center of one line to the center of the next.

Imagine lines 11 nm wide alternating with spaces 11 nm wide. The pitch is 22 nm. This example defines the term. It does not establish that every feature in the announcement was exactly 11 nm wide.

**A 22 nm pitch, a transistor’s gate length, and a process-node name describe different things.** Only the first is the repeat spacing measured here.

## Why does continuing to use a resist family matter?

For a fab, changing materials involves more than changing a name on an inventory sheet. Engineers have to identify a stable operating window, check compatibility with the rest of the process, and establish how to inspect and control it.

A familiar material family on new equipment gives manufacturers another route through the transition. Formulations may still need to change, and transition costs remain. The announcement emphasizes joint optimization of the material, exposure, and etching.

Printing more of a pattern in one exposure could reduce the work of splitting it across exposures and aligning the pieces. A simpler flow and a measured cost reduction are different claims. **Fab data would be needed to quantify the savings.**

The image above is from imec’s announcement. The dark lines on the gray background are the features to inspect. Do they run continuously, and do adjacent lines stay separate?

## What does a good micrograph tell us about a chip?

A clear resist pattern is one necessary piece of evidence. The shape just after development, the shape after etching, and the electrical behavior after metal fill belong to successive stages.

Imagine two fabs spending the same amount to make 100 chips. If one gets 90 working chips and the other gets 50, their costs per usable chip differ. Those numbers are illustrative. <strong>Yield</strong> is the fraction of manufactured products that work as intended, and it matters if shrinking features is to pay off.

imec had previously shown smaller pitches with a different resist family. The question here is **how far CAR can be extended**, rather than which experiment holds the record for smallest High-NA EUV pattern. [Earlier High-NA results and context](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/case-high-na-euv-unlocking-next-era-chip-manufacturing)

## What should the next report show?

Look for the same pattern quality over a larger area, counts of defects and electrical failures, and measured changes in the time and cost of the full process.

The news is **progress in matching materials and processes to an advanced exposure tool for manufacturing**. It marks a step on the way to a chip release. Knowing which step makes the pitch figure much easier to interpret.
