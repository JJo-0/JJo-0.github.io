## What gives a computer a memory?

A photograph or a sentence is represented by many zeros and ones. The labels matter less than the underlying task: making **two physical states that can be distinguished and read again**.

Magnetoresistive random-access memory, or **MRAM**, uses the orientation of small magnets. One magnetic layer maintains a reference direction, while the other can switch. The electrical resistance differs when their magnetizations point in parallel or opposite directions, giving the read circuit a way to distinguish the stored states. [The structure of magnetic memory](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/novel-sot-mram-architecture-opens-doors-high-density-last-level-cache-memory-applications)

Think of a sign with a movable arrow. Assign zero to an arrow pointing right and one to an arrow pointing left. The arrow illustrates the convention. On the chip the two states are orientations of magnetization.

### The stored state and the device that writes it

Separate the storage material, write mechanism, and readout. A notebook may be perfectly usable while a blunt pencil makes writing difficult or poor light makes it hard to read. Encoding information in the direction of a magnet requires a way to switch the chosen cell and a way to detect its state. The contribution here concerns the source of the write signal.

Magnetization describes a physical state with a direction. There is no literal needle rotating inside the cell. Producing two distinct states establishes one property. Reliable writing, stable retention over time, and distinguishable readout each require a different test. The shortest pulse width alone cannot establish all three.

## The harder part is switching quickly with little energy

If the magnetic orientation is hard to change, a written bit stays put. If it changes too easily, the bit may flip unintentionally. Yet a deliberate write should switch it quickly and with little energy.

The same memory has to retain information reliably and remain easy to rewrite on demand.

<strong>Spin–orbit torque (SOT)</strong> uses a current through a layer adjacent to a magnet to change its magnetization. It can separate the write-current path from the read path. Practical devices also have to meet area, write-current, and manufacturing requirements. [How SOT works](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/imec-demonstrates-manufacturability-of-state-of-the-art-spin-orbit-torque-mram-devices-on-300mm-si-wafers) · [Challenges at system level](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/novel-sot-mram-architecture-opens-doors-high-density-last-level-cache-memory-applications)

A fast magnetic material alone is insufficient. **The chip also has to generate the electrical signal that switches the magnet as intended.**

### The barrier that preserves a bit also resists writing

A state that is hard to escape can better resist thermal fluctuations. Deliberately switching it then requires a drive strong enough to overcome that stability. A firmly latched door resists the wind and also takes more effort to open.

Spin–orbit torque allows a current in an adjacent material to influence the magnetization. Torque here describes a change in direction at the material level. Switching depends on the layer combination and thickness, the initial stability, and the direction and waveform of the current. Driving more current does not guarantee a better write.

Replacing a laboratory laser with a compact electrical generator could make integration with a circuit more practical. The generator still needs a power supply, a driver, and interconnects. Miniaturizing the source is one step toward an integrated system.

## What the researchers changed was the pulse source

Researchers at the National University of Singapore built an on-chip <strong>nanoplasma pulse generator</strong> and applied its short electrical signals to a Co/Pt/Co magnetic trilayer. Some earlier ultrafast experiments required bulky lasers. This experiment brought the electrical pulse source onto the chip.

The name describes the generator. It produces a very short electrical pulse and sends it to the magnetic structure. The magnet itself stays magnetic.

The paper reports pulses as short as **6.4 picoseconds (ps)** and magnetization switching without an external magnetic field. Across the tested pulse widths, reducing the duration from 100 microseconds to 6.4 picoseconds reduced write energy by about four orders of magnitude. The authors also attribute part of the efficiency gain to ultrafast Joule heating. [Original paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

A picosecond is one trillionth of a second. Thus 6.4 ps is 0.0000000000064 seconds. The first question to ask about that number is **which interval it measures**.

### Where 6.4 picoseconds fits in a memory operation

The pulse width is the interval over which the electrical signal is applied. A complete memory operation also involves selecting an address, transmitting the signal, changing the state, and reading the result.

A brief press of a doorbell tells you little about the end-to-end time for a parcel delivery. For the same reason, taking the reciprocal of the pulse width does not give the operating frequency of a commercial memory.

“Without an external magnetic field” refers to removing the need for a separately applied assist field. The device still relies on magnetism.

### What waveform reaches the magnetic device?

The generator output and the pulse delivered to the device may differ. Resistance, capacitance, impedance mismatch, reflections, and losses in the connection can reshape a pulse. At very short timescales, the measurement apparatus and transmission path become consequential. Measure the generator and the switching event separately before relating the two.

The public abstract reports pulses as short as 6.4 ps, amplitudes above 100 V, and a slew rate of 21 V/ps. Width is a duration, amplitude is a voltage, and slew rate is the change in voltage per unit time. None of these by itself specifies operations per second, bits written, or the voltage every cell would receive in a product. [Published abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

A pulse source also needs repeatability. Generating one short pulse and delivering similar pulses at the required interval are separate capabilities. How much do writing outcomes vary when width or amplitude changes? The reported measurements do not establish an arbitrary tolerance or repetition limit.

### How was a change in magnetization observed?

The figure descriptions include magneto-optical Kerr effect (MOKE) images. These images show regions whose magnetic state changed. A digital read circuit that reliably discriminates bits would be another experiment. Interpret the observation in terms of the instrument actually used. [Figure descriptions](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

A final image also leaves questions about how the material switched. A region can move collectively, or domains and their boundaries may evolve. Time-resolved observations and final-state measurements provide different evidence. Two switching paths can end in the same state while dissipating different amounts of energy.

## Does a 10,000-fold drop in write energy imply the same drop in data-center power?

No. The four-order-of-magnitude difference is between **write energies under the conditions compared in the experiment**. Neither the best commercial MRAM nor a complete data center served as that baseline.

The energy of an electrical pulse depends on voltage, current, and duration together. A very short pulse can still require substantial power, reducing its energy advantage. A product-level total would also include the pulse generator and the peripheral circuits.

When reading the 10,000-fold figure, ask **which conditions were compared, which components were counted, and whether the difference survives in a complete memory circuit**.

### Shorter pulses and lower energy have different ratios

Electrical energy accumulates voltage times current over time. At constant power, operating for 0.1 seconds uses one-tenth as much energy as operating for one second. If the shorter operation needs 20 times the power, it uses twice the energy instead. This example explains why voltage, current, and duration matter together. The numbers are illustrative.

The pulse-width ratio between 100 μs and 6.4 ps and the four-order-of-magnitude reduction in write energy describe different comparisons. Current and switching conditions can change as well. Read the paper's reference condition before transferring an energy ratio to a comparison with a commercial device.

The accounting boundary also matters. Energy delivered to the magnetic device, energy consumed in the generator, charging and recovery, cell selection, and reading all contribute different terms. A large device-level gain can become smaller once those other costs enter an integrated test.

### Heating can participate in switching

The authors attribute part of the improvement in the picosecond regime to ultrafast Joule heating. The amount of heat, how rapidly it appears, where it is deposited, and how quickly it dissipates can influence magnetization. Picosecond pulses can still generate heat. [Abstract and Figure 5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

If heating helps a selected cell switch, an array must also protect neighboring cells. Does heat build up at the actual cell spacing and write repetition rate? Do the write conditions remain reliable over the operating temperature range? Those questions arise when moving from a single-device observation to a memory array.

## What remains before a memory product?

Demonstrating switching in one structure is one step toward a product containing many cells. Does the same signal reach cells in different positions accurately? How often does a write fail after repeated use? How much energy do the peripheral circuits add?

These are **the next questions for a product-level evaluation**. The public abstract, figure, and data-availability information did not establish lifetime or error rates for a complete memory array. Data and code are available from the authors on request. [Public materials and data availability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

Electrical picosecond magnetization switching had already been reported in April 2026. The distinctive contribution here is **a concrete way to place the ultrafast pulse source close to the device**. [Earlier related study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72582-7)

### A successful write and a low error rate require different evidence

A hypothetical device that works nine times out of ten can support an exploratory experiment. In an array written repeatedly, even a small per-write failure probability can matter. The target depends on the application and the error correction available. Compare reliability using a stated number of trials and a stated definition of failure.

Conditions optimized on one device may not transfer to all devices. Junction dimensions, layer structure, wiring, and temperature can shift the pulse required for switching. An effective operating point on a representative device does not establish array yield.

This account draws on the public abstract, figure descriptions, and available supplementary material. An independent reanalysis of the complete subscription article and raw waveforms was outside its scope. The authors make data and code available by request. The publisher's September 10 correction concerns a funding identifier and leaves the performance findings intact. [Data availability and correction history](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

## What makes the result worth following?

Shrinking an experimental setup asks whether conditions that were once available only in a laboratory can become part of a working circuit.

The next milestone is sustaining fast, reliable writes with multiple cells and their peripheral circuits connected. **Usable high-speed memory will be judged by the whole operation**, beyond its shortest pulse.

### Why a longer pulse could still be useful

Future reports could test addressed writes across many cells, repeated-write failure rates, energy from the power supply through readout, manufacturing variation, and thermal effects. A pulse slightly longer than 6.4 ps could be more useful to a system if it is easier to drive reliably.

For any ultrafast-device result, ask what interval was measured, which reference condition was used, and which parts of the apparatus the measurement includes. The practical advance often comes from meeting several constraints together.

Finally, ask whether the written state remains stable after the fast switching event. A thermal condition that helps during writing may have different consequences during retention. An implementable memory combines fast switching, stable storage, and dependable readout in one device.
