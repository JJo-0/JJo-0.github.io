## What gives a computer a memory?

A photograph or a sentence is represented by many zeros and ones. The labels matter less than the underlying task: making **two physical states that can be distinguished and read again**.

Magnetoresistive random-access memory, or **MRAM**, uses the orientation of small magnets. One magnetic layer maintains a reference direction, while the other can switch. The electrical resistance differs when their magnetizations point in parallel or opposite directions, giving the read circuit a way to distinguish the stored states. [The structure of magnetic memory](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/novel-sot-mram-architecture-opens-doors-high-density-last-level-cache-memory-applications)

Think of a sign with a movable arrow. Assign zero to an arrow pointing right and one to an arrow pointing left. The arrow illustrates the convention. On the chip the two states are orientations of magnetization.

## The harder part is switching quickly with little energy

If the magnetic orientation is hard to change, a written bit stays put. If it changes too easily, the bit may flip unintentionally. Yet a deliberate write should switch it quickly and with little energy.

The same memory has to retain information reliably and remain easy to rewrite on demand.

<strong>Spin–orbit torque (SOT)</strong> uses a current through a layer adjacent to a magnet to change its magnetization. It can separate the write-current path from the read path. Practical devices also have to meet area, write-current, and manufacturing requirements. [How SOT works](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/imec-demonstrates-manufacturability-of-state-of-the-art-spin-orbit-torque-mram-devices-on-300mm-si-wafers) · [Challenges at system level](https://www.imec-int.com/en/articles/novel-sot-mram-architecture-opens-doors-high-density-last-level-cache-memory-applications)

A fast magnetic material alone is insufficient. **The chip also has to generate the electrical signal that switches the magnet as intended.**

## What the researchers changed was the pulse source

Researchers at the National University of Singapore built an on-chip <strong>nanoplasma pulse generator</strong> and applied its short electrical signals to a Co/Pt/Co magnetic trilayer. Some earlier ultrafast experiments required bulky lasers. This experiment brought the electrical pulse source onto the chip.

The name describes the generator. It produces a very short electrical pulse and sends it to the magnetic structure. The magnet itself stays magnetic.

The paper reports pulses as short as **6.4 picoseconds (ps)** and magnetization switching without an external magnetic field. Across the tested pulse widths, reducing the duration from 100 microseconds to 6.4 picoseconds reduced write energy by about four orders of magnitude. The authors also attribute part of the efficiency gain to ultrafast Joule heating. [Original paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

A picosecond is one trillionth of a second. Thus 6.4 ps is 0.0000000000064 seconds. The first question to ask about that number is **which interval it measures**.

### Where 6.4 picoseconds fits in a memory operation

The pulse width is the interval over which the electrical signal is applied. A complete memory operation also involves selecting an address, transmitting the signal, changing the state, and reading the result.

A brief press of a doorbell tells you little about the end-to-end time for a parcel delivery. For the same reason, taking the reciprocal of the pulse width does not give the operating frequency of a commercial memory.

“Without an external magnetic field” refers to removing the need for a separately applied assist field. The device still relies on magnetism.

## Does a 10,000-fold drop in write energy imply the same drop in data-center power?

No. The four-order-of-magnitude difference is between **write energies under the conditions compared in the experiment**. Neither the best commercial MRAM nor a complete data center served as that baseline.

The energy of an electrical pulse depends on voltage, current, and duration together. A very short pulse can still require substantial power, reducing its energy advantage. A product-level total would also include the pulse generator and the peripheral circuits.

When reading the 10,000-fold figure, ask **which conditions were compared, which components were counted, and whether the difference survives in a complete memory circuit**.

## What remains before a memory product?

Demonstrating switching in one structure is one step toward a product containing many cells. Does the same signal reach cells in different positions accurately? How often does a write fail after repeated use? How much energy do the peripheral circuits add?

These are **the next questions for a product-level evaluation**. The public abstract, figure, and data-availability information did not establish lifetime or error rates for a complete memory array. Data and code are available from the authors on request. [Public materials and data availability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41928-026-01699-w)

Electrical picosecond magnetization switching had already been reported in April 2026. The distinctive contribution here is **a concrete way to place the ultrafast pulse source close to the device**. [Earlier related study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72582-7)

## What makes the result worth following?

Shrinking an experimental setup asks whether conditions that were once available only in a laboratory can become part of a working circuit.

The next milestone is sustaining fast, reliable writes with multiple cells and their peripheral circuits connected. **Usable high-speed memory will be judged by the whole operation**, beyond its shortest pulse.
