## PDF p.4 — 5.2 AlexNet: Architecture and input

{/* source-content:P5-C015 */}

### 5.2 AlexNet — Overview / Details.

{/* source-content:P5-C016 */}

AlexNet is introduced as the ILSVRC 2012 winner and a large CNN for its time, with approximately 60M parameters and 650K neurons.

{/* source-content:P5-C017 */}

It classifies an input image into one of 1,000 classes. Each component of the 1,000-dimensional output is interpreted as a class probability, and the components sum to 1.

{/* source-content:P5-C018 */}

Input images are prepared as RGB 256x256 images: resize the shorter side to 256, then crop. For grayscale images, replicate the single channel to obtain three RGB channels.

{/* source-content:P5-C019 */}

During training, take a random 227x227 crop within the 256x256 image and feed it to the first layer.

{/* source-content:P5-C020 */}

AlexNet consists of five convolutional layers and three fully connected layers.

<Part5ArchitectureVisual kind="alexnet" />

{/* source-content:P5-C021 */}

ReLU follows convolutional and fully connected layers. The first two convolutions are followed by local normalization and then pooling. The slide adds that normalization later proved to offer limited benefit.

{/* source-content:P5-C022 */}

A central point is that ReLU accelerated deep CNN training compared with saturating activations such as tanh and sigmoid.

## PDF p.5 — AlexNet: ReLU·augmentation·dropout

{/* source-content:P5-C023 */}

**Source figure.** A CIFAR-10 plot states that ReLU (solid) reached 25% training error about six times faster than tanh (dotted).

{/* source-content:P5-C024 */}

For large positive or negative inputs, tanh saturates and its slope approaches zero, which can slow gradient descent.

{/* source-content:P5-C025 */}

The intuition is that ReLU speeds optimization because its slope in the positive region stays away from zero. The source also notes that its slope in the negative region is zero.

{/* source-content:P5-C026 */}

Mirroring and random crops mitigate overfitting. The source describes mirroring as doubling the dataset and random cropping as expanding it by a factor of 2048.

{/* source-content:P5-C027 */}

Dropout is another form of regularization.

{/* source-content:P5-C028 */}

## PDF p.6 — 5.3 VGGNet: Stacking small kernels deeply

{/* source-content:P5-C029 */}

### 5.3 VGGNet — Overview / Motivation / Details.

{/* source-content:P5-C030 */}

VGG16 is introduced as a 16-layer CNN and a leading ILSVRC 2014 model; the source gives an ImageNet test accuracy of 92.7%.

{/* source-content:P5-C031 */}

Instead of AlexNet’s large early kernels (11x11 and 5x5), VGG uses sequences of small 3x3 filters.

{/* source-content:P5-C032 */}

The first convolution uses a 3x3 receptive field and stride 1, compared with AlexNet’s 11x11 kernel and stride 4.

{/* source-content:P5-C033 */}

Stacking smaller convolutions provides a large receptive field while inserting more nonlinear activations.

{/* source-content:P5-C034 */}

The slide suggests that small convolutions reduce overfitting and describes 3x3 as the smallest size that captures left–right and up–down spatial information.

{/* source-content:P5-C035 */}

VGG16 has approximately 138M parameters and is described as 13 convolutional layers plus three fully connected layers.

## PDF p.7 — VGG16 details and the transition to GoogLeNet

{/* source-content:P5-C036 */}

VGG takes RGB 224x224 inputs; the source describes center cropping to standardize ImageNet input size.

{/* source-content:P5-C037 */}

Convolutions mainly use 3x3 kernels, with some 1x1 convolutions. A 1x1 convolution combined with ReLU increases nonlinearity without changing the receptive field.

{/* source-content:P5-C038 */}

Hidden layers use ReLU. AlexNet-style Local Response Normalization is described as increasing time and memory costs with little accuracy improvement.

{/* source-content:P5-C039 */}

Pooling reduces feature-map dimensionality and parameter burden, while channel counts grow from 64 to 128 to 256 to 512.

{/* source-content:P5-C040 */}

The fully connected layers have 4096, 4096, and 1000 channels.

{/* source-content:P5-C041 */}

Two 3x3 convolutions correspond to a 5x5 receptive field; three correspond to a 7x7 receptive field.

{/* source-content:P5-C042 */}

Three 3x3 layers provide three ReLU operations and use 27C^2 parameters, fewer than the 49C^2 parameters of one 7x7 layer.

{/* source-content:P5-C043 */}

### Section 5.4 introduces GoogLeNet as the ILSVRC 2014 winner, noting its improvement over ZFNet/AlexNet and lower error than VGGNet.

## PDF p.8 — 5.4 GoogLeNet: 1×1 bottlenecks and Inception

{/* source-content:P5-C044 */}

GoogLeNet uses 1x1 convolutions internally and replaces the final fully connected layer with global average pooling. The source credits both ideas to Network In Network.

{/* source-content:P5-C045 */}

An Inception module applies different convolution and pooling branches to the same input in parallel, then stacks or concatenates their outputs.

{/* source-content:P5-C046 */}

NIN’s 1x1 convolution is introduced as a way to increase representational nonlinearity when combined with ReLU.

{/* source-content:P5-C047 */}

GoogLeNet uses 1x1 convolutions for dimensionality reduction, reducing computational bottlenecks and enabling greater depth and width.

{/* source-content:P5-C048 */}

Applying a 48-channel 5x5 convolution directly to a 14x14x480 input gives a stated operation count of 112.9M.

{/* source-content:P5-C049 */}

First reducing the input to 16 channels with a 1x1 convolution and then producing 48 channels with a 5x5 convolution reduces the stated count to 5.3M.

{/* source-content:P5-C050 */}

**Source figure.** The original Inception module places 1x1, 3x3, and 5x5 convolutions alongside 3x3 max pooling in parallel.

## PDF p.12 — ResNet details, BN, and 5.6 WRN

{/* source-content:P5-C074 */}

### Further details.

{/* source-content:P5-C076 */}

ResNet is described as using batch normalization after every convolutional layer.

{/* source-content:P5-C077 */}

BN normalizes hidden activations using the current batch’s mean and variance and is generally applied immediately before the nonlinear function.

{/* source-content:P5-C078 */}

The source says the explanation for BN’s effectiveness remains unsettled. It presents the original internal-covariate-shift account, the hypothesis of reduced interdependence between layers, and the optimization-landscape-smoothing account alongside one another.

{/* source-content:P5-C079 */}

The source states that ResNet does not use dropout.

{/* source-content:P5-C080 */}

### 5.6 Wide residual network (WRN).

{/* source-content:P5-C081 */}

Widening ResNet can produce shallower networks with equal or better accuracy, reducing the layer count and training time.

{/* source-content:P5-C082 */}

From circuit complexity, the source notes that shallow circuits may require exponentially more components than deeper circuits.

{/* source-content:P5-C083 */}

The diminishing-feature-reuse problem is described: in thin, deep ResNets, gradients may bypass residual weights, leaving only some blocks to learn useful representations.

{/* source-content:P5-C084 */}

WRN widens networks by increasing feature planes. It uses deepening factor l and widening factor k: k=1 gives the ResNet width, while k>1 makes it k times wider.

{/* source-content:P5-C085 */}

> **Source footnote.** Preserve the note that BN is sometimes applied after the nonlinear function.

## PDF p.14 — Dense block: sequential/addition/concatenation

{/* source-content:P5-C094 */}

In the dense-block discussion, a standard feed-forward network connects the l-th output to the (l+1)-th input.

{/* source-content:P5-C095 */}

The standard transition is defined as `x_l=H_l(x_{l-1})`, with H_l representing a composite function such as convolution, ReLU, pooling, or BN.

{/* source-content:P5-C096 */}

ResNet uses identity mapping and element-wise addition to obtain `x_l=H_l(x_{l-1})+x_{l-1}`.

{/* source-content:P5-C097 */}

DenseNet concatenates all preceding features and supplies them as `x_l=H_l([x_0,...,x_{l-1}])`.

{/* source-content:P5-C098 */}

DenseNet defines H_l as the sequence BN → ReLU → 3x3 convolution.

{/* source-content:P5-C099 */}

Transition layers between dense blocks use a 1x1 convolution followed by 2x2 average pooling.

{/* source-content:P5-C100 */}

Feature maps within a dense block have the same spatial size, allowing concatenation. The final dense block is followed by GAP and a softmax classifier.

# Editorial and mathematical verification

{/* editorial-content:P5-E001 */}

### P5-E001
Read the VGG slide’s statement that “3x3 is the optimal/smallest possible size” as lecture intuition about the smallest odd kernel that captures spatial context in both directions. A 1x1 convolution also exists; the statement does not establish that 3x3 is universally optimal for every purpose.

{/* editorial-content:P5-E002 */}

### P5-E002
The AlexNet slide’s intuition that “most neurons usually end up positive” is not a general theorem. Keep that sentence separate from the historical explanation that ReLU’s nonsaturating positive branch helped optimization.

{/* editorial-content:P5-E003 */}

### P5-E003
As in the source, retain multiple hypotheses about BN’s effectiveness. Do not identify internal covariate shift as the sole established cause; note that later work discusses optimization landscapes and reparameterization.

{/* editorial-content:P5-E004 */}

### P5-E004
DenseNet’s claim of “higher computational and memory efficiency” concerns parameter reuse and compactness. Dense concatenation can increase activation-memory traffic, so this claim should not be equated with hardware efficiency in modern implementations.

{/* editorial-content:P5-E005 */}

### P5-E005
Preserve the source wording that the SE architecture won ILSVRC 2017, while reading the end of the challenge and SENet’s competition results in their historical context.

## A compact map, reread from a 2026 perspective

AlexNet made large-scale CNN training practical through **ReLU, data augmentation, and dropout**. VGG emphasized **the simplicity of deep stacks of small 3×3 kernels**; GoogLeNet, **1×1 bottlenecks, multi-branch Inception, and GAP**; ResNet/WRN, **identity paths and the depth/width trade-off**; DenseNet, **feature reuse through concatenation**; and SE, **adaptive channel-wise recalibration**.

In 2026, evaluation also includes **ViT/Swin attention backbones**, **modernized ConvNets such as ConvNeXt**, **training/scaling recipes**, **self-supervised or multimodal foundation encoders**, and **calibration, distribution shift, robustness, and transfer**. Asking only which architecture has the highest ImageNet top-1 accuracy is therefore no longer enough.
