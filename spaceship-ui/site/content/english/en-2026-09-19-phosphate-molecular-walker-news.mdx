## 1. A molecule “walks” by changing its attachment site

<strong>Chemical fuel can form a bond, and selectively breaking one of the connections can move a phosphate group to the next foothold on a molecule.</strong> A September 18, 2026 *Nature Chemistry* paper presents a nonenzymatic reaction cycle that repeats this process on glycerol and inositol scaffolds. The moving group is phosphate. The track is a molecular scaffold carrying hydroxyl groups. Fuel drives the rearrangement of bonds along that track.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Imagine a change in which atoms are bonded rather than a miniature robot walking across your palm. Evidence for a molecular step comes from attachment sites before and after a reaction and from the fractions of chemical species over time.

Moving phosphate once is only the starting point. The aim is to repeat the same reaction principle while fuel is supplied, without issuing different external commands for each step. The authors also seek a distribution different from the one favored at thermal equilibrium. The question is whether <strong>fuel consumption, bond exchange and changes in site occupancy</strong> can be linked in one system.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<small>Published September 18, 2026. Article current as of September 19. The review uses the final public paper and Figures 1 and 4. The mass-balance and efficiency examples below explain the concepts without reanalyzing raw NMR data.</small>

## 2. A small machine needs both grip and timely release

### Staying attached alone cannot carry a walker forward

The walker must connect to its next foothold before entirely losing the current one. If the bond is too stable, it cannot leave. This balance resembles a gripper that must hold and release at appropriate moments. Here reaction rates and selectivity supply those functions.

The left cycle in Figure 1a shows fuel entering and waste leaving. The lower species is a phosphate monoester and the upper species a phosphate diester. In this context the monoester connects with one hydroxyl on the scaffold. The cyclic diester reaches two neighboring positions and forms a ring. Hydrolysis can reopen it at the original position or leave phosphate attached at another, depending on which bond breaks.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

R and R′ stand for organic groups linked to phosphate. Black and red bonds distinguish states within the reaction cycle. Arrows show a sequence of chemical transformations. Following fuel through to waste identifies the energy supply for those transformations.

### Ring geometry affects the reaction rate

The right of original Figure 1a compares slow hydrolysis of a stable phosphodiester with opening of a five-membered ring under acidic conditions. The design temporarily produces a cyclic intermediate and exploits selective ring opening. Fuel forms an additional bond, then water breaks one connection so another cycle can begin.<a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

The two half-lives refer to different substances and conditions. Their ratio cannot be interpreted as a speedup of the new walker. The comparison shows how strongly structure and environment alter bond-exchange timescales, which must be compatible for the cycle to run.

Opening the first ring bond differs from severing the last connection and losing the phosphate from the track. Replacing one connection while keeping the other can retain the group through several steps. Processivity concerns this continuity. A single-step yield therefore cannot establish long-distance processivity.

## 3. Figure 1b: one fuel links two chemical operations

### Comparing end and center sites on glycerol

At the top of Figure 1b, G3P is phosphate attached near a terminal hydroxyl on glycerol, and G2P has it at the central position. Blue and red P symbols identify different sites. EDC marks a reaction path supplied with carbodiimide fuel. The full network also contains the cyclic GCP intermediate and side reactions.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

A step has three parts. Phosphate retains its current connection while adding a bond to a neighboring hydroxyl to form a ring. Water opens the ring at one of the two connections. Another cycle can begin from the remaining attachment. Some openings return to the starting site, so each fuel consumption event cannot be counted as forward motion.

Distinct performance measures ask whether fuel entered the desired network, whether it formed a cyclic intermediate, whether opening changed site and whether phosphate stayed on the track over several cycles. These use different denominators. A single efficiency number would hide which part improved.

### Testing a longer track on inositol

The lower inositol scaffold tests movement across five hydroxyl sites, extending the two-position comparison to a longer track. Gray arrows show possible transitions to neighbors. The different stereochemical states at opposite ends relate to an experiment asking whether the group crossed the track.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

A ring-shaped carbon scaffold need not function as a continuously rotating one-way motor. The experiment tests phosphate movement over five footholds. Circular geometry and persistent unidirectional dynamics are separate properties. Glycerol supplied a tractable reaction network and inositol tested extension to a longer scaffold.

## 4. Compare equilibrium occupancy with the fuel-driven distribution

### Equilibrium does not put every molecule at the lowest-energy site

At equilibrium, molecules can still change states. Opposing changes balance in the ensemble, establishing a distribution that needs no sustained external energy consumption. Site populations depend on free energies under the given conditions. Evaluating directionality first requires knowing the distribution approached without fuel.

Long observations of the glycerol system in acidic aqueous solution approached approximately 90% G3P and 10% G2P. In a separate cycling experiment with repeated fuel additions, G2P reached about 64%. Fuel can therefore raise occupancy of the less favored equilibrium site.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Keep the experimental clocks separate. The long approach to equilibrium, repeated dosing experiment and quasi-steady interval in Figure 4 are different measurements. <strong>The 64% value is not the G2P fraction in Figure 4's steady-state interval.</strong> Figure 4 follows a mixture containing cyclic intermediate and by-products as well as the two sites.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

### Fast and slow paths can bias the distribution

Kinetic asymmetry means that the network does not operate identically in opposing directions. Fuel activates a species through one path. If subsequent return paths differ in rate and selectivity, the system can maintain a different distribution from equilibrium. The routes that form and break bonds matter alongside static structural asymmetry.

The authors report a kinetic asymmetry parameter q of 11.9 in their reaction-rate model. Its meaning depends on the network and definition. It is neither an 11.9-fold walking speed nor a single-step probability or thermodynamic efficiency. The 64% occupancy alone cannot determine q.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

A model can connect time courses for several species through one network, going beyond fitting a final ratio. Agreement does not prove that this network is unique. Which states and side reactions are included and which rates were measured independently determine the strength of the interpretation.

## 5. Figure 4: a large fuel dose and sustained supply have different effects

### Panel a: fuel also changes the reaction environment

The left of Figure 4a shows the time course after a large single addition of EDC. Time is horizontal, with relative abundance and pH on different axes. Adding fuel changes both concentration and pH and thus the rates of subsequent reactions. Extended accumulation of an intermediate alone cannot establish stable autonomous operation.<a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

The right shows divided fuel additions. Vertical gray lines mark dosing, dark points track EDC and the rising gray signal shows EDU waste accumulation. The pH plot below shows the acidity range maintained by this strategy. The authors control total fuel and <strong>the environment while the cycle operates</strong>.<a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

The importance of timing is familiar from automation. Applying a large voltage once differs from delivering power steadily. In the molecular system the supply strategy changes the state. The analogy illustrates how equal total inputs can give different results when distributed differently over time.

### Panel b: a flat trace can conceal ongoing reactions

The upper part of b summarizes the G3P, GCP and G2P network. The lower plot combines species fractions from NMR as points and kinetic-model predictions as curves. Ratios remain roughly constant through the marked steady interval. Part a still shows fuel consumption and waste accumulation. Stable concentrations can coexist with ongoing reactions.<a href="#news-ref-3" data-news-citation="3" aria-label="Go to reference 3" data-astro-reload>[3]</a>

Gray by-products appear alongside black GCP, blue G3P and red G2P. Scaling only the two end positions to sum to 100% would remove molecules in intermediates from the account. Check the symbols to see whether changing fractions reflect exchange between two sites or occupancy of additional states.

When fuel addition stops the curves change again. The network's autonomous steps depend on fuel and environmental conditions. An experimenter added fuel several times. Between additions, the network exchanged bonds without a different external instruction for each step. This is the sense of autonomy demonstrated here. Fuel supply and environmental maintenance were not fully automated.

## 6. Reading steady state and efficiency quantitatively

<CandidateEquation id="walker-balance" lang="en" title="Why can reactions continue while concentrations stay constant?" tex={String.raw`\frac{dc_i}{dt}=J_{i,\mathrm{in}}-J_{i,\mathrm{out}}\tag{1}`}>

This relation is a mass balance for interpreting Figure 4. <Math tex={String.raw`c_i`} /> is the concentration of species i and t is time. <Math tex={String.raw`J_{i,\mathrm{in}}`} /> adds incoming production terms per unit time, while <Math tex={String.raw`J_{i,\mathrm{out}}`} /> adds consumption terms for conversion to other species. Both J terms have units of concentration per time.

Production adds to concentration and consumption subtracts. If a hypothetical species is generated at 4 μM/s and consumed at 4 μM/s, its net change is zero, but both processes continue. A concentration trace alone may look identical if both rates are zero.

Measuring fuel use and waste production helps distinguish those cases. Evidence for a nonequilibrium steady state therefore includes flows of energy and matter as well as approximately constant concentrations. The example's 4 μM/s is illustrative and is not a paper rate constant.

</CandidateEquation>

<CandidateEquation id="walker-efficiency" lang="en" title="What is the denominator in 16% fuel efficiency?" tex={String.raw`\eta_{\mathrm{cyc}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{fuel\;used\;for\;cyclization}}}{n_{\mathrm{fuel\;consumed}}}\tag{2}`}>

This expression spells out one of the paper's efficiency measures: the share of consumed fuel that forms the desired ring. The numerator is the amount leading to cyclization and the denominator is all fuel consumed. Both are counted in moles, giving a dimensionless ratio. The paper reports about 16% cyclization efficiency.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Imagine that 16 of 100 fuel-consumption events produce rings. The ratio is 16/100. Other consumption fails to produce that useful intermediate. Even after a ring forms, it can reopen at the starting site, so ring formation does not equal productive steps.

The amount of GCP seen at an instant is the stock currently in that state. The cumulative number of GCP formation events over time can be much larger as GCP also disappears. Keeping stock and throughput separate makes the efficiency numerator meaningful. The explanatory expression and 100-event example have no original equation number in the paper.

</CandidateEquation>

The paper separately discusses about 17% catalytic efficiency and up to 18% coupling efficiency. Similar numerical values do not make them one performance range. Participation of fuel in coupled chemistry, fuel leading to rings and fuel associated with transient net migration answer different questions.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 7. What did the experiments establish?

<CandidateTable id="walker-scope" label="Experimental conditions and scope of the phosphate walker results">

| Question | Public evidence | Interpretation |

| Can fuel shift site occupancy? | Equilibrium comparison and changed occupancy after repeated dosing | Keep percentages from different feeding experiments separate. |
| Can the state be maintained during supply? | Figure 4 concentration, fuel, waste and pH time courses | Persistent state does not imply stopped reactions. |
| Does phosphate move on a longer track? | Migration across five inositol footholds | A short track does not establish long-distance transport. |
| Is this a practical molecular machine? | A nonenzymatic cycle and a design principle for processivity | Strong acid, fuel loss, side reactions and controllability still matter. |

</CandidateTable>

The study links chemical fuel with selective bond exchange without taking over an existing enzyme machine. Phosphate is familiar in biology, but biological relevance does not imply operation in a living system. Important reactions used strongly acidic conditions around pH 1. Biological use would require substantially different compatibility and stability tests.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The demonstrated functions are positional migration and operation of the reaction network. Carrying an external cargo to a target, coordinating several walkers or choosing paths in response to a sensor each requires further work. Naming a potential molecular robot should not obscure which functions were tested.

By-product accumulation matters for operation over longer periods. A main pathway can work over a short experiment while small side reactions accumulate over many repeats. Increasing fuel alone cannot resolve this. Follow the fuel, waste and material lost from the track to evaluate repeatability.

Figure 4 also shows how a useful operating range can fail. Fuel addition changes the environment, which alters intermediate reactions. Conditions needed to sustain a regime matter alongside the numbers observed inside it.

## 8. Future comparisons need an energy balance as well as a step count

Can longer tracks connect more sites? With length, both opportunities for desired steps and chances of stalling or leaving accumulate. Shifting occupancy between two sites differs from reaching one specified position after many steps.

How long can the same conditions be maintained? Even controlled fuel input may allow waste and side products to change the environment. Continuous-flow supply, waste removal and improved selectivity are follow-up design questions. This paper did not implement all these features.

Comparable efficiency measures must separately define occupancy shifts, productive steps and associated free-energy changes for a fixed fuel consumption. Such definitions are needed to compare different machines and identify design improvements.

The team released experimental measurements and NMR data. They permit reexamination of peak assignments, time traces and observables used in the model. Independent preparation and reproduction under the same conditions would be a further test. This article's worked examples did not fit raw data or perform independent synthesis.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-4" data-news-citation="4" aria-label="Go to reference 4" data-astro-reload>[4]</a>

<strong>The design principle is to use fuel to connect two sites briefly, then selectively release one to create a new attachment position.</strong> Repeating that principle requires matching reaction rates, fuel delivery, environment and loss pathways. The conditions under which those connections persist determine the extent of autonomy.
