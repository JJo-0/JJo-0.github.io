A <strong>transistor</strong> is a semiconductor switch that turns current on and off. Four parts explain how a simple field-effect transistor works.

- The <strong>source</strong> supplies electrons to the channel under the bias used to measure this n-type device.
- The <strong>drain</strong> takes up the channel current when a voltage is applied between it and the source. In a FET, which contact plays which role can depend on the bias direction.
- The <strong>channel</strong> is the semiconductor path through which current flows between the source and drain.
- The <strong>gate</strong> is the control electrode. It changes the charge and potential in the channel to open or close the path.

<strong>Monolayer MoS₂</strong> is a two-dimensional semiconductor with three atomic planes in each crystalline sheet: sulfur (S), molybdenum (Mo), and sulfur. Current flows in a much thinner body than in a conventional thick silicon channel, so the gate field can reach the full channel more easily. Contacts, dielectric materials, and geometry still matter. The <a href="#news-ref-6" data-astro-reload>2011 paper on single-layer MoS₂ transistors</a> describes the material and an early device.

<strong>What Figure 1 shows.</strong> Panel a establishes the scale of fabrication, an array on a four-inch wafer. Panel b shows the actual device with its air gap and gate–contact overlap. Panel c compares switching performance in silicon and other devices as the <em>physical channel length</em> becomes very short. The figure defines <strong>what the authors built and what they chose to compare</strong>.

The reported 4.8 nm is the <strong>physical channel distance between the inner edges of the source and drain contacts</strong> in cross section. It is distinct from the spacing between measurement probes, the overall contact layout, the gate length, or the commercial designation “5 nm process node.” The <a href="#news-ref-4" data-astro-reload>MIT introduction to MOSFETs</a> likewise defines channel length as the distance current must cross between the source and drain.

MIT and OpenStax provide general instruction, and the 2011 study documents an early device. <strong>The measurements and processes discussed below come from the 2026 paper and its supplement, listed separately in the references.</strong>

## 1. Can a transistor be made smaller and still turn off cleanly?

A transistor uses gate voltage to open and close a current path. Shorter channels fit more switches into a given area, but become harder to turn off. Using a monolayer of molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), the researchers <strong>measured a subthreshold swing of 88 mV/dec and more than a millionfold change in current in a representative device with a physical channel about 4.8 nm long. They also fabricated an array on a four-inch wafer.</strong> The experiment connects physical size, current control, and fabrication scale.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The 4.8 nm dimension is the measured distance between the source and drain boundaries. The channel is the semiconductor region between those electrodes, and the gate controls its potential and charge. Distinguishing the physical gap, the gate-electrode length, and the commercial node name makes the scale of the experiment clear.

Read Figure 1 from left to right. Panel a shows the wafer, b shows the air gap below the channel and the gate overlapping the contacts, and c compares devices by physical channel length. The energy curves below the schematic depict both the barrier electrons must overcome and the electron distribution at the contacts. The question is <strong>how much control the gate retains when the physical channel becomes this short</strong>.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 2. Why does a shorter channel make it harder to turn off the current?

### The gate and the drain influence the same region

A short channel looks attractive when the switch is on. In the off state, however, a barrier must keep electrons in the source from entering the channel. In a long channel the gate can control that barrier relatively easily. At extremely short lengths the drain field reaches across the device to the barrier, letting electrons pass even when the gate attempts to shut off current. This is a short-channel effect. The paper treats electrostatic coupling and direct source–drain tunneling as important limits on scaling.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Monolayer MoS₂ has almost no thick interior far from the gate. The gate can therefore control the channel potential more effectively. Other electrodes still exert an influence. The material beneath the channel, the overlap between the gate and contacts, and screening of the gate field by electrodes all affect the outcome. The researchers designed the material, geometry, and fabrication process together.

Their design–technology co-optimization (DTCO) has a concrete example. A sacrificial layer roughly 4 nm thick defines the nanogap. Removing it leaves a low-permittivity region, and a MoS₂ sheet spans the opening. The same fabrication step that establishes a tiny dimension also sets the device’s electrical environment.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### Gate length and channel length measure different things

The edge of a thin electrode can form a very short gate. If the source and drain are far apart, the device still avoids much of the leakage challenge faced when those contacts approach one another. The authors compare devices by physical channel length. Check the horizontal axis in Figure 1c and the subsequent comparisons before reading the highlighted points. This is the meaning of the 4.8 nm dimension.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 3. How did the researchers make a 5 nm gap?

<NewsFigure media="sep22-mos2-fig-2" lang="en" />

**Figure 2 shows how the fabrication process creates a gap narrower than 5 nm.** Across the top of panel a, the researchers form the source, deposit a thin sacrificial spacer, form the drain metal, and use angled ion milling. Below, they remove the overlapping metal and sacrificial layer, transfer MoS₂, and form the gate. <strong>The thickness of an atomic layer deposition (ALD) spacer sets the narrow gap.</strong> Photolithography defines the larger electrodes and patterns, while self-alignment establishes the critical nanoscale dimension.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Think of coating a wall with a film of uniform thickness and then removing the film to leave a gap of that thickness. In the actual device, the process must also break metallic connections, remove excess overlap, and electrically isolate the electrodes. Figure 2c measures leakage across the gap before transfer of the MoS₂. It remained below a picoampere per micrometer up to 2.5 V. The off current of a completed transistor is a separate measurement.

Figure 2b shows an important detail: tape. The researchers used tape-assisted peeling to form wafer-scale structures, and <strong>explicitly state that this tape process is incompatible with CMOS processing.</strong> They developed a wet-etch route to remove the sacrificial layer and overlapping metal. The supplement describes its steps and shows example devices. The wafer photograph establishes the demonstrated scale. The wet process addresses a separate question about CMOS compatibility.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a><a href="#news-ref-2" data-news-citation="2" aria-label="Go to reference 2" data-astro-reload>[2]</a>

They then transferred large-area monolayer MoS₂ grown by MOCVD across the gap and formed the top dielectric and gate. The colorized electron micrograph in Figure 2d locates the electrodes. Its 10 μm scale bar applies to the overall layout. The following cross section measures the nanometer-scale gap at the junction.

## 4. Which voltage and current give 88 mV/dec?

<NewsFigure media="sep22-mos2-fig-3" lang="en" />

**Figure 3 tests whether the gap was about 4.8 nm and how effectively the transistor switched at that length.** The cross-sectional TEM image in panel a shows the source–drain gap. The elemental map in b identifies the semiconductor and adjacent layers. Panel c samples the channel length at multiple positions across the width of one device. Across 980 intervals the authors report a mean of 4.8 nm and a standard deviation of 0.9 nm. This measures <strong>edge variation within one transistor</strong>. A distribution across 980 separate transistors would measure something different.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Panel d plots drain current versus gate voltage on a logarithmic current axis. Equal vertical distances indicate equal ratios of current. In the subthreshold region, subthreshold swing (SS) is the gate-voltage change required for a tenfold current change. A smaller SS means a smaller voltage change alters the current by a full order of magnitude. Open the equation for a numerical example.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="mos2-ss" title="What does it mean to need 88 mV for a tenfold current change?" tex={String.raw`\mathrm{SS}=\frac{\Delta V_{\mathrm{GS}}}{\Delta\log_{10}I_{\mathrm D}}\tag{1}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`V_{\mathrm{GS}}`} /> is gate voltage relative to the source and <Math tex={String.raw`I_{\mathrm D}`} /> is the current measured at a fixed drain bias. The numerator is the difference between two gate voltages. The denominator counts the decades of current between the two points. This finite difference illustrates a local slope. The differential form gives its precise value at a point.

When current rises from 1 nA to 10 nA, <Math tex={String.raw`\log_{10}(10)-\log_{10}(1)=1`} />. If that takes 88 mV of gate voltage, SS is 88 mV/dec. At the same slope a three-decade change would take <Math tex={String.raw`88\times3=264\;\mathrm{mV}`} />, and six decades would take 528 mV. This example illustrates how to read a slope. Reconstructing the full operating-voltage range would require the measured curve over the full ON/OFF interval.

Why take the logarithm? Changes from 1 to 10 nA and from 10 to 100 nA have different absolute sizes, but both span one decade. A log scale makes the exponential subthreshold response easier to read. When comparing devices, distinguish a slope averaged between two points from one taken at the steepest part of the curve.

</CandidateEquation>

The representative device has an ON/OFF ratio of about <Math tex={String.raw`1.1\times10^6`} /> between gate voltages of −1 and +1 V at a drain voltage of 0.5 V. Its maximum current modulation over the full measured gate range is higher, about <Math tex={String.raw`8\times10^7`} />. The ratio depends on which two currents are used. Panel e gives the output characteristics and f summarizes SS across devices.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="mos2-on-off" title="Which two currents give an ON/OFF ratio of one million?" tex={String.raw`R_{\mathrm{on/off}}=\frac{I_{\mathrm{on}}}{I_{\mathrm{off}}}\tag{A}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`I_{\mathrm{on}}`} /> is the drain current at the chosen ON gate voltage. <Math tex={String.raw`I_{\mathrm{off}}`} /> is the drain current at the chosen OFF gate voltage under the same drain bias. A higher ratio gives greater separation between on and off currents. Meaningful comparisons specify both gate voltages and whether current is normalized by device width.

For an illustrative example, take <Math tex={String.raw`I_{\mathrm{off}}=1\;\mathrm{pA}`} /> and <Math tex={String.raw`I_{\mathrm{on}}=1.1\;\mathrm{\mu A}`} />. Converting the units gives <Math tex={String.raw`1.1\times10^{-6}/10^{-12}=1.1\times10^6`} />. The ON current is 1.1 million times the OFF current. These two example currents demonstrate the calculation independently of the measured points in Figure 3.

SS measures the <strong>slope</strong> of the subthreshold curve. The ON/OFF ratio compares the <strong>current levels</strong> at two specified operating points. Even a steep slope can yield a modest ratio if the operating-voltage range is narrow or the ON current is small.

## 5. What changed with the air gap and gate overlap?

<NewsFigure media="sep22-mos2-fig-5" lang="en" />

**Figure 5 examines why the air gap and gate overlap helped the gate control a short channel.** It uses quantum-transport simulations interpreted alongside measured curves. Color in panel a shows potential inside the device. Panel b plots the conduction-band barrier at different gate voltages. Panels c–d vary the relative permittivity beneath the channel, e gives charge density versus position, and f varies gate–contact overlap. These structural comparisons come from changes in the model. The researchers did not fabricate and measure every variant individually.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### The air gap reduces unwanted electrical coupling

The role of geometry becomes clearer if we distinguish the capacitance coupling the gate to the channel from the capacitances coupling the source and drain to it. Equation (2) in the paper gives the following approximation.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="mos2-capacitance" title="Why is gate capacitance in the denominator and parasitic coupling in the numerator?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}\mathrm{SS}&\simeq\frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{q}\ln 10\,m\\m&=1+\frac{C_{\mathrm S}+C_{\mathrm D}+C_{\mathrm{it}}}{C_{\mathrm G}}\end{aligned}\tag{2}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`k_{\mathrm B}`} /> is the Boltzmann constant, <Math tex={String.raw`T`} /> is absolute temperature, and <Math tex={String.raw`q`} /> is the elementary charge. The first factor sets a reference from the thermal electron distribution and the logarithmic current scale. At about 300 K, <Math tex={String.raw`(k_{\mathrm B}T/q)\ln10`} /> is approximately 59.5 mV. The factor <Math tex={String.raw`m`} /> expresses the departure from ideal gate control.

<Math tex={String.raw`C_{\mathrm G}`} /> describes gate–channel coupling, <Math tex={String.raw`C_{\mathrm S},C_{\mathrm D}`} /> represents parasitic source and drain coupling to the channel, and <Math tex={String.raw`C_{\mathrm{it}}`} /> accounts for interface traps. Stronger useful gate coupling enlarges the denominator and reduces the additional term. Stronger coupling to source, drain, or traps enlarges the numerator and worsens SS. The value 1 sets the baseline with no additional term.

For example, a ratio of 0.5 between the combined parasitic and trap capacitances and the gate capacitance gives <Math tex={String.raw`m=1.5`} />, or about 90 mV/dec using a 60 mV reference. A ratio of 1 gives roughly 120 mV/dec. These hypothetical values illustrate the trend. Extracting the device capacitances would require additional evidence, as would a full description of other transport effects in an ultrashort channel.

</CandidateEquation>

Air’s low relative permittivity helps reduce parasitic coupling below the channel. Figure 5c varies the permittivity <strong>of the gap beneath the channel</strong>. The dielectric above the channel serves a different purpose: it should maintain effective gate coupling. In the simulations, raising the gap permittivity from 1 to 25 worsens SS to more than 300 mV/dec.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

### An overlapping gate controls the barrier and the electron supply

A second effect occurs at the contacts. The position-dependent charge density in Figure 5e shows whether gate voltage changes only the barrier height or also the electron supply in front of it. Equation (3) in the paper expresses the two contributions as a product.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="mos2-contact" title="Why multiply electron supply by the fraction that crosses the barrier?" tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}J_{\mathrm E}&=q\,n_{\mathrm s}(V_{\mathrm{GS}})v_{\mathrm{inj}}\\&\quad\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{q\varphi_{\mathrm{ch}}(V_{\mathrm{GS}})}{k_{\mathrm B}T}\right]\end{aligned}\tag{3}`}>

<Math tex={String.raw`J_{\mathrm E}`} /> is current density in this transport model, <Math tex={String.raw`n_{\mathrm s}`} /> is carrier density in the contact region, and <Math tex={String.raw`v_{\mathrm{inj}}`} /> is injection velocity. <Math tex={String.raw`\varphi_{\mathrm{ch}}`} /> is the potential barrier from the source into the channel, and <Math tex={String.raw`q\varphi_{\mathrm{ch}}`} /> is the corresponding energy barrier. The denominator <Math tex={String.raw`k_{\mathrm B}T`} /> also has units of energy, so their ratio in the exponent is dimensionless.

The first factor gives the available charge flow. The exponential gives the fraction able to cross thermally. Even with a large supply, a small crossing fraction reduces current, hence the multiplication. The minus sign means that increasing the barrier suppresses transmission. The gate voltage appears in both functions because it can change both supply and barrier.

If carrier supply halves and the exponential term also halves, all else equal, the current ratio is <Math tex={String.raw`(1/2)\times(1/2)=1/4`} />. This illustrates how the effects combine. Panel f explores the structural design by varying the overlap length in simulation.

</CandidateEquation>

Placing MoS₂ on top of the contact metal lets the gate field reach the semiconductor over the contacts. The authors attribute the dual control to the low density of states in that region and the gate overlap. Their simulations compare zero overlap with several nanometers of overlap. The result emphasizes designing where the field acts. It does not establish that increasing the overlap indefinitely would help.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

## 6. The silicon limit in the title and the 60 mV/dec benchmark are different comparisons

The measured 88 mV/dec is above the familiar room-temperature reference of about 60 mV/dec. The authors ask <strong>how much SS degrades in silicon at the same very short physical dimensions</strong>. They calculate a comparison boundary with a quantum-transport model assuming a 3 nm silicon body and a double gate. Those assumptions define the line in Figure 4.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

The result demonstrates retained gate control in an ultrashort channel. It makes no claim to sub-60 mV/dec switching. Maintaining a steeper slope than the comparison device near 5 nm is significant even when the measured SS remains above the thermal reference. Each benchmark answers a different question.

The cross-sectional TEM and electrical measurements describe fabricated MoS₂ devices. The silicon boundary and the changes in permittivity and overlap come from modeling. The study does not report a head-to-head power measurement of commercial silicon and a completed MoS₂ logic chip. SS alone cannot establish chip power, clock speed, or computational accuracy.

## 7. What stands between a four-inch wafer and manufacturing?

Four inches is about 101.6 mm. Comparing that diameter or area with a 300 mm wafer gives a geometric ratio, not a measure of process maturity. Larger areas accumulate variations in film transfer, contact uniformity, defects, alignment, and thermal history. The paper reports measurements on 138 devices to assess repeatability. Establishing yield requires clarifying how the statistical sample relates to the full measurement set and mapping results to wafer position and pass rates.

Even with good off-state behavior, a logic circuit needs sufficient drive current, low parasitic capacitance, stable threshold voltage, complementary devices, and interconnects. The authors emphasize subthreshold characteristics and identify further contact engineering as a way to improve on-state performance. Circuit speed and reliability require separate evaluation.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

Process compatibility also needs specifics. The Methods describe electrode materials, transfer, dielectric formation, and alternative removal steps. Each fabrication line would still have to assess contamination controls, thermal budgets, and long-term reliability. The wet-process alternative deserves consideration alongside the tape demonstration when evaluating the prospects for scaling.

## 8. What evidence should come next?

First, does the same fabrication flow reproduce the device distribution? Separate reporting of representative values, medians, overall manufacturing yield, and local channel-length variation would help identify causes. Wafer maps for the wet process and chip-to-chip variation under the same pass criteria would be particularly informative.

The next question is circuit behavior. Do inverters, oscillators, or memory circuits comprising several transistors retain the balance between leakage, speed, and power? Those are future tests. Prolonged bias and temperature stress could also reveal changes in the contacts, dielectric, and threshold voltage. The paper directs readers to request raw data from the authors, so access for reanalysis matters for replication.<a href="#news-ref-1" data-news-citation="1" aria-label="Go to reference 1" data-astro-reload>[1]</a>

<strong>Combining an atomically thin channel, an air gap, contact gating, and self-aligned fabrication enabled a relatively steep fall in current below a physical channel length of 5 nm.</strong> The meaning of 88 mV/dec, the sampling boundaries of the SS statistics, and the distinction between the tape and wet routes place that measurement in its manufacturing context.
