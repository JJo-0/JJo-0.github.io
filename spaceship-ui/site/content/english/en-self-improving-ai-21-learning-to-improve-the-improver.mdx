An inspection camera reads the digit 0 in a part number as the letter O. Someone adds “check for a zero first in a numeric field” to the reading instructions, and errors fall. So far, the <strong>inspection instructions</strong> have improved. Now suppose the next time a different error appears, the system also changes which failures it reads first and how it proposes a revision. That changes <strong>how edits are made</strong>. How could we tell which of the two improvements we are seeing?

[Chapter 20](/en/posts/self-improving-ai-20-retaining-skills-and-improving-the-improver/) tested whether old skills survived a change to an agent’s harness, the code and instructions around its model. Pages 36–40 of Part II turn to the <strong>improver</strong> itself. Hyperagents gives us an agent and a candidate-selection rule to examine. Learning to Self-Evolve shows how researchers train a model to edit context. The camera and part numbers are worked examples. The paper figures and tables carry the experimental results.

### Worked example 1. Does changing one editing rule help two different inspectors?

Consider a tabletop camera that inspects parts. In this <strong>hypothetical experiment</strong>, inspector A sometimes fails on reflections from metal, while inspector B struggles with partly occluded edges. Give the original editing rule U₀ and its replacement U₁ the same parent inspector and the same failure records. Each rule gets one edit under the same model-call and token budget. For each parent, set aside 20 labeled images that the editors never see. Score the parent and both children on those same 20 images.

| Parent inspector | Correct before editing | After an edit by U₀ | After an edit by U₁ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A · metal reflections | 14/20 | 15/20 | 17/20 |
| B · occluded edges | 16/20 | 18/20 | 17/20 |

Let Δᵢ(U) be the accuracy gain produced by rule U on parent i, and nᵢ the number of correct predictions in the table. Editing A with U₁ adds three correct predictions, a 15-percentage-point gain. Editing B with U₁ adds one, a gain of 5 points.

<Math tex={String.raw`\Delta_i(U)=\frac{n_i(\mathrm{after}\ U)-n_i(\mathrm{before})}{20},\qquad \Delta_A(U_1)=\frac{17-14}{20}=0.15,\quad \Delta_B(U_1)=\frac{17-16}{20}=0.05`} display />

Under the original rule U₀, A gained 5 points and B gained 10. Subtract the two rules’ gains within each parent, then take the mean, weighting each parent equally.

<Math tex={String.raw`\frac{(0.15-0.05)+(0.05-0.10)}{2}=0.025`} display />

Changing the rule produced an average <strong>additional gain of 2.5 percentage points</strong>. For reflection-prone A, U₁ gained 10 points more than U₀. For edge inspector B, it gained 5 points less. Those differences disappear if we look only at the two final U₁ scores, both 17/20. Evidence that the editor also improves in a new inspection setting would require further parent inspectors and independently repeated edits.

### Worked example 2. What does a 10.8-point difference in math accuracy measure?

The other paper cited on the slide, Yin and colleagues’ *Gödel Agent*, allows changes to both the policy that performs a task and the procedure that improves the policy. Table 1 evaluates the resulting <strong>task-solving policy</strong> on MGSM, a multilingual collection of grade-school math word problems. The matched comparison uses Gödel-base, which is closed-book and uses GPT-3.5 to execute the final policy. The MGSM evaluation used 128 validation problems and 800 test problems. Meta Agent Search achieved 53.4% test accuracy and Gödel-base achieved 64.2%. [Gödel Agent, Table 1](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1354.pdf#page=6) · [Appendix B, evaluation protocol](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1354.pdf#page=13)

<Math tex={String.raw`\Delta_{\mathrm{abs}}=64.2\%-53.4\%=10.8\ \mathrm{pp}`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\Delta_{\mathrm{rel}}=\frac{64.2-53.4}{53.4}=\frac{10.8}{53.4}\simeq 0.202=20.2\%.`} display />

The difference between the two accuracies is 10.8 percentage points. Relative to the 53.4% baseline, that is a 20.2% increase. Gödel-free scored 90.6% in the same table under different execution constraints, so it falls outside this comparison. The two matched scores tell us which resulting policy answered more MGSM problems correctly. Measuring <strong>the editor’s own ability to improve</strong> calls for observing the successors it produces on a new task.

### Worked example 3. Hold the editor fixed and measure its gain over 50 revisions

The Hyperagents authors transferred agents developed in paper review and robotics reward design to <strong>grading Olympiad-level math solutions</strong>. The grader receives a problem, a candidate solution, reference solutions, and grading guidelines. It predicts one of the expert grades 0, 1, 6, or 7. Accuracy is the fraction of solutions whose predicted grade matches the expert’s grade, with 100 examples in each of the training, validation, and test splits. Could the transferred meta agent, held fixed, produce a better task agent in this new domain? [Hyperagents, §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=9) · [Appendix C.4, grading data](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=33)

Generate candidates for up to k revisions, then select a candidate by <strong>validation accuracy</strong>. The difference between that candidate’s <strong>test accuracy</strong> and the starting agent’s test accuracy is improvement@k. Here A₀ is the starting grader and Aᵢ is the ith candidate.

<Math tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}i^\star&\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{1\leq i\leq k} S_{\mathrm{val}}(A_i),\\\mathrm{imp}@k&=S_{\mathrm{test}}(A_{i^\star})-S_{\mathrm{test}}(A_0).\end{aligned}`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\mathrm{imp}@50=0.630-0.000=0.630\quad(63.0\ \mathrm{pp}).`} display />

This is the reported median across five runs. The transferred agent began at zero on the new task. The candidates produced by its meta agent, selected on validation data, reached a median test score of 0.630 (95% bootstrap confidence interval for the gain, 0.540–0.630). With the same 50-revision budget, fixing the original, inexperienced meta agent instead yielded a median gain of 0.000. <strong>Fifty counts revisions. One hundred counts test solutions.</strong> The 0.630 measures agreement with expert grades. It does not count Olympiad problems solved by the agent. [Hyperagents, Fig. 3 and accompanying text](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=10) · [Transfer results](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=11)

The paper separates these choices experimentally. The main experiments fixed a handcrafted parent-selection rule that accounts for performance and how many children a candidate already has. This lets the authors study changes to the editor separately. In the additional experiment in Appendix E.5, the system starts with random selection and may revise <strong>the selection rule itself</strong>. ### Worked example 4. Why revisit the parent with the lower score?

Suppose we are improving a grader. Parent A has a normalized evaluation score of 0.82 and has already produced 15 children. Parent B has a score of 0.70 and has produced three. We will plug <strong>illustrative values</strong> into the form of exploration bonus observed in the appendix. Let N be the total number of children, nᵢ the number from parent i, sᵢ its score, and c the weight on exploration. In this example we make one choice by taking the parent with the higher selection score U.

<Math tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}U_i&=s_i+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln(N+1)}{n_i+1}},\\N&=15+3=18,\qquad\ln 19\simeq2.9444.\end{aligned}`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\begin{aligned}U_A&=0.82+0.4\sqrt{2.9444/16}\simeq0.992,\\U_B&=0.70+0.4\sqrt{2.9444/4}\simeq1.043.\end{aligned}`} display />

A leads by 0.12 on the evaluation score alone. B’s exploration bonus is about 0.343, compared with about 0.172 for A. Since the difference exceeds 0.12, the rule with c=0.4 chooses B. Lower c to 0.2 and A scores about 0.906 against B’s 0.872, reversing the choice. B’s 1.043 can exceed one because it ranks candidates. It is neither a predicted accuracy nor a probability that B’s child will succeed. Page 39 compares whether changing how parents are prioritized also changes <strong>final test performance</strong>. [Hyperagents, Appendix E.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=57)

### Worked example 5. How large is the gain in the orange bar, and how uncertain is it?

| Parent selection | Reported test score | Reported confidence interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Fixed random selection | 0.428 | 0.407–0.432 |
| Start at random and allow edits to the selection rule | 0.491 | 0.387–0.512 |
| Fixed rule devised by the researchers | 0.532 | 0.384–0.586 |

The table combines two kinds of aggregation. The score for one run is the <strong>mean of its two domain scores</strong>. The representative score for each method is the <strong>median across runs</strong>. The authors obtained 95% bootstrap confidence intervals by resampling the five runs 1,000 times. The following subtraction compares the reported medians. [Statistical protocol](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=7) · [Figure 11 and reported scores](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.19461v1#page=57)

<Math tex={String.raw`0.491-0.428=0.063,\qquad 0.532-0.491=0.041`} display />

The initial agent scored 0.030. Measured from that starting point, the median in the orange condition rose by 0.461. To ask how much of that gain is associated with <strong>permission to edit the selection rule</strong>, compare it with the condition that keeps improving candidates while continuing to choose their parents at random.

<Math tex={String.raw`(0.491-0.030)-(0.428-0.030)=0.063`} display />

The observed difference is 0.063 score units. Because the score combines both domains, it cannot tell us how much the robot’s success rate changed. Across five runs, the authors found no statistically significant difference between conditions (p > 0.05). The blue bar had the largest median in this experiment. The five runs leave uncertainty about the ranking in future experiments.

### Worked example 6. Why can a high final score still give the editor a negative reward?

Consider <strong>a hypothetical edit</strong> to an instruction for reading part numbers in inspection images. Within each row, score both versions on the same 20 images, awarding one point for each correct part number. Starting instruction A goes from 16 correct to 15. Starting instruction B goes from 9 to 13.

| Starting instruction | Accuracy before editing | Accuracy after editing | Gain from this edit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 16/20 = 0.80 | 15/20 = 0.75 | −0.05 |
| B | 9/20 = 0.45 | 13/20 = 0.65 | +0.20 |

A delivers the higher final score. The ranking changes when we ask <strong>what the edit accomplished</strong>. The net change in correct predictions is a loss of one for A and a gain of four for B. Equation 7 of the paper denotes the mean reward under context c by R̄(c) and subtracts the pre-edit reward from the post-edit reward. The slide uses loss, where smaller values are better, so the order is reversed. If loss is the error rate, the two expressions agree exactly.

<Math tex={String.raw`L(c)=1-\bar R(c),\qquad L(c_0)-L(c_1)=\bar R(c_1)-\bar R(c_0)`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`r_A=(1-0.80)-(1-0.75)=-0.05,\quad r_B=(1-0.45)-(1-0.65)=0.20`} display />

If we score an edit on easier images than its starting instruction, the measured difference also picks up the change in difficulty. The paper compares contexts on a fixed holdout set separate from the batches that supply feedback each round. Repeatedly using that holdout to select candidates also gives its score a different interpretation from a one-time evaluation on fresh data. [LSE, Equation 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18620v1#page=4) · [Equation 7 and evaluation procedure](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18620v1#page=5)

### Worked example 7. How does that reward change the chance of proposing the same edit again?

Suppose the policy can propose either of two edits, A and B, from the <strong>same starting context</strong>. This calculation is independent of the preceding table. A raises accuracy by 0.15 and B lowers it by 0.05. With probability p of proposing A and probability 1−p of proposing B, the expected gain J for one edit is

<Math tex={String.raw`J=p(0.15)+(1-p)(-0.05)=0.20p-0.05`} display />

Let z be the difference between the policy’s two logits, its preference scores before normalization, and compute p with a sigmoid. When z is zero, the two edits are equally likely. The gradient at that point is 0.05. Take one step with a learning rate of 0.4 in the direction that <strong>increases</strong> expected gain.

<Math tex={String.raw`p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\qquad \frac{dJ}{dz}=0.20p(1-p)=0.05\quad(p=0.5)`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`z_{\mathrm{new}}=0+0.4(0.05)=0.02,\qquad p_{\mathrm{new}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-0.02}}\approx0.5050`} display />

The chance of proposing A rose from 50% to about 50.5%. That 50.5% measures <strong>the probability of choosing a particular edit</strong>. Part-number recognition accuracy measures the performance of the resulting instruction. In Equations 9–10, the authors sample an actual text edit and weight the gradient of its log probability by the measured improvement. With just two possible edits in our example, we could differentiate the expected gain directly. [LSE, Equations 9–10](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.18620v1#page=6)
