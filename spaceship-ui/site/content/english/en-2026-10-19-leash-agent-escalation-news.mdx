## In one sentence

LEASH uses internal representations to decide when a smaller model should ask a larger one for help. Its authors evaluated it on OSWorld in a preprint. It needs no newly trained monitoring model, but it does require reference data and calibration.

## Why the distinction matters

As a computer-use task grows longer, small mistakes can accumulate. Calling a strong API model throughout is expensive, while relying on a small open model can increase failures. LEASH bases escalation on how closely the weak model’s representation resembles those seen in previous failures. After handoff, the weak model stays on as a shadow observer of the stronger black-box agent.

## Interpreting the score

A hidden state is a vector of numbers produced as a model processes its input and action history. LEASH builds separate reference groups from past successful and failed runs. The following notation simplifies equations 2–3 in §3.2. The original channel subscript c is omitted here. The implementation uses separate reference data for the initial query and for actions during execution.

## What does the theory guarantee?

The proposition in §5 covers a single handoff decision at the start of a task. It assumes Gaussian representations for the success and failure groups, common covariance, and an exact handoff fraction. Optimizing overall success also requires the benefit of handoff to be nondecreasing with the score. If both models find a task difficult, the weaker model may face high failure risk while handoff offers little benefit. The proposition does not establish optimality for the entire runtime and shadow system.

The calibration split matters too. Appendices A.2–A.3 use cross-validation that keeps all steps from one task in the same group. Putting similar steps from one task into both training and validation could overestimate generalization to a new task. Reusing hidden states still requires this separation.

## Results and comparison conditions

The evaluation used 262 OSWorld tasks. The method requires a separate set of successful and failed reference trajectories and threshold calibration. The shadow threshold used data from 99 tasks separate from the evaluation tasks. The weak model needs a history against which to compare a current representation.

In LEASH, Claude Opus 5 also acts as a Manager that plans and checks subgoals. The always-Opus baseline lacks this Manager, so the comparison is between system configurations. Costs estimate token use at OpenRouter rates and include the Manager, weak and strong agents, and shadow calls. They are estimates rather than invoices or measurements of local operating costs.

The 6.55–11.94 percentage-point gains are relative to random escalation at matched rates. The 73.66% and 72.90% values are OSWorld success rates for specific Qwen and Claude configurations. They do not establish general equivalence. The $0.4117 per task and 19.2% savings also depend on the paper’s prices, calls and definition of a task.

## Who acts after the monitor raises a concern?

In Appendices A.4 and B.1, the small model receives a shadow call every three execution steps of the large model. Its proposed action is never executed. The system computes a score from the hidden state and, when appropriate, sends the large model a neutral message asking it to reconsider its strategy. There is no vote between two actions, and the smaller model cannot impose a correction.

A task can receive up to six interventions. Once two interventions have changed the large model’s action, further interventions stop. Oversight has a finite budget and cannot intercept every error.

Runtime AUROC measures discrimination between success and failure for subgoal segments labeled by the Manager. It does not estimate the probability that an individual mouse click is correct.

**Check your understanding.** Does the system execute the small model’s alternative action as soon as it raises a concern? No. The larger model retains control and reconsiders its decision.

## Limitations and counterevidence

### Matching call rates and matching cost answer different questions

For the Qwen configuration in Table 1, random escalation handed off 64.4% of tasks, achieved 61.72% success and cost an estimated $0.3768 per task. LEASH handed off 64.1%, achieved 73.66% and cost $0.4117. Success improved at a similar handoff rate, but cost rose too. The earlier 19.2% cost saving uses a different comparator, the always-strong model.

The paper says runtime scoring reuses hidden states from ordinary action generation. The full monitoring system still incurs costs. Initial decisions and post-handoff monitoring require small-model calls. Interventions can prompt extra computation by the strong model (Appendix B.4). To interpret the cost comparison, check which of task count, handoff rate and model prices are held fixed.

**Check your understanding.** If two systems call the large model for the same proportion of tasks, must their costs match? No. Call counts, input and output lengths, and computation for management, monitoring and reconsideration may differ. Task handoff rate and total token cost are separate metrics.

In the component ablation, adding shadow oversight to one Qwen configuration changed success from 67.94% to 73.66% and estimated cost from $0.4050 to $0.4117 per task. These results describe that setting. Requiring access to the weaker model’s hidden states limits direct use with two closed models. A representation associated with failure in OSWorld may have another meaning across interfaces, languages or permission settings. A complete equation-to-figure crosswalk, independent reproduction and operational validation remain open.
