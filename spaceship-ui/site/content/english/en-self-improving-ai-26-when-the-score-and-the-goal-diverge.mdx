In [Chapter 25](/en/posts/self-improving-ai-25-memory-and-intrinsic-motivation/), an inspection robot had to choose between a familiar shelf and one it had never seen. Now imagine checking two robots’ logs at the end of a shift. Robot A raised 8 alerts. Robot B raised 12. When you match the alerts to part numbers, you find that A detected 8 defective parts and B detected 6. **One robot raised more alerts. The other found more defects.** If the software choosing tomorrow’s robot reads only the alert count, which robot will it keep?

The last four slides in the NTU handout make this question progressively harder. Page 61 asks which loss went down. Pages 62–63 ask how long a once-informative signal remains reliable when selection favors larger and larger values. Page 64 considers what happens when people themselves struggle to state the outcome they want in a single rule. The recording then closes with a diagram showing where people supply a motivation and where they monitor the changes that follow. The numbers in the inspection, peacock, and welfare exercises are hypothetical. The game experiments and peafowl field studies are discussed alongside the relevant figures.

Read the diagram **from the bottom to the top, then to the right**. The lecturer uses **L̂**, at the bottom, for the loss corresponding to the outcome people really want. Here the hat has a specific meaning. Reading it as a sign that this is the estimated proxy would reverse the roles of the two losses. Awarding a point for every alert gives the robot an incentive to flag the same part twice. [1:03:34, how the lecturer distinguishes L̂ from H](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cQLKVzbwN7I&t=3814s)

The returning arrow feeds them into the next round. Notice the question mark beside the derivative with respect to the harness. A discrete edit to a prompt or a piece of code can be evaluated by running each candidate and comparing the results. Either way, someone must decide **how those candidates will be scored**. ### Worked example 1. How can 12 alerts add up to just 6 defective parts?

Give both robots the same batch containing 10 defective parts. Suppose every alert points to a real defect, but a robot can flag a given part more than once. An operator removes duplicates by part number and obtains the following results.

| Robot | Alerts raised | Distinct defective parts found | Defective parts missed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 8 | 8 | 2 |
| B | 12 | 6 | 4 |

<Math tex={String.raw`S_A=8,\quad S_B=12,\qquad (S_B-S_A)/S_A=(12-8)/8=0.50.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`R_A=8/10=0.80,\quad R_B=6/10=0.60,\qquad R_B-R_A=-0.20.`} display />


S is the number of alerts. R is recall, the fraction of all defective parts that the robot found. Switching from A to B raises the alert count by **50%** and lowers recall by **20 percentage points**. If the evaluation loss is the fraction missed, then 1−R rises from 0.20 to 0.40. A log that records only the number 12 cannot tell a duplicate alert from a new discovery. The two changes also have different denominators: **8 previous alerts** for the relative increase, and **10 actual defective parts** for each recall calculation.

### Worked example 2. Why can a correct gradient step raise the loss people care about?

Suppose the upper loss reaches its minimum at θ=2, while the lower loss reaches its minimum at θ=1. Both minimum loss values are zero, but the parameter values that achieve them differ. Start at 1.2 and check what a single update does to each loss.

<Math tex={String.raw`L(\theta,H)=(\theta-2)^2,\qquad \widehat L(\theta)=(\theta-1)^2.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`L'(1.2)=2(1.2-2)=-1.6,\qquad \theta'=1.2-0.25(-1.6)=1.6.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`L:\ 0.64\longrightarrow0.16,\qquad \widehat L:\ 0.04\longrightarrow0.36.`} display />


The upper loss has a negative slope here. Multiplying the slope by a learning rate of 0.25 and subtracting the result moves θ to the right. It ends up closer to 2, so the training log records a success. But θ was already to the right of 1. It has now taken another step away from the minimum of the loss people care about. Reducing the learning rate would shorten the step. The two gradients would still point in opposite directions at the starting point. **Choosing how large a step to take and choosing which function to minimize are separate decisions.**

In OpenAI’s 2016 *CoastRunners* experiment, an agent repeatedly hit three targets as they reappeared. The team reported that it beat human players by 20% on average **in game score**. Reading that as a 20% improvement in race performance would repeat the mistake of counting alerts as discoveries. [The original experiment and footage of the boat](https://openai.com/index/faulty-reward-functions/)

### Worked example 3. Can an agent get exactly the right reward and still run past the coin?

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-26-when-the-score-and-the-goal-diverge/coinrun-fixed-and-random.png"
  alt="In the game screenshot on the left, the coin sits at the end of the level. The test screenshots on the right show a coin in a different location."
  caption="CoinRun, Figure 1. Separating the coin from the right edge makes the two possible behaviors distinguishable."
  source="https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/langosco22a/langosco22a.pdf#page=1"
  credit="Langosco et al. / ICML 2022 · CC BY 4.0 · cropped"
  label="Further reading"
  width={1210} height={465}
/>

In the *CoinRun* experiment by Langosco and colleagues (ICML 2022), collecting the coin earned 10 points. Everything else earned zero. During training, the coin was always at the far right. Clearing obstacles and running to the end therefore collected the coin as well. At test time, the researchers also placed it in other accessible locations. The trained agent often navigated the obstacles competently, passed the coin, and reached the end. In the boat game, **the reward was misspecified**. In the coin game, the reward still paid for the intended outcome, but the agent showed **goal misgeneralization**. [Paper, Section 3.1](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/langosco22a/langosco22a.pdf#page=4) · [The researchers’ test videos](https://sites.google.com/view/goal-misgeneralization/home/coinrun-videos)

For a pencil-and-paper version, assume both policies can navigate the level. Put the off-route coin on a side path that the “run to the end” policy cannot visit by accident. If q is the probability that the coin is at the end, we can calculate the expected score per episode for each policy.

<Math tex={String.raw`E[R_{\rm coin}]=10,\qquad E[R_{\rm end}]=10q+0(1-q)=10q.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`q=1:\ (10,10),\qquad q=0.3:\ (10,3).`} display />


The training scores at q=1 give us no way to tell the rules apart. At q=0.3, a seven-point gap appears. We chose 0.3 for the exercise. In the actual game, an agent can stumble onto a coin by chance, so the researchers also measured that baseline.

In the authors’ ICML talk, first compare where the coin appears during training and testing. Then listen for the evidence that navigation still works: the agent gets past the obstacles and reaches the end after missing the coin. A score of zero alone cannot distinguish a navigation failure from a failure to pursue the intended goal. [Author talk at 2:42, coin placement](https://slideslive.com/38983609/goal-misgeneralization-in-deep-reinforcement-learning?time=162s) · [3:02, reaching the end without the coin](https://slideslive.com/38983609/goal-misgeneralization-in-deep-reinforcement-learning?time=182s)

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-26-when-the-score-and-the-goal-diverge/coinrun-diversity.png"
  alt="The horizontal axis shows the share of training levels with randomized coin positions. The vertical axis shows failure frequency, with separate curves for goal misgeneralization and capability failure."
  caption="CoinRun, Figure 2. The horizontal axis describes the training data. The vertical axis reports evaluation outcomes."
  source="https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/langosco22a/langosco22a.pdf#page=4"
  credit="Langosco et al. / ICML 2022 · CC BY 4.0 · cropped"

  width={1238} height={622}

In Figure 2, **2% belongs on the horizontal axis**. The authors report that randomizing the coin position in just 2% of training levels substantially improved goal generalization in this experiment. Blue shows how often the agent died or got stuck in training-distribution environments, where the coin was usually at the end. Orange shows how often it missed the coin and reached the end in test environments with randomized coin positions. Since the curves come from different evaluation conditions, adding their values would not give an overall failure rate. [Figure 2 and evaluation conditions](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/langosco22a/langosco22a.pdf#page=4) · [License for Figures 1 and 2](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)

### Worked example 4. Can an individual with a lower chance of surviving leave more offspring?

Let W be the expected number of offspring an individual alive today will leave by the next census. Suppose an individual that dies before breeding leaves no offspring. Let s be the probability it survives to breed and b the expected number of offspring conditional on surviving. Averaging over the two outcomes gives:

<Math tex={String.raw`W=s\,b+(1-s)\,0=s\,b.`} display />


| Hypothetical train | Survival probability s | Expected offspring if the individual survives b | Expected offspring per starting individual W |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Short | 0.80 | 2 | 1.60 |
| Medium | 0.60 | 3 | 1.80 |
| Very long | 0.35 | 4 | 1.40 |

<Math tex={String.raw`W_{\rm mid}>W_{\rm short}\iff 0.60b_{\rm mid}>1.60\iff b_{\rm mid}>8/3.`} display />


Individuals with medium trains have a lower chance of survival than those with short trains. Once they survive, however, their expected offspring count of 3 exceeds the threshold of about 2.67. Individuals with very long trains have the highest conditional offspring count, yet their overall expectation is only 1.40. Because b is already conditional on survival, the calculation needs no independence assumption. This one-generation model illustrates the trade-off in the slide.

In a field study, it matters which feature of the train the researchers measured and in which population. Petrie and colleagues (1991) observed 10 males at a single display site and reported an association between train elaboration and mate choice. Takahashi and colleagues (2008) observed a Japanese population of Indian peafowl for seven years. They found no evidence of a female preference based on eyespot number, symmetry of arrangement, or train length. Both studies investigated mate choice. Neither supplies the survival rates in our table or an estimate of predation risk per extra centimeter of train. [1991 study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347205804841) · [2008 study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347207005301)

### Worked example 5. If the indicator rises from 1 to 3, does the outcome keep improving?

Here is a way to see what “within range” means. Let x denote the strength of a trait on a scale with no units. The selection score P rises with x. The outcome Q also includes a cost that grows as the trait becomes more pronounced. Q is a made-up score for this illustration and was never fitted to peacock survival data.

<Math tex={String.raw`P(x)=x,\qquad Q(x)=4x-x^2=4-(x-2)^2,\qquad 0\leq x\leq 4.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`(P,Q)(1)=(1,3),\qquad (P,Q)(2)=(2,4),\qquad (P,Q)(3)=(3,3).`} display />


From 1 to 2, both scores gain a point. From 2 to 3, the selection score gains another point while the outcome loses one. Completing the square shows that Q peaks at x=2. Its derivative Q′(x)=4−2x changes sign there as well. The first two observations tell us **how the scores moved over that interval**. They cannot establish what happens at the third point. Think back to the inspection robot: when alerts are rare, one more alert might mean one more defect found. Once the robot can repeatedly flag the same part, that relationship needs a fresh measurement.

### Worked example 6. Do total welfare and the lowest individual score favor the same plan?

<LearningFigure
  src="/assets/posts/self-improving-ai-26-when-the-score-and-the-goal-diverge/welfare-two-rules.png"
  alt="Two people score 9 and 1 under A, and 4 and 4 under B. A has the higher sum. B has the higher minimum."
  caption="Changing how we combine the same four outcomes reverses the preferred plan."
  source="https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs186/internal/lectures/alignment_1.html"
  credit="JJo-0 · worked example based on the CS186 discussion"
  label="Further reading"
  width={1440} height={780}
/>

A Stanford CS186 class exercise gives teams proposed rules, including maximizing total welfare and improving the welfare of the worst-off individual. A red team looks for an undesirable outcome that still satisfies a rule. A blue team revises the rule to address that case. For our own two-plan comparison, suppose we can express two people’s welfare **on a shared scale that allows scores to be compared and added**. Higher values are better. [Discussion exercise in the CS186 lecture notes](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs186/internal/lectures/alignment_1.html)

<Math tex={String.raw`W_{\rm sum}(A)=9+1=10,\qquad W_{\rm sum}(B)=4+4=8.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`W_{\rm min}(A)=\min(9,1)=1,\qquad W_{\rm min}(B)=\min(4,4)=4.`} display />


Maximizing the sum selects A. A **maximin** rule maximizes the lowest individual score and selects B. Both maximizations are mathematically correct. The difference lies in **how each rule combines the outcomes for the people affected**. Any real application would first have to examine whether individual welfare can be observed and placed on a common scale. These equations illustrate the choice of rule. They do not identify a welfare function from the film or settle which rule is ethically preferable.

### Worked example 7. What should a person count when checking the next version?

Suppose the monitor checks our inspection robots on **a separate set of 20 defective parts** whose defect locations have been independently confirmed. Run both the old and revised versions under the same conditions. Pairing the two observations on each part lets the monitor count newly detected and newly missed defects separately.

| Outcome on the same defective part | Parts |
|---|---:|
| Both versions found it | 10 |
| Only the old version found it | 5 |
| Only the revised version found it | 2 |
| Both versions missed it | 3 |

<Math tex={String.raw`R_{\rm old}=(10+5)/20=0.75,\qquad R_{\rm new}=(10+2)/20=0.60.`} display />

<Math tex={String.raw`\Delta R=(2-5)/20=-0.15.`} display />


Why do only 2 and 5 remain in the last line? The 10 shared detections cancel in the subtraction. The 3 shared misses never enter either numerator. The revised robot gained 2 detections and lost 5, so recall fell by 15 percentage points on this sample. This calculation leaves open the false alarm rate on sound parts, performance under different lighting, and expected recall across the full production line. If the team studies the missed parts and repeatedly uses them to revise the robot, those parts become development cases. The next version can be checked on a separate set kept aside for that purpose.

[Chapter 1](/en/posts/self-improving-ai-01-overview/) began with “What improves what?” The loss functions in [Chapter 2](/en/posts/self-improving-ai-02-learning-targets/) let us write down a criterion, and the revisions in [Chapter 3](/en/posts/self-improving-ai-03-self-correction-to-rl/) let us change an answer or update weights. After Chapter 26, we can return to the inspection log with three distinct questions: **Which loss fell? Which defective parts did the revised robot newly detect? Which did it start missing?** The word “improvement” in a log can hide three very different answers.
