This study evaluates genetic associations. It did not validate a clinical tool for predicting an individual’s risk.

## In one sentence

A multi-ancestry GWAS with 237,817 cases and 1,679,059 controls, together with a TWAS using expression models trained on European-ancestry data, identified 415 breast cancer risk loci and 196 candidate genes. More discoveries do not in themselves improve prediction for an individual patient. The population used to train each expression model and the population used to test association play different roles.

## Start with variants, loci and genes

A particular position in DNA can carry different bases in different people. This difference is a genetic variant. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) compares variant frequencies and estimates associations with disease in cases and controls. This study pooled summary statistics from several studies. Its 1.9 million participants were not all newly recruited and tested with a single instrument.

The photograph above shows a physical model of DNA that helps explain molecular structure. In a GWAS plot, each dot represents a statistical test at a genomic position. Nearby variants can be inherited together and produce several signals in the same region. A dot, a risk locus and a gene therefore need not correspond one to one. A locus groups association signals within a region of the genome. Identifying which variant acts through which gene is a further question.

Association alone cannot establish that changing a variant would prevent disease. A variant may indirectly tag a neighboring causal variant. This distinction matters when relating the discovery count to potential treatment targets or clinical prediction accuracy.

## Why ancestry and the type of analysis matter

Earlier breast cancer genomic research heavily represented European-ancestry samples, leaving less precise estimates of effects and association patterns in other populations. This GWAS harmonized African-, Asian- and European-ancestry summary statistics and used fixed-effect meta-analysis within and across ancestry groups. The transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) used expression predictors trained on European-ancestry data. The population covered varies by analysis, including analyses of overall breast cancer and ER-positive, ER-negative and triple-negative disease.

### Training an expression model and testing for association

Page 3 of the accepted Article in Press PDF says that normal breast tissue from 115 women of European ancestry was used to construct the expression prediction models because adequate non-European training data were unavailable. The same paragraph says ancestry-specific and multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis summary statistics were used for association analyses.

The training population and the population represented in the disease statistics are separate questions. The limitations paragraph in the accepted Discussion describes TWAS as restricted to people of European ancestry. The Methods use broader language about ancestry-specific and multi-ancestry GWAS summary statistics. Resolving which description applies to each result requires further comparison with the supplementary tables and result-specific analyses. This article therefore distinguishes the **multi-ancestry GWAS from a TWAS described as European-ancestry restricted**. It makes no claim that non-European TWAS or group-specific expression prediction accuracy was validated. The accepted manuscript precedes the final version.

### Even with 1.9 million participants, information differs by group

For overall breast cancer, the Results list 176,909 cases and 1,226,137 controls of European ancestry, 39,204 and 302,914 of Asian ancestry, and 21,704 and 150,008 of African ancestry. These sum to 237,817 cases and 1,679,059 controls. The scale of the combined sample does not imply equal precision across groups or justify applying a risk score trained mainly in European-ancestry data directly to another group. The ancestry labels follow the paper’s genetic analysis categories. They do not define individual identity or fixed biological types.

### How are effects pooled across studies?

The accepted Methods and Figure 2 legend specify inverse-variance-weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis using METAL. The equation below restates that standard method for the reader.

<CandidateEquation lang="en" id="gwas-inverse-variance-fixed-effects" title="How do we combine effect estimates with different levels of precision?" tex={String.raw`\widehat\beta=\frac{\sum_i w_i\widehat\beta_i}{\sum_i w_i},\qquad w_i=\frac{1}{\mathrm{SE}_i^2}`} >

Here i indexes studies, β̂i is a study’s estimated effect for the variant, and SEi is its standard error. The weight wi gives more influence to estimates with smaller uncertainty. If effects use log odds ratios, they must be combined on that scale. Units, effect alleles and reference directions must agree. The calculation requires valid positive standard errors.

A fixed-effect model estimates a common effect. If effects vary with ancestry, study design or measurement, a pooled value cannot represent all the differences. The short equation also cannot resolve overlapping samples treated as independent inputs. This is why the paper’s heterogeneity tests and harmonization procedures need separate attention. The standard equation is background for reading the analysis.

## Evidence and principal results

Suppose two studies estimate effects of 0.10 and 0.20 on the same scale with standard errors of 0.10 and 0.20. Their weights are 100 and 25. The pooled effect is (100 × 0.10 + 25 × 0.20)/(100 + 25) = 0.12. It lies closer to the more precise first study than the unweighted average of 0.15. These are invented numbers for a worked example.

If the second study’s standard error halves, does its weight double? Inverse variance makes the weight four times larger. Can a high Manhattan-plot point establish a large effect or high personal risk? Its height transforms a P value. To interpret it, read the effect estimate, standard error and testing conditions as well.

The abstract reports 415 risk loci and 196 associated genes. Of these, 207 loci and 81 genes had not previously been reported. The ER-positive analysis found 198 loci and 135 genes, and the ER-negative analysis 69 loci and 35 genes. The triple-negative analysis reported 22 loci and eight genes, including 11 newly reported loci and four newly reported genes. Associations at 230 loci and 132 genes reportedly differed by ER status.

## What is a high point on a Manhattan plot?

The horizontal axis of accepted Figure 2 shows variant positions along the chromosomes. The vertical axis is −log₁₀(P), so smaller P values produce higher points. Height conveys evidence in an association test. It does not give an effect size or a carrier’s probability of developing breast cancer. The dashed red line marks the genome-wide threshold of P below 5 × 10⁻⁸. A variant above that line still requires causal investigation.

The upper panel includes all signals. The lower panel excludes previously known associated regions. Their vertical scales differ, so peaks of similar apparent height can have different P values. Nearby inherited variants can cluster in one region. Counting points would therefore give a different number from counting independent risk loci.

These P values came from a fixed-effect meta-analysis that weights variant effect estimates by precision. The assumptions and tests for between-group differences also matter. The authors evaluated heterogeneity with Cochran’s Q and I². These tests do not measure clinical prediction performance within each ancestry group (accepted PDF, Methods p. 3 and Fig. 2 p. 4).

## What questions do GWAS and TWAS ask?

GWAS tests for statistical association between variants and disease. A transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) asks whether genetically predicted gene expression is associated with disease. Expression refers to the amount of RNA produced from a gene. Here, expression predictors were trained using normal breast tissue. Expression was not directly measured in breast tissue from all 1.9 million participants.

Because predicted expression is a model output, the training population and tissue matter. Good prediction in European-ancestry training data cannot guarantee equal accuracy in other groups. An association between predicted expression and disease also leaves open whether expression itself is causal. Nearby correlations among variants and signals affecting several genes can contribute.

This is why the wording difference between the Methods and Discussion is worth retaining. Reporting which populations contributed to GWAS, which trained the expression model and which entered each association test makes the results interpretable.

## Limitations and counterevidence

### Is 415 the sum of the discoveries in every analysis?

The accepted Results report 359 loci for overall breast cancer: 268 from the combined three-ancestry analysis and 91 from analyses of one or two groups. Some subtype-specific loci overlap those from the overall analysis. Adding 359 to counts for ER-positive, ER-negative and triple-negative disease can count the same region several times. The 415 total and each analysis-specific count cover different sets.

A “novel locus” also has a specific definition. The Methods require the lead variant to lie more than 500 kb from a previously reported signal and to show low linkage disequilibrium with nearby known signals (r² &lt; 0.2) in reference data for the comparison ancestry group. A kilobase is 1,000 DNA bases. Linkage disequilibrium describes the statistical tendency for variants to occur together. Meeting these selection criteria does not establish an independent biological cause or therapeutic target.

Sample overlap, phenotype definitions, ancestry-specific linkage disequilibrium and measurement differences can affect a summary-statistics meta-analysis. A fixed-effect model can simplify heterogeneity. The identity of a causal variant or effector gene still requires follow-up. A TWAS association alone cannot prove that expression causes disease. Quantifying gains in African- and Asian-ancestry sample sizes and group-specific prediction would require further reading of the full study.

## Code, data and interests

The official article page links a supplementary PDF and Supplemental Data 1–10 as spreadsheets. The accepted data availability statement directs readers to **summary statistics** from several source populations via the GWAS Catalog and other routes. Access terms differ. For example, requesting MGI summary statistics requires submitting a data-use agreement. These routes do not make all individual genomic and clinical records public.

The code availability statement identifies the [GWAS_BC repository](https://github.com/935133632/GWAS_BC). The repository and example below were inspected, but the full code, inputs and results were not independently reproduced. The authors declared no competing interests and listed public funding, including from the NCI.

The public `TWAS.txt` example separately specifies a European-ancestry female breast tissue expression model and covariance file, and GWAS summary statistics named `3race_Overall.tsv.gz`. This supports the distinction between the training population and association-test input. The example was read without running or validating its inputs or output. It does not establish that every subtype or group analysis had the same configuration. It references institutional local paths, which limits immediate reproducibility from the public file alone.

## What would warrant another look?

Detailed examination of ancestry-specific effects, heterogeneity and validation cohorts, independent replication, fine-mapping and functional studies, external clinical validation of polygenic risk scores, confirmation of access to code and summary statistics, or substantive challenges concerning sample overlap.
