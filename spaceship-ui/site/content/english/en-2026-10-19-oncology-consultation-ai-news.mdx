## Conclusion in one sentence

In a small real-world workflow study, clinicians reported a lower burden when preparing for oncology consultations. The finding supports testing efficiency in a controlled study.

## Significance and study design

Medical LLM research often uses test questions with known answers or retrospective chart summaries. AI-FCP assembled scattered documents into a structured summary for a first consultation and delivered it during actual preparation. The study measured NASA-TLX workload, time per case, preparation success and completeness of the summary.

## Results and comparison conditions

After individual baseline assessments, the team evaluated 33 consultations prepared with AI-FCP. Overall workload fell 28.9%, with an estimated absolute difference of −12.3. Cases were not randomized to preparation with or without AI. The accepted paper reports **successful preparation in 28/33 consultations** and **a sufficiently complete summary in 21/33**. A summary can arrive in time and contribute to preparation while still missing information. Six clinicians contributed those 33 assessments. Nine clinicians participated in the overall baseline. The count of 33 refers to consultations, without confirming 33 unique patients.

## Where the workflow failed

### How many consultations contributed to mean time savings?

Two consultations lacked a numerical estimate. The Results therefore report **3.26 minutes saved on average across 31 consultations**. Clinicians reported saving time in 25 of the 33 consultations. Across those 25 alone, the mean saving was 4.39 minutes. Selecting only the positive responses changes the question and the denominator. Both figures come from clinicians' estimates.

The missing-data sensitivity analysis used assumptions about workload in 39 of 72 eligible consultations absent from the analysis. It estimated what assumptions would cause the primary result to lose statistical significance. Workload was not observed in those 39. They included the ten missing surveys among 43 scheduled AI-assisted preparations as well as other consultations outside the analysis. This simulation explores missing-data risk within an uncontrolled design.

### Repeated consultations share the same clinician

The primary workload analysis used **nine participants** as its units. After-intervention scores from multiple consultations were aggregated for each clinician and compared with that person's baseline. Preparation success, completeness, time and trust were analyzed at consultation level. Ratings from the same clinician can share individual scoring habits.

The authors used mixed-effects models with a random intercept for each participant, allowing clinicians to start from different typical ratings. They used linear models for continuous measures and logistic models for binary outcomes. Observed means and proportions describe the sample. The paper separately reports estimates accounting for clustering and their 95% confidence intervals. The small sample and absence of controls still limit inference (accepted Methods, analysis units and aggregation).

In five unsuccessful preparations, a summary was unavailable when needed or omitted documents added shortly before the visit. Clinicians reported four discrepancies of potential clinical relevance. In two allergy cases the source lacked information, yet the final electronic health record (EHR) displayed “no known allergies.” According to the paper, the original model summary said “no information.” The wording changed during postprocessing and EHR integration.

**Safety evaluation must follow a summary through to the screen used in care.** An undocumented allergy status carries a different meaning from a documented absence of known allergies. The other reports included a classification-location error and an instance of correctly extracting information that had been difficult to see in the source. The four reports therefore cover different events. Participant reporting does not establish an audited error rate (accepted Results, safety evaluation and Table 1a–b).

### What did the workload score measure?

Supplementary Note 5 describes a Raw NASA-TLX survey using 0–100 sliders. At baseline, clinicians recalled a recent day representative of their usual workload. Afterward they rated the consultation they had just prepared with AI. Cases and patients were not randomized between the two conditions, limiting causal attribution. A lower score and estimated time savings concern workflow. Diagnostic accuracy and patient benefit require their own endpoints.

**Check your understanding.** Does an accurate summary guarantee an accurate clinical display? **Answer.** The allergy example shows why the final display needs checking. Transmission and postprocessing can change the meaning.

### Did all six dimensions improve significantly?

Overall workload and the six subscales pose separate statistical questions. The Methods applied a Bonferroni correction to reduce chance findings across multiple subscale tests. The reported threshold was α = 0.05/6 ≈ 0.0083. Alpha is a dimensionless significance level, and six is the number of subscales tested. This is the standard correction used in the analysis.

In Figure 2, frustration met the corrected threshold (p = 0.008). Mental demand (p = 0.024) and physical demand (p = 0.04) moved toward lower scores but fell short of that threshold. Evidence of improvement therefore varied across dimensions. An inconclusive test leaves uncertainty about the underlying change, especially in this small sample. Read effect sizes alongside the uncertainty (accepted PDF, Figure 2 on page 3 and Methods on page 9).

## Limitations and counterevidence

Nine clinicians participated. The analysis included 33 completed surveys among 43 consultations scheduled for AI-FCP. Ten were excluded because surveys were missing. The participant flow matters alongside repeated consultations clustered within clinicians. Scheduling and enrollment affected how many cases each clinician contributed, creating potential survivorship bias. The 3.26-minute saving comes from self-report. The 63.6% completeness assessment is an important safety finding alongside the time estimate. Company employees' participation as coauthors also warrants attention.

## Code, data and reasons to revisit

The accepted paper states that the AI-FCP implementation and inference prompts are proprietary. Analysis scripts may be available on reasonable request, subject to Netherlands Cancer Institute approval. Those scripts neither contain patient-level source records nor grant access to them. Privacy restrictions also cover the research data, with deidentified records subject to requests, institutional approval and ethics conditions. Independent reproduction from public code remains constrained.

The Methods state that the team did not systematically review all 33 summaries, so the frequency of discrepancies cannot be estimated by dividing four reports by 33. The study lacked a prespecified formal safety endpoint and a preregistered statistical analysis plan. The system and prompts were frozen before the study and held constant throughout. Full review of the text, figures and equations, confirmation against the final edition, objective time logs and a multicenter controlled study remain outstanding (accepted Methods, Data governance, Data availability and Code availability).
