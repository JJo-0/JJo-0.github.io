Isaiah 43:18–21: Historical-Grammatical and Diachronic–Synchronic Study — Reordering Memory and Forming Community through New Creation

A. Executive Summary

This Old Testament study integrates MT-based exegesis with comparison of 1QIsaᵃ, LXX, ancient Near Eastern texts, and Hebrew morphology and syntax. Its guiding question is how Isaiah 43:18–21 reorders memories of deliverance, enabling the community to recognize and praise YHWH’s new salvation that sustains wilderness life and forms a people. Synchronic literary-canonical analysis is combined with diachronic historical and redaction criticism[1].

The analysis reads a deliberate movement: after recalling the former sea exodus in verses 16–17, the prohibition on remembrance redirects exilic despair toward unprecedented new exodus and re-creation[2]. Former things indicate fixation on a past pattern, while new action changes water from destructive obstacle into a life-sustaining resource, suggesting ecological and cosmic re-creation[1]. Jackals’ and ostriches’ praise extends salvation’s horizon to nonhuman creatures[4], converging with the purpose of the formed covenant people in 43:21. The study draws on classical commentaries associated with Claus Westermann and Brevard S. Childs and redactional work by Ulrich Berges and Jacob Stromberg, then assesses the interpretive distance of modern teaching applications.

B. Textual unit & structure: Comparing boundaries

Establishing the passage’s boundaries within Isaiah 40–55 is the first step in determining interpretive scope. This report compares proposed units and selects a preferred literary frame.

The 43:14–21 model begins with the messenger formula כֹּה־אָמַר יְהוָה גֹּאַלְכֶם קְדוֹשׁ יִשְׂרָאֵל, opening an oracle of Babylon’s judgment and Israel’s deliverance[5]. After verse 21’s positive purpose, 22’s וְלֹא־אֹתִי קָרָאתָ יַעֲקֹב introduces an adversative turn toward Israel’s disobedience in 22–28. Thus 14–21 forms a larger completed oracle.

The 43:16–21 model notes a fresh כֹּה אָמַר יְהוָה in verse 16[2]. The subunit contrasts past sea deliverance in 16–17 with future wilderness passage in 18–21. Water that extinguished Egypt becomes life-giving provision, forming the report’s proposed chiastic theological contrast[1].

Liturgical or teaching selections often isolate 18–21, but the prohibition on former remembrance presupposes the sea deliverance immediately preceding it[1]. The report therefore identifies 18–21 as a transitional conclusion within 16–21’s integrated rhetorical structure rather than an entirely independent utterance.

D. Verse-by-Verse Exegesis

This section closely examines all four verses through Hebrew morphology and syntax.

Verse 18: אַל־תִּזְכְּרוּ רִאשֹׁנוֹת וְקַדְמֹנִיּוֹת אַל־תִּתְבֹּנָנוּ “Don’t remember the former things, and don’t consider the things of old.” (WEB) Two אַל + imperfect prohibitions form parallel commands. After 16–17’s majestic recollection of sea victory and the extinguished chariots, horses, and army, the prohibition creates a major Isaianic paradox[1]. Ri’shonot and qadmoniyyot refer to identity-forming deliverance rather than merely elapsed time. Zakar and bin target fixation on a method that could limit God, rather than forgetting divine power[3]. The command dismantles a theology that confines future salvation to the exodus pattern.

Verse 19: הִנְנִי עֹשֶׂה חֲדָשָׁה עַתָּה תִצְמָח הֲלוֹא תֵדָעוּהָ אָשִׂים בַּמִּדְבָּר דֶּרֶךְ בִּישִׁימוֹן נְהָרוֹת “Behold, I will do a new thing. It springs out now. Don’t you know it? I will even make a way in the wilderness, and rivers in the desert.” (WEB) הִנְנִי עֹשֶׂה combines presentative and active participle, presenting the new thing as imminent and already unfolding rather than uncertain and distant[3]. עַתָּה תִצְמָח portrays irresistible organic life emerging beneath exile’s desolation. הֲלוֹא תֵדָעוּהָ calls a suffering community to perceive the signs of that new action[3]. Wilderness way and desert rivers (1QIsaᵃ: paths) signify both return from Babylon to Jerusalem and cosmic re-creation within chaos and death-marked terrain[8].

Verse 20: תְּכַבְּדֵנִי חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה תַּנִּים וּבְנוֹת יַעֲנָה כִּי־נָתַתִּי בַמִּדְבָּר מַיִם נְהָרוֹת בִּישִׁימוֹן לְהַשְׁקוֹת עַמִּי בְחִירִי “The animals of the field, the jackals and the ostriches, shall honor me, because I give water in the wilderness and rivers in the desert, to give drink to my people, my chosen” (WEB). Salvation extends ecologically beyond humans. Tannim and benot ya’anah elsewhere evoke ruins, judgment, and lament (Isaiah 13:21; 34:13; Micah 1:8)[4]. Under re-creation, they become praising witnesses (kavad). כִּי־נָתַתִּי explains the response: animals also benefit from water, while the care of עַמִּי בְחִירִי remains central[1].

Verse 21: עַם־זוּ יָצַרְתִּי לִי תְּהִלָּתִי יְסַפֵּרוּ “the people which I formed for myself, that they might declare my praise.” (WEB) The relative זו modifies עַם and states the preceding actions’ telos[9]. As a potter shapes clay purposefully (יָצַר), YHWH forms Israel through suffering and liberation for public testimony and worship (תְּהִלָּתִי יְסַפֵּרוּ), beyond passive rescue. Verse 22’s accusation then contrasts divine ideal with failed response.

G. Former Things–New Thing Interpretation Models

Four competing models identify the referents of ri’shonot and chadashah.

  1. The first exodus / ancestral deliverance model: Associated with Westermann, identifies former things with the founding exodus because 16–17 clearly recalls the sea miracle[1]. The new thing is return from Babylon as a second exodus. [Limit] Former things has other meanings elsewhere in Isaiah 40–55.

  2. Fulfilled-prophecy model: In 41:22 and 42:9, former things may denote fulfilled judgment prophecies or earlier oracles[2]. New things then denote newly proclaimed salvation. [Limit] Weaker connection with Chapter 43’s immediate sea-exodus imagery.

  3. Cyrus’s early victories model: Treats conquests of Media and Lydia as former events and Israel’s full liberation as the new thing. [Limit] Inland Persian conquest fits the chariot-and-sea rhetoric poorly.

  4. Rhetorical macro-category: Encompasses YHWH’s past acts from exodus through Babylonian judgment; the new thing exceeds even this total history[3].

Assessment: The source favors the first exodus as the immediate referent, integrated with the broader rhetorical category at book level.

H. Remembering–Not Remembering Tension Analysis

Isaiah 43:16–17 dramatically recalls exodus, then 18 interrupts remembrance. The study reads rhetorical hyperbole and selective recontextualization rather than contradiction. Isaiah 46:8–11 instead commands remembrance to establish YHWH’s sovereignty against idols[14]. The prohibition in 43:18 does not deny saving history; it warns against fixation on a past method.[3]. In the exodus, water obstructs survival and is dried for passage. In return across the Babylon–Judah desert, water is essential life and must be created to sustain the land[1]. Insistence on a parted sea as the only saving pattern would obscure desert rivers. Covenant consistency therefore includes historical innovation and challenges a closed paradigm.

I. Sprouting / Recognition / Temporal-Aspect Analysis

Now-sprouting (עַתָּה תִצְמָח) and the question הֲלוֹא תֵדָעוּהָ express prophetic temporality and recognition.

  • Time and growth imagery: עַתָּה with imperfect תִצְמָח poetically brings future into the present. Sprouting beneath exile’s winter-like desolation depicts organic, irresistible life already beginning.

  • The rhetorical question’s cognitive structure: Yada calls for perception of divine action, not a test of intelligence: both rebuke and encouragement to a suffering people[3]. This is not a claim that positive attitude creates miracles, but a call to perceive God’s incoming salvation as the covenant people.

K. New Exodus vs Re-Creation Models

Two models debate historical return and cosmic renewal as the scope of the new thing.

  1. New exodus: Return from Babylon is a second exodus, supported by wilderness, redemption, highway, and water motifs throughout 40–55. The focus is Israel’s historical political and social liberation[1].

  2. Re-creation / re-Edenizing: Yatsar and watered wilderness evoke creation language associated with Genesis 1–2. Eden-like transformation extends beyond return into renewed cosmic order[2].

The models are complementary: redeeming Israel also renews a devastated world. New exodus and re-creation intersect as salvation history moves toward restored creation.

L. Nonhuman Creatures / Animal Translation / Praise Analysis

Creaturely response in verse 20 is a theological high point. MT tannim is rendered jackals or related wild canids, while benot ya’anah is commonly associated with ostriches[4]. LXX uses σειρῆνες καὶ θυγατέρες στρουθῶν. Ancient creature names do not map precisely onto modern taxonomy, but the image concerns wild inhabitants of desolation. Isaiah 13:21; 34:13 and Micah 1:8 associate them with ruins, judgment, and lament[4]. Here, desert rivers transform these creatures into worshipers[4]. Humans are not the sole beneficiaries: other creatures share life-giving provision and cosmic praise[1].

N. ANE Historical Comparanda

Isaiah’s language resembles and subverts ancient imperial discourse. Sennacherib and Nebuchadnezzar II advertise roads through difficult terrain, canals, irrigation, and royal provision. The Cyrus Cylinder presents Marduk’s chosen ruler restoring order. Direct literary borrowing need not be assumed, but Isaiah uses a shared repertoire of roads and waterworks to challenge imperial claims: ultimate life-giving kingship belongs to YHWH rather than Marduk or Cyrus.

O. Redaction / Composition Models

Four competing models explain formation and integration into Isaiah.

  1. Deutero-Isaiah core model: Associated with Claus Westermann, treats 43:16–21 as a unified exilic salvation oracle, whose sea–desert contrast reflects a single prophet’s rhetorical skill rather than extensive later editing.

  2. Tradition-history and progressive expansion: Proposes initially separate exodus, wilderness, and creation traditions combined during exile. A short promise of safe return may have acquired creaturely praise and formation language through later theological expansion.

  3. Trito-Isaiah / eschatological expansion: The source associates Odil Hannes Steck, Ulrich Berges, and Jacob Stromberg with post-return reinterpretation in Isaiah 56–66. Isaiah 65:17’s new heavens and earth reuses 43:18–19, expanding historical return into cosmic eschatology[10]. Former things then becomes redefined in relation to former troubles rather than past deliverance[15].

  4. Canonical-shape model: Associated with Brevard S. Childs and Christopher R. Seitz; attends to the sixty-six-chapter final form while retaining awareness of historical development[16]. Remember / do-not-remember tension intentionally links earlier judgment with later glory rather than merely reflecting authorial error or editorial fragmentation.

R. Conclusion

This study integrates past and future, memory and forgetting, desolation and life. Verses 16–17 recall YHWH’s sea deliverance to an exilic community, while verse 18 prevents even that majestic pattern from confining the living God[3]. The new thing is more than safe transit: desert rivers produce life and ecological re-creation exceeding the former exodus[2]. Even creatures associated with ruins and judgment become worshipers[1], while verse 21 gives the chosen and formed people a priestly vocation to proclaim divine glory throughout the world[9]. This is neither advice to forget hurts nor positive-thinking technique. Amid imperial oppression and death-marked space, divine life sprouts (tsamach) and forms Israel as a witness community within cosmic praise. Contemporary application should reorient individual and church vocation within that covenantal and eschatological horizon.
