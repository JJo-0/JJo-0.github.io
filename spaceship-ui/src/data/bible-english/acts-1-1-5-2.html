✦ Assessment: Independent prologue or part of the first scene?

Acts 1:1–5 functions as an **independent prologue** while grounding the larger opening scene in 1:1–11. At 1:6, οἱ μὲν οὖν συνελθόντες: When they had gathered, introduces the disciples’ active question and a new discourse movement. The source therefore treats 1:1–5, combining narration and Jesus’ speech, as a complete introduction creating tension toward Pentecost.

Examining ὧν ἤρξατο ὁ Ἰησοῦς ποιεῖν τε καὶ διδάσκειν: All Jesus began to do and teach

The source reads ἤρξατο as suggesting ongoing narrative activity: Jesus remains Lord and agent throughout Acts. The claim should rest on later appearances/actions too, rather than this verb alone.

Direct appearances and sovereign actions in Acts
  • Acts 7:55–56: Stephen’s martyrdom Stephen sees Jesus standing at God’s right hand.
  • Acts 9:4–6 / 22:8: Damascus road Jesus identifies himself to Saul and commissions him.
  • Acts 18:9–10: Corinthian vision Jesus tells Paul not to fear, to speak, and assures him of his presence.
  • Acts 23:11: Jerusalem imprisonment Paul is encouraged that he must testify in Rome as he has in Jerusalem.
Scholarly interpretations: C. Kavin Rowe and Mikeal Parsons

The source associates Rowe with Jesus’ continuing government of the apostolic community through Spirit, visions, and name, rather than withdrawal into heaven. Parsons is presented as reading ascension as worldwide sovereignty rather than absence of power.

✦ Forty days as literary interval

Like Moses receiving revelation, Elijah traveling to Horeb, and Jesus’ wilderness testing, the apostolic forty days are read as a period of formation: former fugitives become eschatological witnesses through leadership succession, in the interpretation attributed to Daniel Marguerat.

The narrative paradox

A narrative of worldwide expansion begins not with movement but with **waiting in Jerusalem**. This **narrative brake** restrains human haste in the hostile place of crucifixion, emphasizing that mission starts through **divine initiative**, not human planning.

🕊️ The narrative effect of the silent Spirit

In 1:1–5 the Spirit does not speak directly, appearing as the medium of Jesus’ command and the promised baptism. The delay is not absence: the litotes of not many days heightens **suspense toward Pentecost’s coming presence**.

7. Literary/theological synthesis and references (O/P)

A synthesis of Acts 1:1–5 research and bibliography presented in SBL second-edition style.

🎓 Literary/theological synthesis

Acts 1:1–5 does more than recap an earlier work: it reworks Luke’s ending into a literary hinge opening Acts’ expanding world. Its five verses compress Jesus’ continuing rule, the coming Spirit, Jerusalem’s salvation-historical tension, and the Kingdom vision. Ancient secondary-preface conventions lead rapidly into narrative. Waiting in Jerusalem restrains human initiative and emphasizes divine action. Water/Spirit contrast and the promise of not many days create suspense toward Pentecost. The forty-day interval combines resurrection evidence and Kingdom instruction, preparing former fugitives to become witnesses. The opening thus moves from retrospect on what Jesus began to expectation of what the ascended Jesus continues through the Spirit and community. This is the original’s literary/theological interpretation.
