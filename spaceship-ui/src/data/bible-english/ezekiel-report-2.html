2. Ezekiel 2:1–3:11: Literary, Rhetorical, Narrative, and Embodiment Criticism — Scroll Eating and Prophetic Identity

Ezekiel’s call offers an unusually complex narrative of literary form, rhetoric, and embodiment. This report reads 2:1–3:11 as the formation of prophetic identity: a fragile human is raised by the Spirit, physically internalizes an external scroll, and becomes a durable mediator facing resistant hearers. It combines Robert Alter’s type-scene theory, narrative viewpoints associated with Meir Sternberg and Adele Berlin, Ellen F. Davis’s work on textuality, and Michael A. Lyons’s bodily rhetoric to trace the passage’s literary world.

A. Literary unit boundary models

The boundaries between Ezekiel’s inaugural vision and call have long been debated. The source associates Baruch J. Schwartz and Hei Yin Yip with distinguishing early call material from later expansion[1]. This report compares boundary proposals from the perspective of the final literary form.

2:1–3:11

The report’s preferred literary unit. Consistent speaker, framing commission formulas, and a scroll-centered structure.

From the Spirit’s raising (2:1–2) through word reception to final sending (3:11), the unit completes a call and identity transformation.

Using a spatial-narrative approach associated with Shimon Bar-Efrat, the study reads 2:1 as vertical movement: ruah raises the human flattened by 1:28b’s glory. Standing enables dialogue between YHWH and Ezekiel. The sending formula in 3:11 completes that dialogue before the transport to Tel-abib at 3:12. Speaker continuity, bodily location, and completed commission favor 2:1–3:11 as a literary unit.

C. בן־אדם: Repetition matrix and literary function

Ezekiel’s personal name disappears within the unit, replaced by בן־אדם at 2:1, 2:3, 2:6, 2:8, 3:1, 3:3, 3:4, and 3:10. The source counts 93 occurrences throughout the book, treating it as a central discourse marker.

First, it enlarges divine–human distance, repeatedly recalling an earth-formed creature’s vulnerability before the glory (כבוד) of 1:28. In Paul M. Joyce’s theocentric reading, the prophet is a finite instrument rather than an autonomous hero. Second, it provides scene segmentation : each address introduces a command and advances the narrative rhythm. Third, it paradoxically forms identity. The name Ezekiel, “God strengthens,” becomes enacted through the strengthened forehead (חזק) in 3:8–9. The initially anonymous human must recognize frailty to bear the divine message, becoming an archetypal messenger facing Israel.

D. Lexical map of hearing and refusal

The study identifies שמע (shama, hear) rather than דבר (speak) as the controlling verb. Through a viewpoint approach associated with Adele Berlin, it traces an ideological contrast between prophet and audience in their receptivity.

  • Israel’s hearing: The whether-they-hear formulas in 2:5 and 2:7 initially leave a conditional opening. By 3:7, לא־יאבו לשמע אליך declares their unwillingness to hear the prophet because they will not hear God.

  • Ezekiel’s hearing: The prophet must hear (שמע) and avoid rebellion in 2:8, then receive all the words in his heart and hear with his ears (באזניך שמע) in 3:10.

This reversal—Israel refuses hearing; the prophet must exemplify it—redefines prophetic qualification. The essential capacity is prior attentive reception rather than eloquence. If the prophet also stops hearing amid communal resistance, divine communication ends. He must therefore model radical receptivity to another’s voice.

E. The word’s route: Word-transfer flowchart

Ezekiel 2–3 depicts divine words moving through space and body in a word-transfer narrative:

YHWH (Origin) → extended hand (Mediation) → scroll (Objectified text) → Ezekiel’s mouth (Ingestion / boundary crossing) → belly / inward parts (Digestion / storage) → heart and ears (Cognitive internalization) → tongue (Utterance) → Israel (Destination, yet resistant)

Ellen F. Davis’s influential study Swallowing the Scrollargues that Ezekiel exhibits a dynamic of textuality distinct from earlier prophets dependent on oral inspiration[3]. Ezekiel is not the message’s producer but the carrier of a fixed scroll, an embodied text absorbed into flesh and blood, and a performer charged with faithfully enacting the received script.

G. From collapse to standing: Bodily location (1:28b–2:2)

The opening changes posture dramatically: Ezekiel falls on his face before YHWH’s glory in 1:28b, is commanded to stand in 2:1, and is raised by the entering Spirit in 2:2.

Prostration and standing visualize the passage from human incapacity to divine empowerment. Silence under overwhelming vision becomes a dialogical position able to hear and receive commission. Ezekiel stands through the Spirit’s physical intervention rather than self-sufficiency.

H. Rebellious-house repetition and narrative tension

The ordinary expression בית ישראל gradually gives way to “rebellious house” (בית מרי, beit meri) or “rebellious,” repeatedly in 2:5, 6, 7, 8 and 3:9.

Through Meir Sternberg’s information-control and reader-conditioning approach, this repetition builds an ideological frame. The source associates Steven S. Tuell with emphasizing its theocentric perspective[9]. Before Israel speaks or acts, divine speech frames it as resistant, limiting readers’ expectation of repentance and supporting judgment. Ethically, this is a rhetorical portrayal of a historical covenant crisis, not a permanent claim of inherent evil. The dark foil highlights prophetic faithfulness.

I. Hearing or refusal: Redefining prophetic success

Repeated three times in 2:5, 2:7, and 3:11, the hearing-or-refusal formula (אם־ישמעו ואם־יחדלו) condenses the call narrative’s rhetorical purpose[10].

Ordinary rhetoric aims to persuade, but this formula makes audience response irrelevant to whether the commission is fulfilled. Success becomes faithful, unaltered transmission rather than transformation of hearers. It frees the prophet from fear of failure while removing the audience’s excuse that it was never warned.

J. Briers, thorns, and scorpions: Sensory performance criticism

The imagery in 2:6 depicts painful hostility and entrapment from resistant hearers rather than a literal natural setting.

In oral performance, naming scorpions evokes threat and pain, leading hearers to imagine the difficulty and danger of the prophet’s task.

K. The paradoxical command to open the mouth (פְּצֵה אֶת־פִּיךָ)

The command in 2:8 leads into eating. Prophetic calls often involve the mouth: Isaiah 6 cleanses lips with a coal, and Jeremiah 1 places words through a divine touch.

Here the opened mouth primarily receives an external object rather than producing speech. The unsettling reversal makes the prophet first a recipient of another’s text rather than an autonomous generator of discourse.

L. Scroll visibility and textual authority

In 2:9–10 a hand extends and unrolls a scroll. Before hearing its message, prophet and reader confront a written physical object densely inscribed on both sides (פנים ואחור).

Writing on a scroll’s reverse leaves no further margin. In the reading associated with Ellen Davis[3], the written object functions as a prop of fixed authority: Ezekiel may not edit it but must absorb it as given.

M. The sounds of lamentation, mourning, and woe (קינים והגה והי)

The three words qinym vahegeh vehi do more than list synonyms: their meanings and sounds accumulate[12].

  • Hebrew phonetics: The sequence of consonants and aspirated h sounds evokes gasping grief or sharp cries through alliteration and rhythm.

  • Three-stage escalation: From formal funerary lament through low groaning to the almost nonverbal cry of woe, the sequence intensifies toward suffering beyond ordinary language.

N. Eating as performance: Crossing text–body boundaries

Ezekiel does more than read and understand the scroll: he eats and incorporates it into his inward body.

The source cites Nili Shupak’s comparison with Egyptian wisdom, including Ptahhotep and the Loyalist Instruction, where eating or drinking instruction makes it central to life and character[13]. Ingestion crosses the text–body boundary. The external scroll seems destroyed but is preserved within the prophet, who becomes a living scroll.

O. Models of paradoxical sweetness

The catastrophe-bearing scroll tastes like honey (3:3). Revelation 10 later varies the image with sweetness in the mouth and bitterness in the belly. Several models explain it.

  1. Aesthetic delight: Pleasure in literary form even when its content is disastrous; the source finds little contextual support.

  2. The sweetness of revelation itself: Ecstasy in contact with the creator’s revelation regardless of terrifying content; judged plausible.

  3. Delight in obedience: Satisfaction in overcoming fear and yielding the body to YHWH’s will; the study’s preferred interpretation.

  4. Wisdom imagery: Draws on Psalms 19 and 119, where Torah and wisdom are sweet as honey[14]. Receiving the word becomes receiving life.

  5. Narrative irony: A disturbing contrast: Israel tastes judgment’s bitterness while the prophet experiences sweetness in divine justice.

The bitterness and heat of spirit in 3:14 keep the paradox open. Personal sweetness in obedient reception gives way to pain and anger when the bearer enters a hostile world.

P. Irony between Israel and foreign nations

In 3:5–7 God specifies Israel, rather than peoples of unfamiliar speech, as the destination.

Difficult language does not necessarily mean a difficult audience, and familiar language does not ensure receptivity. Imagined foreigners would listen, but same-language Israel refuses. This is a rhetorical indictment of insider privilege and a strategy of shame, rather than a universal claim of foreign moral superiority: comprehension alone does not produce obedience.

R. Stages in prophetic identity

Through 2:1–3:11, the narrative presents the following progression:

  1. Prostrate human / ben-adam: A body overwhelmed by glory, face to the ground and powerless (1:28b).

  2. Receptive hearer: Raised by the Spirit and addressed as one required to hear (2:1–2; 2:8).

  3. Embodied word / eater: Ingests the external lament-text, integrating it into the body (3:1–3).

  4. Resilient messenger / speaker: Carries the text within, equipped with an adamant forehead to withstand refusal (3:8–11).

S. Dramatic irony: Recognition of a prophet among them

The recognition formula in 2:5 anticipates acknowledgment despite refusal, creating narrative suspense.

Readers already know Ezekiel’s divine authorization from Chapter 1, while the audience will reject him. The study locates their eventual recognition in Jerusalem’s catastrophe (Chapter 33), when the ingested lament becomes reality. Apparently powerless words thus become a measure of history.

T. Emotional trajectory in oral performance

An imagined performance before an exilic community moves through extreme emotions[9].

  • 1:28-2:2: An overwhelmed human rising after heavenly light and noise evokes sympathy and fear.

  • 2:3-7: Briers and scorpions evoke enclosure, threat, and entrapment.

  • 2:8-10: The strange scroll, double-sided writing, and sounds of lamentation, mourning, and woe intensify horror.

  • 3:1-3: Eating creates bodily shock; unexpected honey-like sweetness introduces uneasy relief and confusion.

  • 3:4-11: The foreign comparison and collision of Israel’s hard face with the prophet’s forehead restore maximum tension before the final commission.

U. Audit of a radical-satire hypothesis

The source associates Margaret S. Odell and Michael A. Lyons with readings of muteness, extreme sign-acts, and the adamant forehead as satire or deliberate confrontation[6]. Ezekiel’s rigid body and unusual behavior mirror Israel’s spiritual paralysis and idolatrous delusion.

  • Textual Evidence & Explanatory Gains: The mirroring of 3:7–9 supports this reading; muteness in 3:26 can enact an audience’s deafness.

  • Over-reading Risks & Compatibility: Reducing the whole call to performance art or black comedy would diminish the overwhelming theophany and Spirit’s action. The study therefore accepts satire as a supplementary framework for rhetorical judgment rather than a replacement for the larger theological reading.

V. Close reading

  • 2:1–2: As YHWH speaks (דבר) to prostrate ben-adam, ruah (רוח) enters (תבא) and raises him: divine action restores the passive body as a participant in dialogue.

  • 2:3–7: Rebelliousness (מורי) frames the audience. Repeated אל־תירא paradoxically underscores its frightening, scorpion-like hostility.

  • 2:8–3:3: At the narrative center, written text crosses into the body as nourishment, making the judgment message inseparable from its bearer.

  • 3:4–7: The contrast of foreign language and resistant will exposes the irony of imagined foreign reception and deliberate same-language refusal.

  • 3:8–11: The renewed commission hardens the prophet beyond stone and sends him like a catapult bearing the word toward Tel-abib’s despairing exiles.

W. Final literary-theological conclusion

Ezekiel 2:1–3:11 reconstructs prophetic ministry amid Babylonian exile’s historical and theological ruins. Its radical rhetoric makes the prophet an embodied text: he physically ingests already-written judgment rather than independently generating inspired speech. Against resistance beyond gentle persuasion, YHWH forges a hard-foreheaded counterforce. The prophet opens his mouth to receive catastrophe, bears obedience’s paradoxical sweetness, and enters a rejecting world. Repeated commission regardless of hearing binds success to faithful bearing rather than audience repentance. The passage portrays divine text taking bodily form through a fragile human and persistently confronting closed ears.
