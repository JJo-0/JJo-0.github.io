Historical-critical audit method and evidence classification

The supplied study compares geographical, political, archaeological, and chronological claims in Acts with Josephus, Tacitus, Suetonius, Roman inscriptions, and papyrus databases. Its stated aim is historiographical assessment rather than doctrinal defense or secular dismissal. The classifications below remain the study’s assessments, not an independently repeated audit.

Grade A

Independently corroborated

Contemporary or near-contemporary external evidence—inscriptions, excavations, or other ancient texts—strongly supports the event, person, or institution.

Grade B

Strongly plausible

Direct external evidence for the event is limited, but it fits the period’s political, social, cultural, and Roman institutional setting.

Grade C

Internally attested only

Historically possible, but presently dependent on Acts’ own narrative.

Grade D

Disputed / in tension

Subject to substantial debate because of chronological problems or tensions with Paul’s letters or Josephus.

Grade E

Historically non-adjudicable

Supernatural intervention, visions, and theological interpretations whose truth cannot be determined within the methodological limits of secular historical inquiry.

Absolute chronological anchors and the Roman imperial timeline

Acts covers roughly three decades, from the early 30s to the early 60s CE. This study identifies the **Gallio inscription** found at **Delphi** as a crucial absolute chronological anchor for New Testament chronology.

Key absolute anchor

Delphi Gallio inscription (SIG 801)

A letter from Claudius to Delphi, reconstructed from nine fragments

Term of office assigned by the source
May 51 CE – April 52 CE
Inscription wording “…my friend Junius Gallio, proconsul of Achaea … Claudius’s twenty-sixth imperial acclamation…” (translation of the study’s excerpt)
Chronological calculation The source dates Claudius’s twenty-sixth acclamation to January–August 52 and applies a May 1 start for provincial governors, placing Gallio’s appointment in May 51.
Application to Acts 18 The study dates Paul’s appearance before Gallio during his eighteen-month Corinthian stay to summer 51–spring 52, using it as an anchor for his ministry chronology.
* Caution: The inscription provides a chronological anchor for this episode; it should not be exaggerated into proof of Acts’ complete inerrancy.

The Claudian famine (Acts 11:28)

The source cites Josephus (*Ant.* 20.51–53), Tacitus, and Suetonius for successive food shortages in imperial provinces, including Palestine, during Claudius’s reign. It reads Luke’s “whole world” as period hyperbole while judging a regional famine’s historical core well supported.

Claudius’s expulsion of Jews (Acts 18:2)

The source cites Suetonius (*Claud.* 25.4) on disturbances associated with “Chrestus,” interpreted here as Christ, and Cassius Dio 60.6.6 on restrictions on assembly rather than expulsion. It takes these differing accounts as evidence of administrative measures surrounding Jewish–Christian conflict in Rome around 49.

Epigraphic audit of local administrative titles

Luke uses diverse provincial titles—proconsul, politarch, Asiarch, grammateus, and protos. The source contrasts nineteenth-century doubts about some terms with later inscriptions and archaeological discoveries supporting the accuracy of local terminology.

Thessalonica (Acts 17:6) Grade A
Politarchs / πολιτάρχας

The source reports more than sixty Macedonian politarch inscriptions, including Thessalonica’s Vardar Gate, despite the title’s absence from classical literature.

Cyprus (Acts 13:7) Grade A
Proconsul / ἀνθύπατος

Cyprus became a senatorial province in 22 BCE. The source associates a Soli inscription with administration under L. Sergius Paulus.

Ephesus (Acts 19:31, 35) Grade A
Asiarchs & city secretary (Grammateus)

Ephesian inscriptions attest Asiarchs involved in imperial festivals and the grammateus (γραμματεύς) as a leading civic official.

Melite / Malta (Acts 28:7) Grade A
Chief official (Protos / πρώτῳ)

The source cites bilingual inscription CIL X 7495 as evidence for the Maltese title *primus Melitensium* (Prōtos).

Philippi (Acts 16:20) Grade A
Magistrates (Praetors / στρατηγοί)

Fits the practice of magistrates in the Roman colony of Philippi using the prestigious title praetors rather than duoviri.

Corinth (Acts 19:22; Romans 16:23) Grade B
City official Erastus (Erastus inscription)

The inscription “Erastus pro aed[ilitate] s.p. stravit” is known. The source favors identifying its Erastus with the biblical figure but cautions that identity is not certain.

Cross-audit: Paul’s undisputed letters versus Acts

Some of the strongest scholarly tensions concern fine-grained chronology and theology between Acts and Paul’s undisputed letters, including Galatians, Romans, and Corinthians.

Critical Tension (Grade D)

1. Jerusalem visits and council chronology

• **Galatians 1–2:** a first visit three years after conversion and a second after fourteen years (two visits recorded).
• **Acts 9, 11, 15:** post-conversion visit, famine relief, and council (three visits recorded).

Conflict model On this view, Luke invented the Acts 11 famine visit or divided the council tradition to emphasize Paul’s closeness to Jerusalem.
Harmonization model Identifies Galatians 2 with the Acts 11 famine visit (South Galatian hypothesis), dating the letter before the decree and explaining its omission.
Theological Adaptation

2. Torah observance and natural-theological defense

• **Acts 21:** Paul funds Nazirite purification expenses at the temple to demonstrate Torah observance in Jerusalem.
• **Galatians / Romans:** Paul strongly denies justification through works of law.

Scholarly assessment: Two explanations compete: Luke softened the portrait to demonstrate Christianity’s harmlessness within the empire, or Paul adapted to his audience, becoming as a Jew to Jews (1 Corinthians 9:20).

3. Audit of Paul’s Roman citizenship and legal proceedings

Paul’s letters do not explicitly claim citizenship by birth. The source nevertheless judges Acts’ account of gubernatorial coercitio, protection from flogging under the Lex Iulia de vi publica, appeal to Caesar, and trial procedure consistent with first-century Roman legal papyri (Evidence Grade B).

Ancient historiography, speeches, and the “we” passages

Ancient historians’ speech-writing conventions and Acts

Thucydides 1.22 explains that historians reconstruct speeches around what a speaker would appropriately say in the circumstances, in an authorial style, rather than producing word-for-word transcripts.

Model A: Verbatim preservation An essentially direct record of speakers’ words; judged highly unlikely in the source’s account of modern scholarship.
Model B: Historical core + authorial reconstruction (Preferred) Early Christian oral tradition shaped through Luke’s distinctive style and theology.
Model C: Entire invention Purely literary composition without a historical core; the source argues that accurate local administrative detail weighs against this position.

The “we” passages (Acts 16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16)

Four major hypotheses explain the four unexplained shifts from third-person “they” to first-person plural “we”:

1. Authorial participation / eyewitness The traditional view: Luke the physician accompanied Paul on particular journeys and sea passages. The source regards chapter 27’s nautical detail as strong support.
2. Use of a travel diary source The author used firsthand notes from a companion such as Silas or Timothy, retaining “we” without fully adapting the pronoun.
3. Literary topos A rhetorical convention creating immediacy in ancient sea travel accounts. A difficulty is that other voyages in Acts do not use “we.”
4. Pseudonymous effect A later author deliberately inserted “we” to acquire eyewitness authority.

Textual criticism: ECM III & GNT6 / UBS6 audit

The source describes textual reconstruction based on INTF’s Editio Critica Maior (ECM III, 2017) and the Coherence-Based Genealogical Method

52 Text Changes in Main Text

Acts has an unusually complex transmission history. The source reports **52 textual changes** from NA28 in **ECM III**, and **155 split guiding lines (♦)** where two readings are accorded equal standing. It further states that this text underlies **GNT6 (UBS6)**. These edition and publication claims are translated as supplied rather than independently certified here.

The Western text and Codex Bezae (D05)

The source describes the Western text, represented by Codex Bezae, as **about 8.5–10% longer** and more dramatic than the standard Alexandrian text. It cites fifth-century **P127 (Oxyrhynchus 4968)** for the circulation of expanded readings in Egypt, and favors extensive second-century apologetic and anti-Jewish scribal editing over Luke’s own authorial revision. Its claims of scholarly agreement are retained as the source’s assessment.

Qualitative comparison of authorship and date models

Authorship: Luke as Paul’s companion versus a later anonymous writer

The source regards nautical and administrative knowledge as supporting a companion-Luke model, while differences from Paul’s letters and chronological tensions favor an independent literary author. It prefers a writer who possessed eyewitness travel material and built a distinctive theological narrative.

Dating: The source’s majority assessment of 70–90 CE

A pre-70 date appeals to the omission of Paul’s death, but is weakened by Luke’s retrospective treatment of the temple’s destruction. The source finds a second-century date less persuasive and prefers **70–90 CE**, as an account defending the church’s position after Jerusalem’s fall.

🛡️ Final red-team review: Self-audit and responses

Q1. “Does archaeological accuracy uncritically validate the whole narrative?”

→ The source says no: accuracy concerning an Ephesian secretary or Cypriot governor does not establish the literal wording of speeches or the occurrence of miracles.

Q2. “Have tensions between Acts and Paul’s letters been erased through harmonization?”

→ The source says no: it retains the conflict model (Grade D), especially the tension between Acts 15’s decree and Galatians 2.

Q3. “When Josephus and Acts differ, is one automatically assumed correct?”

→ The source says no: it marks the Theudas–Judas chronology in Gamaliel’s Acts 5 speech as seriously disputed (Grade D), judging an anachronism in Acts the stronger historical explanation.
