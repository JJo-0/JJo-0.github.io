1. Ezekiel 2:1–3:11: Historical-Grammatical Exegesis and Textual Criticism — Exile, Prophetic Calling, and Scribal Culture

A. Executive summary

This report examines historical, grammatical, textual-critical, and theological dimensions of Ezekiel’s call in 2:1–3:11. Against the trauma of sixth-century BCE Babylonian exile, Ezekiel depicts a prophet physically receiving God’s word and being sent to an unresponsive, rebellious community[1]. The passage combines ancient Near Eastern scribal culture, priestly purity and embodiment, and prophetic-call patterns to explain how a messenger receives authority amid Israel’s collapsed covenantal and political order.

The study begins with the Masoretic Text (MT), drawing on the Septuagint (LXX), Papyrus 967, and Qumran research to reconstruct the textual history[3]. It argues that Ezekiel’s autonomous prophetic agency is displaced as divine רוּחַ raises him and ingestion of a sacred scroll embodies the message within him[5]. The completeness of judgment on a scroll written on both sides, together with its paradoxical honey-like sweetness, makes the commission depend on divine sending rather than audience acceptance or success[6]. The passage should be interpreted through ancient treaty violation, embodied trauma, and scribal textual authority rather than projecting later pneumatology or New Testament Son-of-Man Christology backward. Mimetic hardness equips the prophet to proclaim sovereign YHWH’s word against communal resistance.

B. Textual unit & structure

Ezekiel 2:1–3:11 forms the center of the wider call narrative (1:1–3:15). The divine glory (כְּבוֹד־יְהוָה) and cosmic throne (Merkabah) of 1:1–28a lead into the prophet’s collapse at 1:28b, introducing call and commission. Spirit-driven movement and seven days of silence in 3:12–15 conclude the scene; the watchman’s duties begin at 3:16. Thus 2:1–3:11 is a coherent subunit of empowerment and internalization of the message.

The sequence moves through reception, internalization, and confrontational sending. First, 2:1–2 contrasts human incapacity before divine glory with empowerment through רוּחַ. Second, 2:3–7 identifies Israel as rebellious and announces a commission independent of whether people listen. Third, 2:8–10 warns the prophet against rebellion and introduces the scroll written on both sides. Fourth, 3:1–3 depicts the written revelation becoming embodied through eating. Finally, 3:4–11 contrasts Israel with foreign-language peoples, intensifies its resistance, and gives the prophet a harder face and forehead before sending him to the exiles (גּוֹלָה). Israel’s rebellion and prophetic counter-obduracy are arranged symmetrically within a scribal and priestly process of receiving the word.

D. In-depth verse-by-verse exegesis

Ezekiel 2:1 — Human finitude and divine command “Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you.” (WEB) This first command addresses Ezekiel collapsed before divine glory (1:28b). בֶּן־אָדָם denotes mortal humanity rather than an office. עֲמֹד (Qal imperative) requires active standing, which the overwhelmed prophet cannot yet accomplish on his own. וַאֲדַבֵּר (Piel first-person imperfect) places the initiative for revelation with YHWH.

Ezekiel 2:2 — Restored agency through the divine Spirit “The Spirit entered into me when he spoke to me, and set me on my feet; and I heard him who spoke to me.” (WEB) The gap between command (2:1) and performance (2:2) is bridged by רוּחַ rather than autonomous will. Throughout Ezekiel, this Spirit is more than cognitive inspiration: it takes hold of and moves the prophet’s body as an agent of prophetic action[9]. וַתַּעֲמִדֵנִי (Hiphil consecutive imperfect) depicts the Spirit actively setting Ezekiel upright. Divine possession restores him physically as an agent capable of fulfilling the commission[5].

Ezekiel 2:3–4 — Rebellion and the rhetoric of covenant violation Ezekiel is sent to Israel, described in the WEB as “a nation of rebels” and as “impudent and stiff-hearted.” The Hebrew combines בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל with the striking plural גּוֹיִם הַמּוֹרְדִים, interpreted here as rhetorically assimilating Israel to rebellious nations. מוֹרְדִים and פָּשְׁעוּ echo political rebellion in ancient suzerain–vassal treaties, indicting persistent covenantal resistance to YHWH. קְשֵׁי פָנִים and חִזְקֵי־לֵב pair outward impudence with inward inflexibility.

Ezekiel 2:5–7 — Prophetic independence from outcomes Whether people listen or refuse (אִם־יִשְׁמְעוּ וְאִם־יֶחְדָּלוּ), they will recognize a prophet among them. Briers, thorns (סָרָבִים וְסַלּוֹנִים), and scorpions (עַקְרַבִּים) accompany the command not to fear (אַל־תִּירָא). The contrast of שָׁמַע and חָדַל relocates success from audience repentance to faithful proclamation. The audience’s eventual recognition of divine authority is central. The difficult plant and animal language accumulates metaphors of hostile hearers rather than describing a literal habitat[1].

Ezekiel 2:8–10 — The scroll vision and warning against rebellion The prophet must not rebel (אַל־תְּהִי־מֶרִי) but open his mouth and eat. A hand extends a מְגִלַּת־סֵפֶר, written כְתוּבָה פָּנִים וְאָחוֹר, bearing קִינִים, וָהֶגֶה, and וָהִי. The warning shows that prophetic status does not guarantee obedience. The densely written opisthograph, a product of scribal culture, visualizes judgment’s overwhelming completeness; its contents are lamentation, mourning, and woe[6].

Ezekiel 3:1–3 — Physical internalization: Embodiment of the word The command to eat (אֱכוֹל) and fill belly and inward parts (בִּבְטְנְךָ; וּמֵעֶיךָ תְמַלֵּא) culminates in honey-like sweetness (כִּדְבַשׁ לְמָתוֹק). Ezekiel opens his mouth to receive the word (אָכַל). The mouth–inward parts–belly mapping embodies written revelation physically. Sweetness lies in obedient reception of divine order, Torah, and wisdom, rather than enjoyment of judgment’s destructive content[11].

Ezekiel 3:4–7 — The foreign-language paradox and envoy logic The prophet is sent to speak God’s words (בִּדְבָרַי), not to peoples of difficult speech (עִמְקֵי שָׂפָה וְכִבְדֵי לָשׁוֹן). Israel refuses him because it refuses God (כִּי־אֵינָם אֹבִים לִשְׁמֹעַ אֵלָי). This counterfactual contrasts foreign peoples who might obey despite a language barrier with same-language Israel’s refusal. The problem is volition rather than communication. Ancient envoy logic identifies rejection of the messenger with rejection of the sovereign sender.

Ezekiel 3:8–11 — Mimetic hardness and the commission to the exiles God hardens the prophet’s face and forehead beyond the people’s, like adamant harder than flint (כְּשָׁמִיר חָזָק מִצֹּר), and sends him to his exiled compatriots (בְּנֵי עַמֶּךָ הַגּוֹלָה). שָׁמִיר (emery / flint) describes the divinely bestowed resistance[7]. This is resilience and mimetic hardness against hostility. Having fully received the word, Ezekiel is commissioned to the historical Babylonian exiles as a divine messenger whose mandate is independent of their reception.

G. בן־אדם: Word study

בֶּן־אָדָם (ben-adam), first used in 2:1, occurs 93 times in Ezekiel according to the source, functioning as God’s distinctive address to the prophet.

  • Semantic analysis: Literally “son of man,” the construction indicates a member of humankind: ben expresses belonging or character, and adam humanity associated with earth. Its sense here is mortal human or finite creature.

  • Contextual function: The reading associated with W. Zimmerli and M. Greenberg contrasts Ezekiel’s frailty and lowliness with Chapter 1’s transcendent Merkabah and divine glory (כְּבוֹד־יְהוָה).

  • Caution about later conceptual developments: Ezekiel’s בֶּן־אָדָם should be distinguished in its primary context from Daniel 7:13’s apocalyptic humanlike figure (כבר אנש) and Jesus’ messianic Son-of-Man language (ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου). Projecting later exalted Christological meanings backward obscures the passage’s emphasis on creaturely finitude.

H. רוח: Analysis of agency

The role of רוּחַ in 2:2—entering and raising the prophet—has a distinctive place within biblical prophetic tradition.

  • Physical agency (Wind / Force): This study distinguishes Ezekiel’s ruah from later Trinitarian formulations and merely inward insight. Research associated with J. Lapsley and I. Lilly treats it as powerful divine wind or force seizing, moving, and raising the body through empowerment and possession[5]. Compare transport in 3:12, 3:14, 8:3, and 11:1, and movement to the dry-bones valley in 37:1.

  • Dispossession and restoration of prophetic agency: Ezekiel does not stand by autonomous resolve. Agency has collapsed at 1:28b and becomes possible through ruah’s forceful intervention. The prophet’s body thus anticipates disempowered exilic Israel’s restoration, as in the dry bones[5].

I. Vocabulary map of the rebellious house

The study contrasts the ordinary designation בֵּית יִשְׂרָאֵל with Ezekiel’s repeated, provocative בֵּית הַמֶּרִי, “rebellious house,” counted here sixteen times.

  • Borrowing political and diplomatic vocabulary: מֶרִי, מוֹרְדִים (participial מָרַד), and פָּשְׁעוּ (perfect פָּשַׁע) draw on ancient suzerain–vassal language for tributary resistance and political revolt.

  • Theodicy through covenantal rebellion: Ezekiel maps Judah’s political disaster following rebellion against Nebuchadnezzar onto covenant violation against YHWH[10]. Idolatry and disobedience become political-covenantal revolt rather than merely ethical failure. Jerusalem’s destruction and exile are explained as judgment by a just sovereign rather than divine incapacity.

K. Models of scroll eating

The study compares three major approaches to Ezekiel’s eating in 3:1–3.

  1. Embodiment / internalization model — M. Odell: The interpretation associated with Margaret Odell reads the act as prophetic initiation embodying judgment within the priestly prophet, rather than a memory technique[6]. The prophet’s body becomes a sanctuary holding God’s word.

  2. Scribal authorization model — E. Davis: Ellen Davis and related readings identify a transition from oral prophet to scribal prophet dependent on fixed text. Swallowing written revelation gives proclamation the authority of divine instruction that cannot be negotiated or altered.

  3. Trauma and disempowerment model — D. Smith-Christopher: Trauma-oriented critics attend to bodily coercion: the opened mouth and compelled swallowing of an unwanted judgment scroll[1]. They interpret this as reflecting exile’s extreme loss of power and traumatic stress. Unusual bodily action becomes somatized communication under oppression. This is an interpretive model rather than a retrospective clinical diagnosis[2]. (Revelation 10:8–11 later appropriates Ezekiel’s scroll-eating imagery intertextually within an apocalyptic setting.)

L. The sweetness–woe paradox

The scroll contains קִינִים, הֶגֶה, and הִי—lamentation, mourning, and woe (2:10)—yet tastes כִּדְבַשׁ לְמָתוֹק, sweet as honey (3:3). The study explains this bitter-content / sweet-reception paradox through two theological settings.

  • Wisdom and Torah traditions: Psalms 19:10 and 119:103 associate God’s instruction with honey-like sweetness. In the source’s priestly reading of Ezekiel (1:3), even judgment on homeland and temple is received as the sweetness of divine order and holy justice[12].

  • The inward joy of obedience: After the warning against rebellion (2:8), receiving the word fully into belly and inward parts becomes joyful union with revelation. The bitterness of spirit in 3:14 is the subsequent pain of confronting hostile reality while bearing that word, rather than a contradiction of sweetness at reception.

M. Israel versus foreign-language peoples

Counterfactual rhetoric in Ezekiel 3:4–7 intensify the portrayal of Israel’s unresponsiveness.

  • The prophet is not sent to peoples of unfamiliar speech (עִמְקֵי שָׂפָה וְכִבְדֵי לָשׁוֹן). The hypothetical claim that they would listen despite linguistic barriers functions as rhetorical shaming.

  • Israel shares language and culture with Ezekiel but refuses to listen (כִּי־אֵינָם אֹבִים לִשְׁמֹעַ). The obstacle is deliberate unwillingness, not ambiguity or inadequate communication technique.

  • Envoy logic: Ignoring an envoy insults the sending king. Ezekiel 3:7 applies that diplomatic logic: refusal of the prophet continues rebellion against the sovereign YHWH.

O. Competing redactional models

Four major hypotheses address how the call in 2:1–3:11 relates to Chapter 1’s glory vision and subsequent material.

  1. Model A — Literary unity: The reading associated with M. Greenberg and L. Allen treats 1:1–3:15 as a carefully unified narrative by the historical Ezekiel. Discontinuity and repetition represent the prophet’s overwhelming experience rather than necessarily editorial seams.

  2. Model B — Expanded core call tradition: Using form criticism, W. Zimmerli locates an original call tradition in the commission and scroll vision of 2:3–3:11*. The grand Merkabah vision and linking material are treated as later expansion by Ezekiel or an Ezekielian priestly school.

  3. Model C — Progressive scribal expansion: T. Mackie’s comparison of MT and LXX is presented as evidence of later scribes adding theological modifiers, such as Israel’s treatment as nations and cumulative hardness language in 2:3–4, to strengthen coherence and emphasis[17]. Scribes thus function as interpreters rather than merely copyists.

  4. Model D — Variant literary editions: The source associates I. Lilly and H. Yip with reading the Old Greek and MT as parallel literary editions with distinct theological orientations, beyond simple copying mistakes. MT intensifies judgment and rebellion, while Greek traditions sometimes soften or rearrange material[13].

Ezekiel’s silence and bodily actions express traumatized somatization.

No objection motif or verbal protest accompanies swallowing; this may reflect exilic disempowerment[1].

A counterreading sees divine control and wisdom-like joy rather than pathology; psychological reduction is a risk.

Medium

The commission in 1:28b–3:15 forecloses audience repentance.

The hearing-or-refusal formula (2:5), hardened forehead (3:8), and linguistic paradox (3:6).

Later watchman material and Chapters 18 and 33 urge personal repentance and survival and assign responsibility to the prophet.

High (Restricted to this call unit)

Q. Conclusion

Ezekiel 2:1–3:11 depicts revelation becoming an authoritative text bound to divine origin amid national destruction and forced migration. Israel is portrayed as a rebellious covenant-breaker, rhetorically assimilated to other nations, with resistance accumulating in hard faces and hearts. God responds by placing ruah within the prostrate, finite ben-adam and compelling him to stand[5]. Eating the densely written judgment scroll transforms the prophet from a speaking mouthpiece into an embodied, mobile scribal text[6]. The counterfactual receptivity of foreigners sharpens the indictment, while shamir-like mimetic hardness equips him against the audience’s rocklike resistance[7]. The commission is independent of whether hearers repent: proclamation itself fulfills the task. This call narrative gives resilience to a prophet entering a hostile exilic community[1]. It also establishes him as evidence through which the audience will eventually recognize that YHWH’s authorized prophet stood among them[2]. Unable to stand by himself, the creature becomes a messenger through the word filling his body, addressing a devastated community with final judgment and the possibility of re-creation.
