Isaiah 43:18–21: Literary, Rhetorical, Discourse, and Metaphor Criticism — Memory, Reordered Space, and Creaturely Praise

A. Literary Unit Boundary Models

Literary boundaries are crucial. Deutero-Isaiah’s overlapping motifs and shifting speakers make segmentation difficult, and the meaning of verse 18 changes with its frame. Four principal models follow.

First, 43:18–21 as an independent unit: אַל־תִּזְכְּרוּ begins a strong turn, while תְּהִלָּתִי יְסַפֵּרוּ completes the purpose in 21[1]. Its weakness is severing the contextual referent of רִאשֹׁנוֹת. The former things need preceding verses to identify them.

Second, 43:16–21 as a salvation oracle. Form- and literary-critical readings associated with Claus Westermann take כֹּה־אָמַר יְהוָה in 16 as a new beginning[3]. Sea passage and Egypt’s defeat in 16–17 contrast with wilderness passage in 18–21. This frame gives rhetorical coherence and identifies former things with exodus[4].

Third, 43:14–21 combines Babylon’s judgment and new exodus. The source associates Joseph Blenkinsopp and Ulrich Berges with attention to verse 14’s redeemer formula and Babylonian judgment[6]. Babylonian liberation in 14–15 overlaps historically with Egyptian liberation in 16–17, locating the new thing in concrete return rather than generic renewal.

Fourth, 43:16–28 includes the covenant lawsuit. Rhetorical readings associated with Hyun Chul Paul Kim emphasize the turn from praise vocation in 21 to וְלֹא־אֹתִי קָרָאתָ יַעֲקֹב in 22[8]. Saving grace and creaturely praise are followed by failure and inability, exposing the new thing’s dependence on divine grace rather than merit.

The study prefers 43:16–21 as the primary frame, while retaining the subsequent reversal. Arbitrarily closing the context would diminish the final form’s theological movement.

B. Parallel / Progressive / Concentric Structure Audit

A structural audit first tests linear progression: interruption of memory (18), new action and recognition (19a), concrete ways, rivers, and beneficiaries (19b–20), and formation for praise (21). The purpose expressions לְהַשְׁקוֹת and יְסַפֵּרוּ cluster toward the end, supporting a teleological movement from interruption to attention, realization, and response[10].

A competing A–B–C–B′–A′ chiasm might pair past with future praise, new action with chosen people, and center wilderness way / rivers. The study finds insufficient morphological or semantic correspondences, applying poetic criteria associated with Robert Alter and Adele Berlin.

It therefore favors progressive or epexegetical expansion. Way / rivers in 19 develops into water / rivers plus creatures and people in 20: a ripple from declaration to widening benefit rather than a mechanically returning symmetry.

C. Remembering–Not Remembering Rhetorical Map

The double prohibition אַל־תִּזְכְּרוּ / אַל־תִּתְבֹּנָנוּ shocks a tradition in which remembrance (זָכַר) sustains identity and covenant life, as in Deuteronomy 8 and Psalm 77[13].

The pair is more than synonymous repetition: zakar recalls events, while titbonanu intensifies attentive consideration into possible fixation. The warning moves from recollection toward captivity to a paradigm.

Intertextual readings associated with Patricia K. Tull distinguish this from declaring the past worthless or requiring amnesia[14]. After leading readers toward exodus in 16–17, the speaker interrupts their lingering there. This literary reorientation breaks epistemic paralysis that would prevent recognition of a different saving act[15]. Reducing it to a universal psychological command to erase past hurt narrows the ancient rhetoric’s salvation-historical intention.

E. Attention–Recognition–Response Flowchart

The cognitive movement is backward gaze → attention to the speaking God → recognition of new action. The source describes a forceful rhetorical reorganization of perception rather than self-help positive-thinking practice.

  1. Attention: הִנְנִי (hinni, behold I) foregrounds the divine speaker’s presence and sovereign intention, beyond a visual command.

  2. Recognition: עַתָּה תִצְמַח הֲלוֹא תֵדָעוּהָ does more than seek agreement or rebuke despair. Against 42:18–19’s blindness, the rhetorical question announces certainty and awakens passive observers into active witnesses[17].

  3. Response: Recognition is not a causal prerequisite for God to create passage. It is the human reshaping of perception before prior divine action.

F. Sprouting / Way / Water Metaphor Matrix

Three sensory metaphors make the abstract new thing (חֲדָשָׁה) concrete.

First, sprouting: an historical act receives the botanical image צָמַח (tsamach)[19]. It validates an initially small and hard-to-see beginning, implies irresistible growth, and demands close attention. It anticipates water and wilderness life, sharing agricultural motifs with 55:10–13 while serving here as ecological renewal’s prelude.

Second, way: a pathless midbar becomes open passage toward a destination rather than closed, disorienting terrain.

Third, water: desert rivers (נְהָרוֹת) reverse impossible survival into livable conditions.

G. Wilderness Spatial Poetics and Movement Map

Midbar and yeshimon function in parallel[20]. Midbar can denote marginal pastoral terrain; yeshimon intensifies the image into extreme resource-poor desolation.

The spatial transformation does not remove the place and relocate people into utopia. Wilderness remains, but ways and water change its meaning from death-space into life-giving passage. Applying this to contemporary uncertainty can be helpful analogy, provided communal return and ecological transformation are not reduced to individual psychology.

Newness does not devalue former salvation. It reconstructs its story in an exceeding mode the old sea pattern could not predict. This praises divine freedom rather than asserting that everything new is good and old is bad.

I. Water-as-Obstacle / Water-as-Life Hypothesis Audit

Water’s changed role is central. In 16, sea and mighty waters obstruct escape and become instruments of judgment on Egypt rather than simply personified enemies. In 19–20, water and rivers quench thirst and revive dead terrain[24].

Threatening water → life-giving water warns against fixed symbolism, whether always trial or always Spirit. Natural imagery is a flexible literary vessel holding historical memory and eschatological hope.

J. Repetition map: Wilderness, desert, water, and rivers in Hebrew

Deliberate repetition between 19 and 20 creates progression.

  • Wilderness (מִדְבָּר, midbar): With בְּ in both 19 and 20 — two occurrences.

  • Desert / wasteland (יְשִׁימוֹן, yeshimon): With בִּ in both 19 and 20 — two occurrences.

  • Rivers (נְהָרוֹת, neharot): In 19 and 20 — two occurrences.

  • Water (מַיִם, mayim): Added once in 20, parallel with rivers.

Mere duplication from different editorial layers does not fully explain the pattern. Verse 19 promises through אָשִׂים (imperfect), while 20 affirms provision through נָתַתִּי (perfect / prophetic perfect), connecting concrete beneficiaries with realized action[2]. Epexegetical expansion moves promise toward reality, integrates humans and other creatures, and prepares praise in 21.

K. Nonhuman Response and Animal-Imagery Analysis

Verse 20 attributes honor to wild creatures (חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה; תְּכַבְּדֵנִי)[26].

This is not a biological report of humanlike animal speech. It depicts saving provision renewing a wider ecosystem rather than Israel alone[28]. It also creates a foil: if wild creatures recognize life-giving grace, what response belongs to the covenant people? This anticipates and intensifies verse 21.

L. The strangeness of jackal and ostrich imagery

Tannim and benot ya’anah should be read within Isaiah’s intertextual landscape[26].

Isaiah 13:21–22 and 34:13 associate them with terrifying ruins after judgment on Babylon and Edom. In 43:20 they receive water and praise: agents of desolation become witnesses to life. The reversal portrays grace reaching even places of extreme despair.

N. Divine agency–human response matrix

Separating expressions by agent reveals the passage’s account of salvation.

  • Divine action: עֹשֶׂה (Do), אָשִׂים (Place), נָתַתִּי (Give), בְחִירִי (My chosen), יָצַרְתִּי (Form): initiative, creativity, and provision.

  • Creaturely human / animal response: אַל־תִּזְכְּרוּ (Stop fixation), תֵדָעוּהָ (Recognize), recipients of לְהַשְׁקוֹת (Give drink), תְּכַבְּדֵנִי (Honor), יְסַפֵּרוּ (Recount): reception and response.

Testing the relationship: No Hebrew grammatical basis makes verse 18 an if-clause controlling 19. The sequence is divine declaration → reshaped perception → life-sustaining provision → formation and praise. Modern self-help causality must not confuse recognizing salvation with causing it.

O. Communal identity: Belonging, election, formation, praise

Three descriptions in 20b–21 progressively form Israel’s relational identity.

  1. My people (עַמִּי, ammi): Reaffirms fundamental exodus-covenant belonging despite exile’s apparent abandonment.

  2. My chosen (בְחִירִי, bechiri): Emphasizes sovereign election enduring historical failure and deportation[24].

  3. A people formed for myself (עַם־זוּ יָצַרְתִּי לִי, am-zu yatsarti li): Yatsar, associated with creation and pottery, portrays re-formation from historical ashes[31].

The appositions designate the same people while deepening from belonging to election and re-creation. Their purpose is a worshiping, witnessing community rather than private self-realization.

P. Praise–proclamation–memory cycle

Piel safar in תְּהִלָּתִי יְסַפֵּרוּ means recounting and public testimony beyond emotional singing[10].

The unit ends in responsive speech rather than simply completed arrival in Jerusalem. Divine action is spoken by the prophet, recognized and experienced by creatures and people, then recounted by the saved community. Renewed exodus experience thus generates new literary and historical witness for later generations. Reducing this to worship music or congregational evangelism totals narrows its scale.

Q. Close reading: Reversal and tension after 43:22

Reading 22–28 after the triumphant grace of 18–21 creates strong tension.

  • Promised identity (21): A people formed to declare praise: an ideal purpose.

  • Actual conduct (22–24): Jacob fails to call, Israel is weary of God, and sin burdens the relationship.[6].

The adversative waw interrupts expectation. The promised new salvation is not a reward for good worship or moral achievement. Despite failed response, the people are formed through verse 25’s unilateral forgiveness. Grace, failure, and forgiveness must be read together[35]. Isolating 18–21 can encourage and commission hearers but risks losing Deutero-Isaiah’s radical grace toward the unqualified if the following indictment is excluded.

T. Final literary-theological conclusion

Isaiah 43:18–21 offers neither private optimism nor forced amnesia. It temporarily suspends foundational exodus memory and redirects attention toward God’s emerging new creation. The one who dried the sea now creates rivers in dry terrain, transforming the pattern of deliverance. Creatures formerly marking cursed ruins become praising recipients and anticipate the people’s response. Sovereign election and formation arise from grace, with the aim of public testimony rather than survival alone. The following lawsuit exposes failure and makes this community depend on forgiveness rather than religious merit. The passage thus enlarges the horizon of divine saving power, raising a past-bound community toward a renewed eschatological vocation.
