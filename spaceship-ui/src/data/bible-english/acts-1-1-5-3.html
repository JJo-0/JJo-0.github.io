C. Apostolic-authority matrix

The chosen apostles in Acts 1:2 and Matthias in 1:15–26

  • 1. Eyewitness accompaniment: Accompanying Jesus from John’s baptism through ascension, Acts 1:21–22, defines first-generation witness status.
  • 2. Sovereign divine selection: Jesus’ choice and prayer before lot-casting ground authority in divine initiative rather than only communal agreement.
  • 3. Resurrection proofs and instruction: Forty days of appearances and Kingdom teaching transmit authoritative tradition.
  • 4. Empowerment by the Spirit: Human memory/ability is insufficient without the promised power of Acts 1:8.
  • 5. Worldwide commission: Authority serves witness and sacrifice, rather than internal rank or domination.
Avoid anachronistic projection of episcopal succession: The source reads apostolic office as a combination of historical witness and eschatological charisma, not institutional inheritance alone. Directly identifying Acts 1 with later jurisdictional episcopal-succession doctrines risks anachronism.

E. Kingdom and Israel-restoration models

God’s Kingdom in Acts 1:3 and the restoration question in 1:6

1. Israel’s physical/spiritual restoration: A scripturally rooted expectation of Davidic/covenantal restoration, not necessarily merely mistaken nationalism.
2. God’s universal rule: Israel’s restoration opens toward worldwide divine sovereignty, as read through Isaiah 40–66.
3. Messianic kingship: Confession of the ascended Jesus as Lord offers a political-theological alternative, associated with Daniel 7.
4. An eschatological new age: An inaugurated but not-yet-completed salvation-historical tension.
5. A contrasting community: The church witnesses to the Kingdom rather than being simply identical with it.
Theological assessment: The source argues that Luke expands rather than discards Israel-restoration hopes through Spirit and mission to the nations.

Prophetic empowerment of already believing disciples for mission; criticized here for minimizing communal indwelling.

Model E: Integrated, F. Macchia

Salvation, empowerment, healing, tongues, and restoration overlap in the Spirit’s outpouring.

11. Reconsidering the church’s birth

The source challenges treating Pentecost as creation from nothing: the community already interprets Scripture, prays, and selects Matthias. It instead reads Pentecost as empowering an existing Israel-renewal community, a commissioning from inward gathering to public mission,rather than its absolute beginning.

Module 5: Supersessionism, misuse, and CECC/steelmanning

Assess supersessionist risks and interpretive controls against leadership abuse and spiritual ranking. Apply claim–evidence–counterevidence–confidence analysis and strong alternative readings.

K. Supersessionism risks

The source rejects reading Judaism as obsolete and the church as a simple replacement Israel. Restoring the Twelve and gathering diaspora Jews foreground renewal within Israel; Gentile mission is interpreted as scriptural expansion rather than Israel’s disposal.

L. Power and the ethics of waiting

  • Against abuse justified by “the Spirit said”: Apostolic authority remains accountable to historical witness and communal deliberation, including Acts 15.
  • Against using waiting to evade responsibility: Waiting includes prayer, Scripture, and communal preparation rather than passive inaction.
  • Against spiritual rankings from Spirit baptism: Gifts serve community and witness rather than privilege or status.

M/N. CECC matrix and steelmanning

Claims, evidence, counterevidence, and confidence

Claim 1: Spirit baptism is an empowerment experience subsequent to salvation. Source confidence: Moderate

Evidence: Acts 8’s delayed Spirit reception and the believing disciples awaiting the promise in 1:5.

Counterevidence: Acts 2:38 and 1 Corinthians 12:13 can be read as integrated initiation.

Steelman assessment: Luke emphasizes missionary empowerment, but a mandatory salvation sequence for every believer does not follow mechanically.
Claim 2: Acts 1 grounds exclusive authority for modern spiritual leaders. Source confidence: Very low

Evidence: Commands through the Spirit in 1:2 and empowerment in 1:8.

Counterevidence: Apostolic qualification requires accompanying Jesus and witnessing resurrection, 1:21–22; Peter is also accountable in Acts 11/15.

Steelman assessment: Charisma does not authorize arbitrary domination; it remains accountable to tradition and community.
Claim 3: Social-memory theory dismantles Gospel/Acts historicity. Source confidence: Low

Evidence: Memory approaches examine how present communities reconstruct the past rather than simply copy it.

Counterevidence: Reconstruction does not necessarily mean invention; oral tradition may retain eyewitness constraints.

Steelman assessment: The source uses memory criticism to examine how historical recollection forms missionary communities, rather than treating it as automatic denial of history.

Module 6: Application, conclusions, and bibliography

The original specifies a seven-sentence application and twelve-sentence synthesis, followed by references formatted according to the SBL Handbook’s second edition.

O. Modern application

Seven-sentence format retained

The promise of the Spirit and waiting in Acts 1:1–5 invite reflection on contemporary mission and leadership. Communities can be tempted to prioritize human planning and institutional resources. The narrative instead locates initiative in God, with waiting understood as active communal discernment and prayer. Leaders must not use claims of spiritual guidance to justify personal domination. Charisma serves mission under accountability to historical testimony and community rather than private privilege. Ranking believers by gifts or experiences distorts the Spirit-baptism theme. Communities are therefore called to become humble witnesses grounded in the cross and resurrection and dependent on the Spirit.

P. Final synthesis

Twelve-sentence format retained

Acts 1:1–5 is more than a historical introduction, shaping early Christian memory and eschatological identity. It connects Jesus’ earthly ministry and suffering, resurrection appearances, and the apostolic community’s cultural memory. The source grounds apostolic authority in witness, instruction, and the Spirit rather than simply later institutional succession. Israel-restoration hopes are expanded toward universal divine rule rather than discarded. Waiting in Jerusalem establishes mission’s outward movement from Israel’s sacred center. Spirit baptism encompasses initiation, empowerment, and restoration in a dynamic theological portrayal. Water and Spirit sequences vary, emphasizing divine freedom rather than mechanical ritual causality. Canonical comparison finds both tension and convergence with Paul. Later traditions appropriate the passage through confirmation, regeneration, holiness, or charismatic experience. Distinguishing historical, canonical, and reception layers helps identify later doctrinal projection. The source challenges Pentecost as creation of a community from nothing and emphasizes divine initiative in mission. Ultimately, the intersection of Spirit empowerment and cross-shaped memory frames the community’s continuing witness.
